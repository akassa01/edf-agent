# Personal Case — Raj and the Solomon Paradox

*Reference case — Humanist archetype (Ashmit, personal case, June 14). Illustrative composite. Individual scale; the Solomon Paradox is the whole spine.*

*Editorial note: the original text refers to "the 17th camel." The house standard is the **18th camel** (Islam & Susskind). Read accordingly.*

The United States is home to over 1 million international students with almost 2/3rd of those at the undergraduate and graduate level. Raj is one of them. An undergraduate student hailing from India, the largest source of international students in the United States. His first major decision came almost four years ago, when he decided to pursue his undergraduate studies at New York University rather than Delhi University in India. He had approached the decision like a scientist, weighing the pros and cons and their probabilities. He weighed factors like the difference in cost of education versus difference in post-college earnings. Since he was pursuing economics, a generally lucrative major, his decision tipped in the favor of New York University. He expected to use the OPT (Optional Practical Training) under his academic visa to secure a job and then receive a H1B visa. His plan allowed him to earn in US dollars for enough years to recoup his academic investment and then some. It seemed like the right decision.

Now, his situation is different. Political developments in the United States have reduced the scope of hiring international students. He is supposed to graduate in 3 months but has not yet secured a high paying job as he had expected. The road ahead seems bleak. He is forced to consider another decision. Does he go back to India after graduation or continue in the United States with the lower paid option?

He is not alone in this situation. In fact, his college best friend Farhan faces the exact same decision. When advising Farhan, Raj tries to reason through the decision. Returning to India was a safer option. He would regain his family's social capital, which would help him secure a high paying job in India. His parents could also help him repay the education loan as well. His spending on housing and necessities would be saved. Financially, he may be better off returning to India. Yet, he had more to consider. His US education had given him social capital within the United States. Most of his friends were now either Americans or international students staying in the United States. His job, while lower paying by his US standards, paid higher than a potential job in India when adjusting for currency exchange rates. He would remain independent. The downside was the lack of guarantees. If the political situation remained the same, he may not receive an H1B, forcing him to return after a year. He now considered the opportunity cost. Does an additional year in the United States with a lower portion of his earnings saved and uncertainty for the future outweigh returning to India with low independence and lower overall income?

For Raj, it seemed that Farhan should stay in the United States and take the risk as he did not lose much by having to potentially return to India a year later rather than right after graduation. Raj had successfully identified the x in this situation by removing the noise in this decision and pinpointing it to the uncertainty of future. Furthermore, he suggested that Farhan is not limited to the United States, his international education opened doors for him in Europe or the Middle East, alternatives that provided a different weightage to his considerations. He had identified the camel outside the binary, a decision that may have the best likelihood of success. Raj approached Farhan's decision like a pragmatic decision maker.

Now, Raj was also forced to consider his own options. His suggestion to Farhan was one approached with logic like a thinker or engineer, but in his own case, he had his feelings to consider. He felt like a failure. He had failed to secure the job he had expected. He had failed to consider the real likelihood of receiving an opportunity to continue living in the US. He felt that his only viable path was to return and cut his losses. This decision was not one based on a pragmatic approach. His feelings, rather than being a data point which could help him make the best decision for himself, had become overwhelming and overshadowed his pragmatism. He had failed to be a pragmatic decision maker and become a victim to the Solomon Paradox.

The Solomon Paradox refers to the human likelihood to behave with egocentrism, being able to offer wiser suggestions to others but failing to apply it themselves. The Solomon Paradox is one that drives human decision making, and Kross & Grossmann's studies indicate that by self-distancing oneself from the decision through third person pronouns, humans are able to overcome this paradox. Engineering Policy runs on weighted considerations of the three decision-making approaches and metaphors, yet they may all fail, if one overwhelms the others. The decision maker must self-distance themselves from their decision to ensure that the feelings component is not the only one considered.
