# The McNamara Fallacy

*Reference case — Scientist archetype. Canonical voice.*

In 1961, Robert McNamara arrived in Washington as Secretary of Defense with a reputation as the most analytically rigorous mind in American business. He had turned Ford Motor Company around using systems analysis, breaking complex problems into measurable components, optimizing each one, and trusting the numbers to tell the truth. When Kennedy brought him to the Pentagon, he brought that entire operating philosophy with him.

Vietnam was his first real test, and he approached it the way he approached everything: by finding the right metric. The problem with conventional warfare indicators was that they were qualitative. Territory held, morale, political will. These were hard to track and harder to report. Body count was clean. You could tabulate it, chart it, brief it, and compare it week over week. If America was killing more of the enemy than the enemy was killing Americans, America was winning. McNamara built an entire strategy around this metric.

But the thing it measured didn't matter. The Viet Cong and North Vietnamese weren't fighting a war of attrition. They were fighting a war of endurance, rooted in a centuries-long history of resisting foreign occupation. They could sustain casualties that would be catastrophic by any Western military model because the thing they were fighting for wasn't a position on a map or a graph on a computer system. It was something a spreadsheet has no column for. McNamara's model was internally consistent and externally meaningless.

The failure here is an epistemological one. McNamara never asked what the Vietnamese were actually fighting for because that question didn't fit his framework. A scientist optimizes within a model; they rarely question whether the model is the right one. He confused precision for accuracy. His numbers were precise. They were measuring the wrong thing with extraordinary care.

A pragmatist in McNamara's position would have started not with "how do we measure progress" but "how does the other side define winning?" That requires setting down the spreadsheet and sitting with the messiness of motive, history, and identity — none of which are quantifiable but all of which are causally decisive. It might have meant bringing in historians of Southeast Asia, Vietnamese political analysts, people who understood the texture of the conflict rather than its body count. The pragmatist doesn't abandon measurement; they interrogate whether the thing being measured is the thing that matters.

(something to flag — the US also went into the war with a lot of ego, something that principled pragmatism does not address)
