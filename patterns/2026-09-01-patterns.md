## Pattern updates — six new cases

### Pattern 1: The Enforcement Gap at the Border — domestic variant confirmed

Prior instances were primarily international (treaty compliance, regulatory framework misapplication across state boundaries). Aberfan adds a well-documented domestic industrial variant: the National Coal Board received reports from its own engineers flagging tip drainage conditions and classified them as requiring future attention rather than present action. The Tribunal found "ignorance combined with indifference" — a formulation that names the combination without explaining its structure. The structure is organizational: reports filed to the right person were not the same as reports acted upon, because the incentive to act was not held by the same person who received the information.

**The domestic variant:** The enforcement gap in the domestic case is not "no law required action." The Mines and Quarries Act 1954 clearly required tip safety. The gap was between legal obligation and organizational practice — the same gap as in the international cases, compressed into a single institution rather than across sovereign states. The new distinction: international enforcement gaps require diplomatic or treaty mechanisms to close; domestic enforcement gaps require organizational redesign of accountability pathways. Both are enforcement gaps. Only the remedy layer differs.

**Book implication:** The enforcement gap chapter should not be framed as "international treaty compliance vs. domestic regulation." The underlying pattern — obligation exists, information exists, action does not — is scale-invariant. What varies is the mechanism required to close it.

### Pattern 2: The Credibility-Position Trap — machine-vs-human variant

Six prior instances of this pattern involved human actors whose institutional position functioned as a veto on whose evidence counted. The Post Office Horizon case adds a mechanized variant: a computer system's output was treated as having higher epistemic standing than human testimony as a matter of architecture, not just politics. This is not "my position says your evidence is wrong." It is "the data type says your evidence is wrong."

The practical consequence is more insidious than the standard version. In the standard version, the discounted party can at least formulate the dispute: "my evidence is as good as yours, and you are using position to override it." In the machine variant, the dispute cannot be formulated, because the machine's output is classified as a different category of evidence — objective, auditable, not-a-matter-of-opinion. The subpostmaster who says "I did not steal this" has no access to the evidence that could rebut the machine's output, because that evidence (the Peak logs) is proprietary and not disclosed.

**The new sub-variant:** *Asymmetric evidence access creates an unanswerable credibility trap.* Human testimony that contradicts a proprietary system's output cannot be admitted without access to that system's internal logs, which the institution controls. This is distinct from the positional case because no amount of persuasion or reframing resolves it; it requires a structural change to evidence access.

**The Flint connection:** Flint is also a credibility-position trap variant. MDEQ's regulatory interpretation functioned as a veto on community testimony: residents' evidence was classified as "public relations noise" rather than as scientific signal. The mechanism differs — MDEQ's veto was positional, not technological — but the result is the same: the affected party's testimony was structurally inadmissible, not merely disbelieved.

**Book implication:** The credibility-position trap chapter needs three sub-variants: (a) standard positional — institutional standing determines whose evidence counts; (b) ontological — the parties disagree about which mode of knowing is legitimate (Cairo, prior digest); (c) technological — data-type asymmetry renders human testimony structurally inadmissible against machine output. Each requires a different countermeasure. Ontological requires framework negotiation before evidence; technological requires mandatory disclosure and adversarial audit.

### Pattern 3: The Standards-Setter Captures the Standard — self-regulation variant confirmed

Asilomar adds the self-regulation sub-variant that was anticipated in earlier digests but lacked documentation: the actors who define the moratorium and set the safety thresholds are the same actors who will benefit from the research continuing once those thresholds are defined as met.

The Asilomar flag identifies the specific mechanism: "The actors who attended Asilomar were the ones who had incentive to frame the problem as manageable by scientists." The scientists who attended were overwhelmingly those working on recombinant DNA research. They correctly identified several real risks and correctly suspended work on a subset of experiments. They also defined the problem as technically solvable by scientists applying their own judgment — which is exactly what this actor class would define it as. Environmental advocates, patient groups, and the general public were not co-architects of the framework.

**The selection effect:** Asilomar is frequently cited as a model for AI governance (scientists pause, self-regulate, define safety criteria, resume). The Asilomar case now adds the appropriate caution: this model works best when the relevant risks are primarily technical and within the scientific community's own field of expertise. When risks are social (whose research gets done; who owns the benefits; who bears the costs of accidents), the self-regulation model captures the standard in favor of the researchers. This is not cynicism about the scientists' good faith — most were genuinely motivated by safety concerns. It is a structural observation about who was in the room.

### Pattern 4: The Impossible Timing Problem — false-alarm extension

Sencer adds a temporal dimension that prior instances of the Impossible Timing Problem lacked: the false alarm. Prior cases (Orbinski/MSF neutrality, Blix/Iraq verification) faced the problem of decision under irreversible time pressure, where acting too late has catastrophic consequences and acting too early has uncertain ones. Sencer faces the additional problem that acting on incomplete information and being wrong creates compound costs extending across subsequent decision cycles.

The false alarm's compound trust debt: the 1976 swine flu vaccination program produced GBS cases and program deaths. Subsequent public health emergencies — HIV in the 1980s, H1N1 in 2009, COVID-19 in 2020 — operated in the shadow of those GBS numbers whenever vaccination campaigns were proposed. Sencer's decision was not merely wrong in its own time frame; it imposed a discount on future public health authority that he could not have anticipated and that principled pragmatism has no standard tool for managing.

**The asymmetry:** The standard urgency-actionability trap concerns the gap between urgency and the capacity to act meaningfully in one time cycle. The false-alarm extension concerns the gap between acting on urgency now and the diminished capacity to act on urgency in future cycles. This asymmetry is not resolvable by better information — the GBS risk was known and could not be definitively quantified against the pandemic risk. It is a temporal structure of public health decision-making under uncertainty that exceeds any single decision cycle.

## New Pattern 7: The Diagnosis-Remedy Gap

This pattern was latent in prior cases but is now documented clearly enough across four instances (Gabcikovo, Flint, Post Office, and, looking back, Srebrenica) to warrant naming.

**The pattern:** A diagnosis becomes externally available — a court ruling, an independent audit, an academic study, a news investigation — and correctly identifies the problem and its remedy. The remedy is not implemented. The diagnosis stands unchallenged. The gap between correct diagnosis and executed remedy is as large as the gap between original problem and diagnosis.

**Documented instances:**

*Gabcikovo:* The ICJ named the 18th camel in 1997 and instructed both states to negotiate joint adaptive management. Both accepted the judgment. Twenty-seven years later, the joint management regime does not exist. The ruling was not wrong. The political cost of the winning parties accepting partial wins exceeded the political cost of frozen non-compliance.

*Flint:* By January 2016, the science was unambiguous. The remedy — corrosion inhibitor, service line replacement, medical monitoring — was well-defined. Criminal accountability, civil compensation, and lasting infrastructure fixes were each subject to separate re-bureaucratization processes. As of 2021, most criminal prosecutions had collapsed; lead service line replacement was incomplete; civil settlement required future claim waiver from the most affected residents.

*Post Office Horizon:* The 2021 Court of Appeal ruled 39 convictions an "affront to the public conscience." The public inquiry continued through 2024. Full exoneration, compensation, and prosecution of responsible individuals moved on separate tracks at different speeds, with each layer subject to its own institutional resistance.

*Srebrenica (week 32):* ICTY indicted and convicted responsible parties. ECHR found the Netherlands had violated the Convention. Dutchbat survivors and Dutch government completed settlements. The political framework that created the mandate gap — Security Council veto structure; UN peacekeeping authorization doctrine — remained unchanged.

**The structure:** In each case, the diagnosis comes from outside the institution that produced the harm (a court, an independent commission, an academic institution). The institution accepts the diagnosis formally and contests the remedy practically. This is not defiance — it is the translation problem between an external finding and the institutional machinery required to implement it. Courts can declare; they cannot compel the budget allocations, organizational redesigns, and political decisions that implementation requires.

**The principled pragmatism implication:** The toolkit — joint fact-finding, adaptive management, 18th camel options, stakeholder convening — is oriented toward generating a better diagnosis or a better decision pathway. It has almost no tools for the specific problem of a correct diagnosis that cannot be translated into executed remedy. This is a genuine limitation, and the corpus now makes it visible across multiple archetypes and scales.

**The chapter implication:** The book may need a chapter on what comes after the diagnostic tools succeed. The working title: "When Principled Pragmatism Wins and Nothing Changes."

**What is under-represented in the flag corpus:**

- *Moral licensing:* No case yet where having done a "good thing" once creates confidence that a harmful related thing can also be done. This is adjacent to Haber (Ypres) but is not quite the same.

- *Distributed culpability:* Cases where no single actor is wrong but the aggregate produces catastrophe. Boston Molasses gestured at this; it needs more direct documentation.

These three are worth seeding into the next generation cycle as candidate flag types.

## Cross-case regularities — four-week compound

**The feeling mode's systematic inadmissibility.** Four of the six new cases involve "feeling" as the crowded-out mode (Flint, Post Office) or as a component of the crowded-out compound (Aberfan's community petitions; Sencer's patient right to informed consent). Across the full corpus, feeling is exactly balanced at 24 with doing and thinking for the clean crowded-out label. But the qualitative character of feeling's exclusion is distinctive: in almost all the feeling-crowded-out cases, the mechanism of exclusion is epistemic — the feeling dimension's inputs (community complaints, patient testimony, survivor accounts) are reclassified as something other than evidence. They become public relations concerns, anecdotes, emotional responses, or signals of irrationality. They are not weighed against technical evidence and found wanting. They are recategorized before the weighing begins.

This is worth distinguishing from the doing-mode trap (where urgency prevents deliberation) and the thinking-mode trap (where data silences narrative). The feeling-mode trap is primarily a categorization problem: the affected party's testimony is methodologically excluded from the evidentiary domain, not merely outvoted.

**The scale invariance of the pattern.** The Diagnosis-Remedy Gap (Pattern 7) appears at community scale (Flint), individual scale (Post Office), and international scale (Gabcikovo). The False-Alarm Trust Debt (Pattern 4 extension) appears at international scale (Sencer/WHO). Pattern 2's machine variant (Post Office) is individual scale. This confirms a recurring observation: the underlying dynamics of principled pragmatism's limitations are not scale-specific. The book cannot be organized by scale (international chapters, community chapters) without losing the cross-scale regularities. The patterns are the organizing principle; the cases within each pattern should span scales.
