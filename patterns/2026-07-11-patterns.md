## 1. Crowded-out modes: doing is the most neglected dimension

Across 8 cases, the mode that was displaced breaks down as:

| Crowded-out | Count | Cases |
|---|---|---|
| **Doing** | 4 | challenger-oring, emma-daniel, who-covid-pheic, gerd-nile |
| Feeling | 2 | mcnamara-vietnam, hawaii-false-alert |
| Thinking | 2 | raj-solomon, inderlok-dmrc |

The most common failure mode is not misdiagnosis (crowded-out thinking) or
empathy failure (crowded-out feeling). It is the gap between understanding a
situation and knowing what to do about it. Four of eight cases break down at the
transition from "we see the problem" to "we can act on it."

This cuts across archetypes: Challenger (scientist-dominated) and the WHO COVID
case (humanist-dominated) both crowd out doing, but for different reasons.
Boisjoly crowded out doing because thinking in the wrong register — engineer-to-
engineer rather than engineer-to-authority. Tedros crowded out doing because the
institution lacked a middle tool. The pattern is the same; the mechanism differs.

**Hypothesis for Prof. Islam:** The urgency-actionability trap is the workshop's
most transferable concept. Cases where urgency is high and the responsible actor
has no proportionate tool reliably produce the crowded-out-doing pattern —
regardless of whether the dominant mode is thinking, feeling, or both.

## 2. Flags accumulating toward an institutional architecture critique

The eight flags so far:
1. **Ego** (mcnamara): principled pragmatism does not address what drives actors to
   ignore contrary evidence for reasons of self-image.
2. **Raw organizational power** (challenger): principled pragmatism cannot dissolve
   a hierarchy that has already decided.
3. **Actionable space ≠ outcome** (emma-daniel): the framework gives you a space
   to act, not a guaranteed result.
4. **Trust decay cannot be rebuilt on demand** (hawaii): institutional credibility,
   once lost, is not recoverable through better decision-making in the next round.
5. **Self-awareness does not grant immunity** (raj-solomon): knowing the bias does
   not exempt you from it.
6. **Cost distribution unresolved** (inderlok): the framework identifies the 18th
   camel but does not tell you who pays for it.
7. **Structural conflict of interest between institution and principals** (who-covid):
   an institution whose authority is member-state-derived cannot act against its
   principals at the speed the problem requires.
8. **Governments may need the conflict to function** (gerd-nile): political will
   may be absent not because leaders don't see the solution but because the conflict
   is a resource — mobilizing, identity-sustaining, electorally productive.

The first four flags describe limitations at the level of the individual actor
(ego, power, uncertainty, trust). Flags 5–6 describe limitations at the level of
self-knowledge and cost distribution. Flags 7–8 describe limitations at the level
of the institutional architecture itself — limitations that principled pragmatism
cannot reach because the problem is not the quality of the decision but the
structure of the system in which the decision is made.

This is an emergent progression. The corpus may be developing a taxonomy:
EDF works best at the individual and procedural level; it faces structural limits
when the decision-maker's institutional incentives are misaligned with the
outcome EDF recommends. Flags 7–8 are the first two entries in the "structural"
category.

**Hypothesis for Prof. Islam:** This is a chapter in the book. "Where EDF stops"
is not a limitation to apologize for; it is a map of the remaining problem space.
The corpus is building toward a claim: EDF is necessary but not sufficient when
(a) the principals are also the obstacles, or (b) the conflict is politically
productive for at least one key actor.

## 3. Decision pathways: Reframe dominates; Implement and Expand are absent

| Pathway | Count |
|---|---|
| **Reframe** | 6 (mcnamara, challenger, emma-daniel, raj-solomon, who-covid, gerd-nile) |
| **Redesign** | 2 (hawaii, inderlok) |
| Implement | 0 |
| Expand | 0 |

Six of eight cases call for Reframe — shifting the question rather than solving
the current one. Two call for Redesign — building a new system rather than
optimizing the existing one. No case in the corpus calls for Implement (execute
an existing plan more faithfully) or Expand (apply a working solution to a
larger population or scope).

Two possible readings:
- The corpus is genuinely selecting for the hardest cases, where the problem
  cannot be solved within its own frame. This would be appropriate — the most
  teachable cases are the ones where the frame is the problem.
- The selection process is biased toward Reframe cases because those are the
  cases where EDF's distinctive contribution (the 18th camel, the Solomon check,
  the shared-question construction) is most visible and most legible.

If the second reading is right, the corpus needs cases where EDF's value is in
better implementation or better scaling, not only in reframing. Prof. Islam should
decide which kind of cases the book prioritizes.

## 6. Cross-case regularity: the 18th camel usually requires a category shift, not a better option within the existing category

In every case where an 18th camel exists and is defended:
- McNamara: shift from "measuring killing" to "understanding the other side's winning conditions"
- Challenger: shift from "engineering probability" to "institutional liability"
- Emma/Daniel: shift from "intervene or respect" to "widen the support network"
- Hawaii: shift from "speed vs. accuracy" to "design for recovery"
- Inderlok: shift from "extend or don't extend" to "build a new underground station"
- WHO COVID: shift from "declare or don't declare" to "what can the DG do unilaterally tonight"
- GERD: shift from "water rights negotiation" to "energy partnership co-management"

In every case, the 18th camel is not a better option within the existing frame.
It is a move to a different frame entirely — a category shift, not an optimization.
This is what distinguishes it from compromise. The corpus is building a consistent
answer to "what is the 18th camel?" It is the option that is only visible once
you leave the frame the other two options share.

**This is book material.** The pattern — 18th camel as category shift, not
optimization — is the most teachable and generalizable finding the corpus has
produced so far.

## 7. Prospective cases: first entry

The GERD case (2026-07-11) is the corpus's first prospective entry. The synthesis
block is a live recommendation rather than a counterfactual. This is worth
monitoring: does the prospective framing change which elements are most useful
pedagogically? The argument that the 18th camel is available but may not be
politically survivable is sharper in a prospective case (where the reader can
imagine acting on it) than in a retrospective case (where it becomes a
counterfactual exercise). Consider whether future prospective cases should always
be paired with their most likely real-world moment of decision — the next
scheduled negotiation, the next election cycle, the next filling season.
