**Reframe grows to 16 cases** with camus. The camus case is structurally distinct from the 15 previous Reframe cases: it is the first where the reframing move is available in literal real time at a specific, documented, internationally observed moment — and is not made. Previous Reframe cases show actors who could not identify the third question or who lacked institutional access to raise it. Camus knew the third question (federal arrangement with minority protections), had articulated it in 1958 in writing, and had a platform specifically insulated from the political pressures that normally suppress it. The missed Reframe at Stockholm is the corpus's clearest case of the gap between knowing the move and making it.

**Indus adds the corpus's second prospective Redesign case.** The three previous prospective cases used Reframe (gerd-nile), Expand (tigris-euphrates), and Implement (tuvalu). Indus-waters uses Redesign: the proposal is not to restart the old PIC mechanism as-is, or to renegotiate the full treaty, but to create a new technical instrument (the Indus Basin Climate-Technical Protocol) that separates hydrological management from the legal and political dispute. The four prospective cases now use four different pathways. This is the corpus's only prospective Redesign — and notably, it is also the only prospective case where the binding constraint is not informational or institutional capacity but strategic: a party's interest in keeping the technical and political fused.

**Redesign (19 cases) has now split cleanly into three structural sub-types:**
- *External system redesign* (grenfell, tenerife, chernobyl, hawaii, inderlok, harbin, fukushima): the system to be redesigned is an external architecture — a building, a protocol, a governance structure — and the actor's task is to identify and propose the architectural change
- *Internal framework redesign* (gandhi, pauling, bangladesh): the system to be redesigned is the actor's own conceptual apparatus — the actor must modify a framework they cannot see as a framework, because it functions as a definition of the problem space
- *Technical architecture redesign within a contested political space* (indus, hyatt-regency, citicorp): the technical redesign is available and well-specified, but it requires political conditions the framework cannot create; the redesign is correct and the pathway to implementing it is blocked by factors external to the redesign itself

This three-way split within Redesign is now the corpus's most analytically productive internal structure. For Prof. Islam: do these sub-types warrant chapter-level separation in the book, or are they better handled as a single chapter on the "Redesign" pathway with illustrative contrast?

## 4. New cross-case pattern: the "resource paradox" in individual/humanist cases

The four cases in the individual/humanist cell form a progression that culminates in a pattern not previously named: the **resource paradox**, in which the quality that grants the actor authority to act is the same quality that prevents acting.

In emma-daniel and raj-solomon, the failure is about missing resources: emma lacks authority; raj lacks self-distance. The 18th camel and the Solomon Paradox remedy are straightforward prescriptions because the failure mode is a missing element the actor can in principle acquire.

In gandhi and camus, the failure is constituted by a resource the actor actually has. Gandhi's moral authority depends on the indivisibility of ahimsa — the resource that makes him able to lead is the resource that makes the regional camel illegible to him. Camus's credibility in both communities depends on his authentic knowledge of both — the resource that gives him unique standing for a federal proposal is the same resource that makes it psychologically impossible for him to deliver one.

The resource paradox names a class of cases where prescriptive frameworks that work by identifying what the actor is missing cannot reach the actor, because the failure is constituted not by absence but by the way the actor's authority is structured. You cannot give Gandhi more authority and thereby enable him to accept the regional scope limit. You cannot give Camus more empathy and thereby enable him to take a side. The remedy (more of X) is unavailable because X is already at its maximum and is itself the problem.

**For Prof. Islam:** This pattern may be the fourth cells most pedagogically powerful, since it directly challenges the framework's assumption that identifying the missing resource is sufficient to prescribe the remedy. In cases where the failure is not a missing resource but an excess of one, the framework's prescriptive confidence is misplaced — and the honest flag is not "what's missing" but "why the prescription cannot reach the actor."

**Updated counts (40 cases):**

| H (candidate) | Inaccessible-prerequisite limit | 0 | indus | 1 |

**Category A (Actor's own limits) grows to 9 cases** with camus. The nine cases now divide into three structural sub-types more clearly:
1. *Domain mismatch* (pauling, marshall): the actor's authority was earned in one domain and does not transfer; the framework can name the mismatch but not impose humility
2. *Constitutional constraint* (gandhi, camus): the actor's authority is constituted by refusing to calculate; the framework can name the move but cannot reach an actor whose authority requires not making it
3. *Cognitive/perceptual limits* (yom-kippur, mbeki, raj-solomon, and others): the actor's framework screens out information their analytical mode cannot process; the framework can name the gap but not supply the missing perception

The camus case adds a novel feature to the constitutional sub-type: unlike gandhi, whose indivisibility constraint was a matter of principle, camus's constraint is affective — it is organized around specific people (his mother, the Belcourt community) rather than a philosophical position. The actor knows the remedy (self-distance); the remedy is psychologically unavailable when the people you love are the stakes. This is a sub-variant worth tracking separately: the actor who knows the prescription and cannot fill it.

**Category H (candidate): the "inaccessible-prerequisite limit."** The indus case surfaces a pattern not represented in any existing category: the situation where the framework has correctly designed the camel and identified the institutional convener with standing, but where the political prerequisite for the camel — the condition under which the convener would be able to convene — is inside a strategic calculation that the party making it has every incentive to keep opaque. The gap is not a missing tool, institutional authority, or diagnostic clarity (those are Categories B, D, E). It is that the solution is available contingent on a political condition the framework cannot verify from the outside.

Category G describes the failure to formulate the question before harm is done. Category H (candidate) would describe the failure to implement a correctly formulated solution because a necessary political precondition is strategically withheld. Both are limits that precede the framework's main analytical work — Category G before diagnosis, Category H after prescription. The difference is that Category H is the framework's limit at the end of its work (it has prescribed correctly) rather than the beginning (it cannot yet ask the right question).

One case does not constitute a category. But the indus case is the second prospective case in the corpus to surface a limit of this kind — tuvalu raised a related problem (Australia's bilateral recognition being meaningful and insufficient), and the next prospective case that surfaces this pattern would warrant naming H.

## 6. 18th camel cross-case pattern: 40 cases; the "unavailable camel" sub-pattern stabilizes

Six new camels across the last two runs (lapindo, love-canal, bangladesh, pauling, gandhi, mbeki) plus two this run (camus, indus) bring the corpus to 40. All 40 camels remain category shifts rather than compromises.

The **camus camel** changes the epistemic register of the dispute: from "which side do you support?" to "here is a constitutional architecture that changes what is being contested." The camel is Camus's own idea, published in the 1958 preface; the case documents that having the camel is not the same as deploying it.

The **indus camel** changes the political framing of the dispute: from "you must concede your legal position" to "neither of us is conceding anything by sharing river data." The "without prejudice" framing is the structural mechanism; it does not require political agreement, only the institutional format to make the separation visible and credible.

Three camels in the corpus are explicitly flagged as "potentially unavailable" — camus (the political space for the federal proposal may have closed before Stockholm), indus (the political prerequisite for the Protocol depends on a strategic calculation India does not disclose), and tuvalu (Australia's bilateral framework is meaningful and insufficient for the 12 other SIDS watching). This is a new sub-pattern worth tracking: camels that are technically correct and politically contingent. Previous digests noted camels that "require a trusted insider who speaks the actor's moral language" (gandhi, mbeki); the unavailable-camel sub-pattern is distinct — the camel doesn't require an insider, it requires a political condition that the framework cannot establish from the outside.

## 7. Prospective cases: four, spanning all four pathways; the indus case identifies the prospective format's characteristic limitation

The corpus now has four prospective cases:
1. **gerd-nile**: Reframe (live negotiation anchor)
2. **tigris-euphrates**: Expand (data compact extension)
3. **tuvalu**: Implement (treaty execution)
4. **indus-waters**: Redesign (technical architecture for a contested treaty)

All four prospective pathways are now represented. The prospective format has accumulated enough cases (four) to observe its characteristic limitation: in all four cases, the recommendation requires a political act by an identified actor before a specified deadline, and in three of the four (gerd, tuvalu, indus), the actor's willingness to make that act is contingent on political conditions that the framework describes but cannot create. The prospective format surfaces the framework's limit faster than the retrospective format does, because the retrospective case can always say "the pathway was available at the decisive moment" without requiring the pathway to actually be taken. The prospective case says "the pathway is available now" — and the stress test then asks whether the political prerequisite exists, which is a question with no answer available from inside the framework.

This is an important pedagogical observation for the book: the prospective case is not a harder version of the retrospective case. It is a different kind of case, because it is falsifiable in real time. Gerd-nile was written in 2026; by 2027, the Nile negotiations will have produced a result or not, and the framework's recommendation will be measurable against an actual outcome. This is the corpus's only empirically testable content.
