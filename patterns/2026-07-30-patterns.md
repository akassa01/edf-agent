**Implement (tuvalu) is the corpus's first entry.** Previously absent for 24 cases, it is now represented by the only prospective case with a live governance architecture problem: the move is not to redesign the Falepili Union or reframe what statehood means but to build, now, the governance infrastructure the treaty created the need for. The pathway opens when the 18th camel is designed but unbuilt — the problem is not conceptual (Reframe) or system-level (Redesign) but implementational (act before the window closes). This is structurally different from the other three pathways, and the corpus needed exactly this case to illustrate it.

**Expand now has two cases.** Both tigris-euphrates (2026-07-24) and nubian-aswan (2026-07-27) are international scale, and both involve a working precedent or existing mechanism that could be extended to a broader scope. The structural comparison: tigris-euphrates extends a bilateral MoU to a third state; nubian-aswan extends the concept of "internationally protected heritage" from monuments to peoples. The first is a diplomatic coordination problem; the second is a jurisdictional redesign of international heritage law. Expand does not mean "copy and paste" — it means applying an existing logic to a population or scope it was not designed for, which requires both the logic and the political will to extend it.

**Redesign (16) now leads for the first time.** The previous digest showed a near-tie (Redesign 12, Reframe 11). The four new Redesign cases — freeman, cochabamba, harbin, fukushima — are all cases where the system itself is structurally wrong rather than the question being wrong. The pattern by archetype:

- Scientist/Redesign (now 4): freeman, capetown, yom-kippur, chernobyl. These cases share a structure: the scientist's analysis is correct, the system is the problem, and the fix requires a different architecture rather than a different question.
- Engineer/Redesign (10): the largest sub-cluster in the corpus. Engineers who crowd out thinking tend to face system-level problems (building codes, regulatory architecture, communication protocols, backup power positioning) where the right move is mechanical rather than conceptual.

## 4. Flag taxonomy update: Category E dominates; a potential Category F emerging

The five-category taxonomy from the July 24 digest still holds, but Category E has grown substantially, and one new flag (freeman) may require a sixth category.

**Updated counts:**

| Category | Name | Previous | New cases | Count |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| A | Actor's own limits | 5 | harbin | 6 |
| B | Tool's limits | 4 | minamata, cochabamba | 6 |
| C | Community and political limits | 5 | — | 5 |
| D | Institutional architecture limits | 4 | nubian-aswan, tuvalu | 6 |
| E | Diagnostic-prescriptive gap | 6 | midgley, fukushima | 8 |
| F (candidate) | Feedback destruction | 0 | freeman | 1 |

**Category E (diagnostic-prescriptive gap) is now clearly the largest category** with 8 entries. The new additions are structurally similar but importantly different in emphasis. Midgley: the agent cannot tell from inside whether their system boundary is adequate — "the signal arrives in the form of a Nobel Prize or an epidemiological finding, and it arrives decades after the chemistry has been at work." Fukushima: the framework can design the mandatory disclosure mechanism but cannot change the liability-suppression asymmetry — "the party with the most accurate information about a risk has the strongest institutional incentive to suppress it." Both are about the gap between knowing and acting, but where the tiger-euphrates flag was about external political will, these flags are about the structural incentives that prevent action by the party holding the information.

**cochabamba belongs in Category B** (tool's limits) with a new sub-pattern: when the humanist refusal to accept the 18th camel is vindicated by the counterparty's exit, principled pragmatism cannot adjudicate in advance between the camel and the absolute demand. This is new: previous Category B flags were about the camel's design limitations (who pays; outcome uncertainty; professional liability incentives). The cochabamba flag is about the camel's strategic status — it may be correct, or the absolute demand may be correct, and the determination is contingent on a decision the other party makes after yours.

**freeman may require Category F: feedback destruction.** The lobotomy flag — "when a medical intervention removes the patient's capacity to articulate distress, adverse outcome data is structurally unavailable — the person who can no longer form the persistent complaint that signals harm is counted among the improved" — describes a failure mode not present in any other flag: the measurement instrument is destroyed by the intervention itself. This is not a tool's limit (Category B) in the sense that the camel is available — there is no camel, because the population that would validate the camel can no longer report its experience. It is not a diagnostic-prescriptive gap (Category E) because the gap is not between knowing and acting but between acting and ever knowing. If this pattern recurs (intervention that destroys its own adverse-outcome signal), it warrants its own category. One case does not constitute a category. Prof. Islam should decide whether this is a genuine sixth type or a boundary case within Category B.

**minamata** belongs in Category B: the burden-shifting protocol and provisional registry can be designed; the structural trap they cannot resolve is whether full liability destroys the vehicle of compensation before it pays. The source of restitution may be destroyed by the demand for it.

**nubian-aswan** belongs in Category D: the international heritage framework defines protected heritage as monuments, not peoples. The engineering camel (expand the UNESCO framework) requires changing rules the framework cannot set.

**tuvalu** belongs in Category D: international law has no framework for a state whose territory becomes uninhabitable through others' emissions. Bilateral recognition is meaningful and insufficient.

**For Prof. Islam:** Category E at 8 entries is becoming the book's most important structural finding. It is not a category of failures — it is a category of limits. EDF can reach a correct diagnosis and a viable pathway every time. What it cannot do is supply the conditions under which the pathway is walkable. That boundary — the edge of principled pragmatism — is now mapped well enough to teach. It deserves a chapter, probably the final one.

## 5. New cross-case pattern: the counterparty's veto problem

The cochabamba case introduces a pattern absent from the previous 31 cases: the situation where the 18th camel is available, offered (in modified form), rejected by the principled party, and the outcome is subsequently vindicated by the other side's exit rather than by the camel's success.

This differs structurally from all previous cases. In every previous case, the 18th camel was either:
(a) never offered — the actors chose between the two stated options and the camel is identified retrospectively (most cases)
(b) offered and accepted — the pathway is executed and produces a better outcome (none in the corpus yet)
(c) designed but arrived too late — through litigation rather than administration (minamata, 1973 ruling)

Cochabamba is the first case in category (d): the camel was designed, the principled party rejected it, and the rejection was vindicated not by the camel's success elsewhere but by the counterparty's decision to exit. This creates the counterparty's veto problem: EDF can design the camel, but whether the camel was correct depends on what the other party does next. If Aguas del Tunari had stayed and fought (which it could have), the Coordinadora's absolute demand would have produced a worse outcome than the hybrid concession. The determination of which was "correct" is contingent on a subsequent choice that the framework cannot predict or control.

This matters for pedagogy. The case is not an example of EDF succeeding (the camel was rejected) or failing (the outcome was better than any negotiated camel would have produced). It is a case of EDF reaching its boundary in a specific way: the tool produces a correct camel; the correct strategic choice depends on predicting what the other side will do; the framework cannot make that prediction. This is different from the diagnostic-prescriptive gap (Category E), which is about political will after the pathway is chosen. The counterparty's veto problem is about which pathway to choose when the correct answer depends on a variable the framework cannot observe in advance.

## 6. Prospective cases: now three, with distinct structural roles

The corpus now has 3 prospective cases: gerd-nile (2026-07-11), tigris-euphrates (2026-07-24), and tuvalu (2026-07-30). All three use live recommendations rather than retrospective counterfactuals. Their structural roles differ:

- **gerd-nile**: Reframe. The question is wrong; reframe it before the next negotiating round (the Blue Nile dam-filling dispute was live when written).
- **tigris-euphrates**: Expand. A working bilateral mechanism needs to be extended to a third actor (Turkey's invitation to the data compact was live).
- **tuvalu**: Implement. The governance architecture needs to be built before the migration creates path dependence. The move is not conceptual — it is operational.

The three cases together cover three of the four pathways (Reframe, Expand, Implement). A prospective Redesign case would complete the set — a situation where the system itself needs structural change and the decision point is live. The next prospective slot (week_index 16, if the schedule holds) should consider this.

A structural difference between retrospective and prospective flags: retrospective flags describe why the 18th camel failed or why the framework cannot reach beyond what it reached. Prospective flags describe what the framework cannot guarantee going forward. This is harder to anchor — the limitation is still operative — and the flags for gerd-nile and tuvalu are necessarily more conditional ("if X, then the camel fails") than retrospective flags ("the camel failed because..."). This is appropriate and should be explicit in the pedagogy.

## 7. 18th camel cross-case pattern: holds at 32; category shift remains the core move

The July 24 digest established this pattern across 24 cases. It holds at 32. Seven new camels across 8 cases (cochabamba was rejected; the rest were designed):

- Freeman: from "apply the intervention" vs. "prohibit the intervention" to "require independent long-term outcome tracking by parties not funded by the intervention's proponents, with patient advocacy organizations co-designing the measurement protocol"
- Harbin: from "suppress the disclosure" vs. "full immediate disclosure" to "staged disclosure with verified data released incrementally as upstream treatment advanced, with a dedicated hotline distinguishing boiled-water safety from full contamination"
- Fukushima: from "build a higher seawall" vs. "accept the risk" to "move the backup generators above the credible flood line — a bounded corrective action that does not require triggering the full regulatory cascade"
- Midgley: from "continue TEL production" vs. "halt TEL" to "commission an independent chronic population-level health assessment co-designed with Hamilton, using ethanol markets as a control"
- Minamata: from "full individual certification" vs. "no certification" to "burden-shifted certification standard with Chisso disproving causation, plus a provisional health registry separating support from liability determination"
- Nubian-aswan: from "dam proceeds, communities compensated financially" vs. "dam halted" to "expanding the UNESCO heritage framework to encompass living communities and their intergenerational relationship to place — not the monuments but the people"
- Tuvalu: from "diaspora assimilation" vs. "empty-island governance" to "Tuvaluan Digital Commons — a legally-constitutionalized governance platform enabling distributed national participation"

The pattern continues: the 18th camel is always a category shift. Every camel changes what is being contested (who validates the measurement, how much must be disclosed immediately, where the generators go, what the safety study asks, who bears the burden of proof, what heritage means in international law, what national governance can look like when the territory is gone) rather than optimizing within the existing contest (how much to disclose, how high a seawall, how strict the certification criteria).
