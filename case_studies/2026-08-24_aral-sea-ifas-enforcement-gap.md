# The Sea That Diplomats Could Not Save

In the spring of 1989, Soviet scientists published a map of the Aral Sea that, if you did not already know what you were looking at, resembled nothing so much as a broken mirror—two disconnected bodies of water where a single sea had been, with a pale grey expanse between them where the water had been but was not anymore. The map was a confession. The Soviet cotton program had been diverting water from the Amu Darya and Syr Darya rivers since the 1960s, feeding an irrigation network that covered more than seven million hectares of Central Asian desert and produced two-thirds of the USSR's cotton. The sea, which had not been asked, had been paying the bill. By 1989, it had lost more than half its surface area. Salinity had risen from 10 grams per litre to 45—above the threshold at which most of the endemic fish species can survive.

Three years after the map was published, representatives of the five newly independent post-Soviet states that shared the Aral Sea basin—Kazakhstan, Uzbekistan, Turkmenistan, Tajikistan, and Kyrgyzstan—gathered in Almaty and signed an agreement to save it. The agreement established an Interstate Commission for Water Coordination. It set water-use quotas at 1988 Soviet levels. It called for the elaboration, within the year, of an enforcement and liability mechanism.

The enforcement mechanism was never written. The sea continued to shrink.

The Scientist in the room at Almaty already knew the numbers. Philip Micklin of the U.S. Geological Survey had published the comprehensive English-language assessment in *Science* the year before independence: minimum annual inflow needed to stabilize the sea, 35–40 cubic kilometres; actual inflow in the early 1990s, below 10. Canal conveyance losses in the irrigation systems—water diverted from the rivers but lost to seepage and evaporation before reaching any field—were running at approximately 40 percent of what was diverted. The data was not contested. The Soviet Academy had been modeling the crisis since the early 1970s. Everyone in the Almaty conference room had access to it. The science told you what was happening and at approximately what rate; it did not tell you who was going to pay to stop it.

The Humanist's reading of Almaty begins at Moynaq, a fishing port in the Karakalpak Autonomous Republic of Uzbekistan. In the 1960s, Moynaq sat on the shore of the Aral and ran a cannery that processed 25,000 tonnes of fish annually. By 1989, the sea had retreated 100 kilometres. The cannery was importing fish from the Pacific Ocean—from the Barents Sea, from the Baltic—to stay open. When it finally closed, the trawlers were left where they sat, rusting in a desert that had once been the seabed. Dust storms rising from that exposed lakebed—a crust of salt and DDT residue accumulated from decades of pesticide-laden irrigation drainage—blew across the region and produced respiratory disease, infant mortality rates, and cancer incidence far above Soviet averages among the five million people who lived nearby. The Karakalpaks had no political standing in the IFAS negotiating structure. Their health crisis was the evidence of what was at stake; they were not parties to the agreement.

The Engineer's reading of Almaty is this: the five states that signed the agreement controlled radically different portions of the relevant infrastructure. Uzbekistan, which controlled the Karakum Canal and the primary irrigation network that consumed the most water, was the state without whose voluntary cooperation no quota could function. Kazakhstan controlled the lower Syr Darya and had a different relationship to the North Aral—the smaller of the two remaining bodies of water, fed exclusively from Kazakhstan's side of the basin. Turkmenistan, Tajikistan, and Kyrgyzstan had their own irrigation claims. The IFAS structure—a rotating five-state presidency with no enforcement standing—was designed for coordination among partners in a single centrally planned state. It was not designed for enforcement among five sovereign governments with competing national interests and no coercive authority over one another.

Where is X? Article 4 of the February 18, 1992 Almaty Agreement—specifically the sentence that was not there. Kazakhstan had proposed including a penalty clause for quota violations. Uzbekistan had opposed it. The compromise was silence: quotas were established, enforcement was deferred to a mechanism that would be elaborated "within 1992." That elaboration never happened. The binding constraint was not hydrological—the monitoring network existed, the data was legible, the quotas were calculable. The binding constraint was political: the cotton economy was how Uzbekistan's government controlled rural labour and generated hard currency. Agreeing to enforce water quotas meant agreeing to risk the economic foundation of the regime. No enforcement clause survived that calculus. The sea lost another decade.

What did 1+1 produce? The five states unanimously agreed to "save the Aral Sea." But Kazakhstan was answering: how do we restore the North Aral within our territory? Uzbekistan was answering: how do we maintain cotton revenues while satisfying international pressure? The Karakalpaks were asking: how do we get Moynaq's fishermen back? The IFAS founding document ratified a shared formula while each signatory answered a different question. One sea, five separate ones. The urgency of the language and the emptiness of the mechanism were not in contradiction—they were the deal.

The 18th camel appeared in 2005, thirteen years after the Almaty agreement and without any Uzbek cooperation. Kazakhstan, using an $86 million project financed largely by the World Bank ($64.5 million of that loan), completed the Kok-Aral Dam: a 13-kilometre earth dike built entirely within Kazakhstan's territory across the strait that connects the North and South Aral. The dam stopped the North Aral's water from draining south into the disappearing South. By 2006, the North Aral Sea had risen three metres. By 2008, its volume had increased 68 percent. Salinity dropped below fish-survival thresholds. Aralsk's fishing industry began, hesitantly, to revive. The 18th camel was not a five-party agreement to save the whole sea—it was enabling the one actor with unilateral capacity and an unsatisfied national interest to act within its own borders. The full sea is not coming back. The North Aral is.

The principled pragmatist's move at the February 1992 meeting was to Redesign the agreement before signing it—not to abandon the shared formula but to separate what could be agreed with enforcement from what required further negotiation. Concretely: write a canal-efficiency protocol with World Bank financing attached (reducing conveyance losses saves 10–15 km³/year without cutting cotton output, giving Uzbekistan a benefit rather than a cost); enable Kazakhstan to proceed immediately with a North Aral recovery feasibility study as a unilateral confidence-building measure; include a minimum-flow reporting clause in the IFAS operating rules, with multilateral development bank project lending conditioned on filing those reports. Full quota enforcement could remain aspirational. What could not remain aspirational was the one piece of infrastructure and the one piece of conditionality that did not require Uzbekistan to choose between the treaty and its survival.

(The flag: diplomatic agreements for shared-resource management fail when one signatory's domestic political survival depends on non-compliance. Principled pragmatism can find an 18th camel for technical coordination problems—the Kok-Aral Dam proves it. But it has no camel for the case where compliance requires a government to undermine the basis of its own power. The Aral Sea's partial recovery is both the success story and the confession: principled pragmatism worked for Kazakhstan, acting alone within its own borders. It did not work for the shared sea. The framework finds the partial solution; it does not resolve the structural political economy that prevented the full one. That is not a failure of engineering diplomacy. It is its honest limit.)

---

**Problem** — How five sovereign states inheriting a Soviet monoculture economy dependent on the same shared water resource can agree to reduce withdrawals when any reduction falls asymmetrically and no binding enforcement mechanism exists or can be created without the consent of the state that benefits most from non-compliance.

**Stakeholders** — Kazakhstan (downstream North Aral, has unilateral option); Uzbekistan (controls the primary irrigation infrastructure, cotton revenues are regime-critical); Karakalpaks (bear the worst health burden, have no formal standing); Turkmenistan/Tajikistan/Kyrgyzstan (upstream water rights, secondary interests); World Bank and UNDP (technical assistance, project financing, no conditionality leverage as constituted in 1992).

**Binding constraint** — Uzbekistan's cotton political economy: cotton revenues funded the Karimov regime's hard currency earnings and rural labour control. Enforcing water quotas meant threatening regime stability. No international agreement could override that constraint without external conditionality leverage that did not exist and could not be quickly created.

**Decision pathway** — Redesign. At the February 1992 signing, the available move was to redesign the agreement structure: separate what could be agreed with enforcement (canal efficiency, reporting requirements) from what required further negotiation (quota reduction), and include the unilateral Kazakhstan option in the IFAS operating protocol rather than treating the full-sea restoration as the only possible outcome.

**Tools** — (1) Canal efficiency financing: reduces water demand by 10–15 km³/year through conveyance loss reduction, without cutting cotton output; changes coordination by giving Uzbekistan a benefit rather than a cost. (2) North Aral unilateral project: enables Kazakhstan to act within its borders, demonstrates partial recovery is achievable, and changes learning by making the goal concrete and funded rather than aspirational and shared. (3) Donor conditionality clause: links multilateral development bank project lending to minimum-flow reporting, giving international finance institutions formal standing in IFAS technical meetings.

**Metrics** — Annual inflow to the Aral from Amu Darya and Syr Darya (km³/year via ICWC river gauges); canal conveyance efficiency in Uzbekistan's primary systems (% delivered vs. diverted, via IFAS hydrological monitoring and remote sensing); North Aral sea level (metres above sea level via satellite altimetry).

**90-day commitment** — In the 90 days following the February 1992 signing: commission a World Bank canal-efficiency assessment using available Soviet data; secure an in-principle World Bank commitment to fund a North Aral dam feasibility study; draft a minimum-flow reporting clause for IFAS operating rules before Uzbekistan assumes the first rotating chair; invite Karakalpak representatives to IFAS technical committees as observers with documented standing.
