# The Stamp of Approval

In the spring of 1979, a construction coordinator at Havens Steel Company placed a phone call to the engineering office of G.C.E. International in Kansas City, Missouri. The Hyatt Regency Crown Center was under construction — a prestige hotel for a city investing heavily in its own civic ambition — and the ironworkers had encountered a problem. The original structural drawings called for a single continuous hanger rod running from the ceiling down to the second-floor walkway, passing through a connection point at the fourth-floor level. Threading that rod through the fourth-floor box beams without cutting into the steel was proving difficult. Havens had worked out what appeared to be an elegant simplification: instead of one long rod, use two shorter ones, each easier to thread, joined at the fourth-floor box beam. Did the engineers have any objection?

Daniel M. Duncan, G.C.E. International's project engineer, told them that would be fine.

Duncan was a licensed structural engineer working at a firm led by Jack D. Gillum, a well-regarded figure in Kansas City's engineering community. The Hyatt commission was the kind of project that moves in a professional world defined by schedule commitments, client expectations, and the ordinary pressure of construction proceeding in parallel with design review. The call from Havens Steel arrived as what it presented itself as: a constructability question. How do you build the thing more easily?

The question Duncan did not ask was the structural one. In the original design, a single continuous rod carried the combined weight of both the fourth-floor and second-floor walkways in a single load path to the ceiling. In the revised design, the upper rod ran from the ceiling only as far as the fourth-floor box beam, where it terminated. A second rod then ran from that box beam down to the second-floor walkway. This looked geometrically similar to the original. It was not structurally equivalent. The fourth-floor box beam connection now had to carry not just the fourth-floor walkway but the full weight of the second-floor walkway below it, transmitted upward through the second rod and concentrated at a single point. The load on that connection had doubled. The National Bureau of Standards investigation would later find that the revised connection could withstand roughly 30 percent of the minimum load the building code required.

Nobody ran the numbers. The shop drawings arrived reflecting the revised design, and G.C.E. International placed the engineer's stamp on them.

The Scientist sees a particular kind of failure: not a failure of analytical capacity but a failure to engage that capacity. The load calculation was not difficult — it was the arithmetic of a modified load path, executable with a licensed engineer's training and an hour of time. The data was all there. The problem was that no one treated the phone call as a trigger to perform the calculation. A professional norm had quietly substituted for a structural calculation: the engineer stamps the shop drawings, therefore someone has reviewed the structure. The norm and the verification are not the same thing. When the norm is treated as if it were the verification, the verification does not happen.

The Humanist sees what the Scientist, by design, tends not to look for: the guests who attended the Friday-night tea dance at the Hyatt Regency Crown Center on July 17, 1981. One hundred fourteen of them died when the fourth-floor walkway collapsed onto the second-floor walkway at 7:05 in the evening; 216 more were injured. These guests had no access to the professional conversation about hanger rods and constructability that had taken place two years earlier. They had no vocabulary for what a shop drawing was, what an engineer's stamp meant, or what it did not require. Their safety had been entirely delegated, through a chain of professional certifications, to actors they had never heard of — and who had never thought about them.

The Engineer looks at the system of authority and accountability that produced this outcome. G.C.E. International had authority to approve structural modifications. Havens Steel had authority to propose them. The construction schedule created sustained pressure for rapid approval. No mechanism in the system required the approving engineer to document that a structural calculation had been performed before issuing the approval. Professional norms held that an engineer's stamp on a shop drawing implied structural review — but those norms did not specify what review required, and no one was obligated to verify that the review had happened. Each actor exercised their authority within the norms of their profession. The norms collectively substituted *assumed* verification for *performed* verification.

Where was the intervention point? Not July 17, 1981, when nothing was reversible. Not the shop drawing stamp, which was already downstream of the decision. The fulcrum was the phone call itself — specifically, the moment when Duncan answered Havens Steel's constructability question as a constructability question rather than returning it as a structural problem. "Two rods instead of one" is a constructability solution if the load path doesn't change. It is a structural modification if it does. The question of which category it fell into required a calculation. The calculation was the decision.

The options as presented were: use the original design, which was difficult to thread, or use the two-rod design, which was not. The 18th camel was a third response: *send it back.* "If you need a different connection geometry, give me the geometry and I will design a connection from scratch that preserves the load path." This is not a compromise between the options on the table. It reclassifies the contractor's problem from a coordination question to a structural design task, requires a calculation before any approval is issued, and restores the engineer's role as designer rather than reviewer. It would have added time to the schedule. It would not have killed 114 people.

The pathway available at the decisive moment was Redesign — not of the walkway connection, but of the shop-drawing review protocol itself: to distinguish constructability coordination (which contractors and architects can manage without structural sign-off) from structural modification (which requires a licensed structural engineer to perform and document a specific load calculation before any approval is granted). That Redesign was available to Missouri's state licensing board, to ASCE's professional standards process, to every construction contract in the country. None of those actors exercised it before July 17, 1981.

*(The flag: when professional liability is distributed across a system — engineer of record, reviewing engineer, contractor, building official — the diffusion creates a condition in which each actor can assume someone else has verified the critical condition. No one has to be negligent for the system to fail. Principled pragmatism can identify the intervention point — the phone call, the un-asked question — and propose the Redesign. What it cannot do is construct the regulatory culture in which "the stamp means someone checked" is enforced rather than assumed. That culture requires regulatory authority. And regulatory authority of that kind is typically the thing a catastrophe unlocks.)*

---

**Problem** — What process should govern a structural engineer's approval of a contractor's proposed design change during construction, when the change looks like a constructability solution but alters the structural load path?

**Stakeholders** — Building occupants who bear all the risk and have no visibility into the decision chain; structural engineers whose professional stamp carries legal force they may or may not exercise deliberately; contractors who need timely approvals to maintain schedule; building owners who commission certification without specifying what it requires; state licensing boards whose enforcement mechanisms define what the professional stamp actually means

**Binding constraint** — No regulatory requirement forced structural engineers to document that a load calculation had been performed before approving a connection change. The professional norm — the stamp implies review — operated in lieu of the verification, with no mechanism to confirm the norm was being followed.

**Decision pathway** — Redesign: distinguish constructability coordination from structural modification in the shop-drawing review process, and require documented structural calculation before any connection geometry change is approved

**Tools** — (1) Mandatory field-modification authorization form requiring the name of the reviewing engineer and a load calculation reference before approval is issued — changes interaction by making approval a traceable, documented act rather than an assumed one; (2) Peer review requirement for all connection geometry changes by a structural engineer other than the designer — changes learning by breaking the proximity bias that treats a similar-looking modification as structurally equivalent; (3) Building official construction hold at any modified connection until the filed calculation has been reviewed — changes coordination by giving building owners and permit authorities visibility into structural changes before, not after, they become permanent

**Metrics** — (1) Percentage of documented connection modifications with an attached load calculation, tracked via permit records; (2) Number of field modifications returned to the engineer for structural review vs. approved as constructability coordination, tracked via permit office classification; (3) Proportion of structural modification reviews that identify a load-path change, as a proxy for how many "constructability" approvals were actually structural decisions

**90-day commitment** — Counterfactual: in the spring of 1979, G.C.E. International returns Havens Steel's proposal to the design team with a written notation that any change to connection geometry requires a structural calculation before approval. The calculation is performed, the doubled load is identified, and the connection is redesigned with adequate capacity. The shop drawing is stamped with the calculation on file. The Hyatt Regency walkways, when they open in 1980, carry the loads they will be subjected to.
