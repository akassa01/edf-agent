# The Row That Wasn't in the Model

New York, 1987. Giovanni Andrea Cornia, an economist at UNICEF's global headquarters, is finishing the manuscript of what will become one of the most cited and least-implemented reports in international development history. He has spent two years assembling data from forty-one sub-Saharan African countries. In thirty-seven of them, child mortality has risen during the period of structural adjustment. He has the comparison: countries that received World Bank health investment loans show declining mortality. Countries that received structural adjustment loans show the reverse. The manuscript is called *Adjustment with a Human Face*. He knows the World Bank will read it carefully, respond courteously, and continue writing letters of intent that do not contain a row for under-five mortality.

He was right.

The intellectual architecture of structural adjustment had been laid down six years earlier, in a report authored by Elliot Berg and his colleagues at the World Bank. The Berg Report of 1981 was not careless work. It correctly identified the macroeconomic pathologies of sub-Saharan Africa: currencies overvalued by political necessity, fiscal deficits that could not be sustained, state enterprises that consumed public resources without producing public value, and trade barriers that protected the wrong sectors. The prescriptions followed from the diagnosis. Devalue. Remove subsidies. Privatize. Liberalize. Restore fiscal balance, and growth would follow. The model was internally consistent. The economists who designed it knew what they were measuring, and they measured it well.

The model had a row for GDP growth, a row for inflation, a row for the current account balance and the debt service ratio. The row it was missing was the one Cornia was holding in 1987. And the absence of that row was not a technical oversight.

The Scientist's reading of what the model lacked is precise: human capital is not only a welfare variable. It is a growth variable. A generation of malnourished children does not grow up to be a productive workforce. A collapse in school enrollment does not self-correct in a single fiscal cycle. What the Berg Report model treated as a transitional friction — the short-run social costs of adjustment — Cornia's data suggested was something else entirely: a compounding harm, visible in mortality rates, that would take decades to undo. Both datasets existed by 1987. Only one was in the letter of intent.

The Humanist reads what the model could not encode at all. In Zambia, as in Ghana and Kenya, the maize meal subsidy was not an economic distortion in the sense the Berg Report meant. It was the material contract between the post-independence state and its citizens — the daily proof that the government that had won sovereignty had delivered something ordinary and concrete. When Zambia removed the maize subsidy in December 1986, fifteen people were killed in the riots that followed. President Kaunda reinstated the subsidy and broke publicly with the IMF on May Day 1987, announcing it as a declaration of sovereignty. It was a principled refusal and an economic impossibility: Zambia still needed the loans. By 1989 he was forced back. When his government removed subsidies again in June 1990, twenty-seven people were killed, more than a hundred were wounded, and an attempted military coup followed within days. The letter of intent did not have a row for any of these events. The instrument had no mechanism for registering that the conditions it imposed were destroying the government whose compliance it required.

The Engineer sees the design failure at the level of the instrument. The conditionality framework was built to optimize a single output: restore debt serviceability by restoring fiscal balance. Political stability was a constraint to be respected, not a resource to be managed. The design had no feedback loop for human capital destruction — that damage appeared not as a red flag in a program review but as a failed government, or a UNICEF report, years after the letter of intent was signed. The engineering failure was systemic: the instrument optimized for one output and generated externalities it could not see because it had not built the sensing apparatus to detect them.

1+1 produced two separate questions. The IMF was answering: how do we restore fiscal balance and debt serviceability? The Zambian government — and Cornia — was answering: what adjustments can a state make without destroying the minimum social infrastructure required for it to function? These were not the same question, and only one appeared in the letter of intent. The other appeared in the mortality statistics, approximately two years after the program was designed.

Where is X? The conditionality annex of the letter of intent — specifically, the blank line where social spending floors were not written. The fiscal targets were in the document in precise language: deficit ceiling, inflation target, subsidy removal schedule. The child mortality threshold was not. That blank line was the intervention point: the document that set the terms of adjustment was the document where a different design was still possible.

The 18th camel was sitting in Cornia's manuscript. The binary on offer was: accept the standard conditionality program, or default and lose access to international capital. *Adjustment with a Human Face* proposed a third thing — not "adjust" vs. "don't adjust," not a soft program vs. a hard one, but a program that made minimum health and education expenditure as contractually binding as debt service ratios. Retain the fiscal discipline. Add mandatory social protection floors as co-conditions of lending, written into the same letter of intent as the fiscal ceilings. Health and education spending minimums. Child mortality as a real-time program indicator, tracked quarterly, with automatic review if it rose. The World Bank and IMF eventually incorporated social floors into their programs — in the 1990s, after a decade and a generation of preventable harm.

The Solomon Paradox applies institutionally. The Bank's economists would have flagged immediately that cutting food subsidies during a price shock in any OECD economy was political dynamite — a generator of unrest that would destabilize the very government whose compliance the adjustment required. They applied this political economy analysis to Europe and North America reflexively. They did not apply it to Zambia. The analytical capacity was fully present. It was not directed inward, toward the institution's own prescriptions.

What would a principled pragmatist have done at the decisive moment? In 1981, when the Berg Report was published and the conditionality framework was being standardized: convene a joint working group with UNICEF to define social spending floors; commission social impact assessments for the three largest pending programs before the letters of intent were drafted; propose to the IMF board a human face annex — mandatory health and education floors as co-conditions of lending, not as optional supplements. No delay to adjustment programs. The floor design built in parallel, at the same urgency as the fiscal targets.

*(The flag: when the institution measuring success is also the creditor, and when borrowers have no alternative lender, the feedback loop is structurally captured. UNICEF could publish the mortality data. It could not change the letter of intent. Principled pragmatism identifies the 18th camel and builds the case for it. It cannot compel a creditor to redesign an instrument that continues to perform its primary function — extracting debt service — regardless of its secondary effects on human capital. The gap between Cornia's 1987 report and the IMF's eventual adoption of social floors in the 1990s is not a failure of diagnosis. It is a record of how long institutional accountability takes to catch up with external evidence when the institution controls both the instrument and the definition of success.)*

**Problem** — International lenders must design conditionality programs for sovereign debt restructuring that restore fiscal balance, but the standard instrument generates predictable human capital destruction and political instability that undermine the long-run objectives the programs are supposed to serve.

**Stakeholders** — IMF and World Bank (debt service, narrow macroeconomic mandate, US Treasury approval); African finance ministers (program compliance, political survival); urban poor in Zambia, Kenya, Ghana, and Zimbabwe (food prices, health access, child survival); UNICEF and WHO (mortality data, advocacy without enforcement power); future generations (who inherit the compounding cost of reduced health and education investment during adjustment years).

**Binding constraint** — The blank line: social protection floors are absent from the letter of intent, making human capital expenditure negotiable and debt service non-negotiable, with no real-time feedback mechanism to detect the difference.

**Decision pathway** — Redesign: the conditionality instrument itself, to include mandatory social spending floors as co-conditions of lending alongside fiscal targets.

**Tools** — Social protection floor as mandatory co-condition (changes coordination: brings health and education ministries into the letter-of-intent negotiation, not only finance ministries; requires WHO/UNICEF sign-off alongside fiscal review); child mortality and school enrollment as leading program indicators (changes learning: makes human cost visible to the lender in real time, not only in UNICEF reports two years later); independent social impact assessment prior to conditionality design (changes interaction: requires WHO and UNICEF participation in program design, not only retrospective documentation of consequences).

**Metrics** — Child under-5 mortality rate tracked quarterly against regional non-program baseline, with automatic program review if U5MR rises more than 5% above pre-program baseline; social spending as a share of government expenditure pre- and post-program, independently verified; program completion rate compared to political stability index across adjustment cohort.

**90-day commitment** — Counterfactual: World Bank convenes joint working group with UNICEF to define social spending floors; commissions social impact assessments for three largest pending adjustment programs (Zambia, Kenya, Ghana); proposes human face annex to IMF board for consideration within six months. No delay to adjustment programs occurs. The social floor design is built in parallel.
