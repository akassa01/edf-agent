# The Conviction That Couldn't Convince

In the microbiology laboratory at Royal Perth Hospital, on a morning in July 1984, Barry Marshall swallowed a beaker of liquid containing ten million bacteria and waited.

He had tried everything else. The abstract he and his colleague Robin Warren had submitted to the Gastroenterological Society of Australia the previous year had been rejected without presentation — twelve of sixty-eight abstracts turned away, theirs among them, the committee finding no reason to give floor time to the claim that a bacterium caused peptic ulcers. The full paper they published in The Lancet in June 1984, documenting bacterial presence in the stomachs of one hundred patients with gastritis and peptic ulcer, was read and dismissed: the bacteria were most likely contaminants, or a consequence of the ulcers rather than their cause. What Marshall had was correlation. What the profession required, before it would revise forty years of antacid prescriptions and the surgical careers built on removing portions of hyperacid stomachs, was proof of causation. And so Marshall drank.

By day eight, endoscopy confirmed acute gastritis. The biopsy was positive. The hypothesis — that the curved bacterium Warren had noticed on biopsy specimens in 1979, later named Helicobacter pylori, was the primary cause of peptic ulcer disease — had been tested against the only body in the experiment Marshall could unambiguously control. He treated himself with antibiotics and bismuth, and recovered. Koch's postulates, the gold standard of infectious disease causation, were satisfied. Marshall was right.

And for the next decade, almost nothing changed.

The Scientist's reading of this episode is that the evidence was complete and correct. Warren had been observing the curved bacterium since 1979, living in the acid environment bacteriology textbooks said was sterile. Marshall joined the project in 1981 as a registrar and spent three years building the clinical case. The Lancet paper was rigorous. The self-experiment was unambiguous. The small double-blind trial that followed demonstrated antibiotic eradication of H. pylori and ulcer remission. By the mid-1990s, multiple FDA-approved regimens existed; a CDC survey in 1997 — thirteen years after the Lancet paper — documented that a substantial fraction of US physicians still did not associate peptic ulcer disease with the bacterium. In 2005, Marshall and Warren received the Nobel Prize in Physiology or Medicine. The Scientist sees a discovery correctly made and eventually adopted.

The Humanist sees the decade between them.

The gastroenterology profession in 1984 was not a collection of dullards refusing evidence out of spite. It was a community whose clinical identity was built on the hyperacid-stress model of peptic ulcer disease — a model that had produced Tagamet, the first drug ever to exceed one billion dollars in annual global sales, and Zantac, the world's best-selling pharmaceutical by 1988. Accepting Marshall's bacterium meant not just adding an item to the diagnostic checklist. It meant revising the foundation on which careers, curricula, surgical practices, and commercial relationships rested. The profession's resistance was not anti-scientific. It was the immune response of a knowledge community to a finding that, if true, implicated the knowledge itself. Marshall treated this response as an obstacle to be overcome by more evidence. He was correct that it was irrelevant to the science. He was wrong that it was therefore irrelevant.

The Engineer's reading: the pathway from a heterodox finding to changed clinical practice runs through specific gatekeeping institutions — medical society endorsement, NIH consensus development, pharmaceutical licensing, medical education revision. The NIH Consensus Development Conference on peptic ulcer disease, held in 1985, reviewed the evidence and found it insufficient for recommending antibiotic therapy. This was not a scientific error; it was an institutional verdict shaped by whose testimony was weighted most heavily, which in turn reflected the standing of the witnesses. Marshall was a hospital registrar with no department chair behind him, no US academic network, no standing at the NIH. The pathway for a finding to change practice required institutional standing Marshall did not have. The self-experiment was the opposite of what the engineering situation required: it proved causation to Marshall's own satisfaction while leaving the institutional engineering entirely undone.

Where is X? The September 1984 International Symposium on Campylobacter Infections in Brussels, where Marshall presented the self-experiment data to an international audience for the first time. A room of the world's gastroenterological researchers. Marshall at the front with endoscopy results showing he had infected and cured himself. This was the most precise moment where he could have converted "here is what I proved" into "here is what we should prove together." A proposal from that floor — Marshall and two skeptical European co-investigators designing a multi-site double-blind trial — would have produced evidence the profession could not claim was idiosyncratic to one registrar in Perth.

The 18th camel was not a different experiment. It was a different kind of invitation.

The apparent binary: the profession accepts Marshall's individual evidence and revises the ulcer model, or the profession rejects the evidence and waits. Both options positioned Marshall as the sole generator of truth, asking the profession to adopt it or refuse it. The third option: propose a joint replication study in which the skeptics design the experiment. Not "here is my evidence, now believe me" but "here is my hypothesis, let us test it together." When a skeptical department chair in Cleveland runs the protocol and H. pylori appears in her biopsy specimens, the finding belongs to her evidence base as much as to Marshall's. The resistance is structural — the professors most capable of sponsoring such a trial were also the professors with the most to lose if Marshall was right. But finding one willing collaborator and working through that collaborator's institutional standing was the engineering problem Marshall never solved.

What 1+1 actually produced: Marshall was answering "how do I make this evidence undeniable?" The profession was answering "who is this registrar, and what does it mean for us if he is right?" These are not the same question. Marshall's demonstrations answered the first with growing force. The second, which was actually blocking adoption, required a different action: not more proof, but an invitation to co-produce the proof. The Solomon Paradox: any gastroenterologist in that profession would have advised a colleague facing disbelief for a heterodox finding to propose a multicenter trial, get the skeptics as co-investigators, make the evidence theirs as well as yours. Marshall gave exactly this advice to no one, because no one had come to him with this problem. He was inside it. Inside it, he could see only how to make the proof more overwhelming. Not how to make it more adoptable.

(The flag: a correct finding arrived at by an institutionally marginal figure faces a structural double-bind that principled pragmatism cannot dissolve from within it. The professors who could have sponsored a collaborative trial were also the professors with the most to lose if the finding was true. The 18th camel required a collaborator with institutional standing. Neither Marshall nor Warren had enough standing to compel that collaboration. The framework can identify the pathway; it cannot grant you the position from which to walk it.)

---

**Problem** — Barry Marshall had sufficient evidence that H. pylori causes peptic ulcers; his strategy for making that evidence matter treated professional adoption as a scientific problem rather than a social and institutional one.

**Stakeholders** — Marshall and Warren (the discoverers, without institutional standing to convene multi-site trials or set the NIH agenda); the gastroenterology profession (the gatekeeper community, initially resistant); pharmaceutical companies (commercial interest in the antacid model); NIH and academic medicine (the institutions setting evidence standards for clinical practice change); approximately 4 million Americans with active peptic ulcer disease at any given time (affected parties with no standing in the room).

**Binding constraint** — Marshall's position as a hospital registrar. He could generate evidence; he could not convene the institutional process that would make that evidence belong to the mainstream.

**Decision pathway** — Reframe: shift from "how do I prove that I am right?" to "how do I make the skeptics co-produce the evidence that changes their own practice?" Who acts: Marshall, approaching a senior gastroenterologist willing to sponsor a multi-site replication trial as a genuine test rather than a validation exercise. Through what process: skeptics design the inclusion criteria and run the endoscopies; the finding emerges from within the profession's own evidence-generating system.

**Tools** — Co-investigator replication trial (converts the relation from adversarial to collaborative; makes the evidence belong to the mainstream, not to a peripheral figure; changes learning); explicit scoping of professional implications at presentations (separates "is the bacterium there?" from "what would we have to change if it is?" — creates space to evaluate the science before confronting the institutional consequences; changes interaction).

**Metrics** — Number of independent labs confirming H. pylori in blinded multi-site study (measures adoption of the finding by the profession's own evidence system; method: published trial results, coordinated by the European H. pylori Study Group established in 1987); time to NIH consensus endorsement compared against the actual 1984–mid-1990s timeline (measures the institutional lag; method: historical analysis against the modeled co-investigator-trial timeline).

**90-day commitment** — October 1984: Marshall approaches a senior gastroenterologist at University of Melbourne or University of Edinburgh to sponsor a co-investigator replication trial. November 1984: protocol drafted with skeptic input on inclusion criteria. December 1984–January 1985: trial begins at three sites; skeptics run their own endoscopies with blinded pathology.
