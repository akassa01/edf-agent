# The Only Check: Frances Kelsey and the Thalidomide Decision

In September 1960, Frances Oldham Kelsey received her first major assignment at the U.S. Food and Drug Administration: a new drug application for Kevadon, a sedative-hypnotic already approved across Europe and marketed in West Germany under the name Contergan. She was new to the job, a pharmacologist who had spent her career studying how drugs behave in biological tissue, including fetal tissue. The company that submitted the application, Richardson-Merrell of Cincinnati, expected routine processing. By the standards of the day, they were not wrong to expect it.

Kelsey opened the file and found it wanting.

The application contained the pharmacological data the company had gathered — clinical reports, animal studies, summaries of European use — but the data was company-generated and, in Kelsey's judgment, insufficient to establish safety across the patient population that mattered. She declared the application "incomplete," which under the 1938 Federal Food, Drug, and Cosmetic Act reset the sixty-day review clock rather than rejecting the drug outright. It was a procedural move, not a veto. Richardson-Merrell submitted additional materials. She declared it incomplete again. Over the next sixteen months, this sequence repeated four times.

The pressure did not remain polite. Richardson-Merrell sent delegations to the FDA. They wrote to Kelsey's supervisors characterising her review as capricious delay. They threatened legal action. A new hire resisting a drug already approved across most of the developed world was not, institutionally, on solid ground.

In February 1961, she read a letter in the *British Medical Journal* from a British physician reporting cases of peripheral neuritis — damage to nerves in the extremities — among patients taking thalidomide. The connection she drew from this report was not in the published literature: if the drug's mechanism affected peripheral nerve cells, it might affect developing fetal tissue. She wrote to Richardson-Merrell demanding evidence that Kevadon was not harmful to the fetus. The company could not produce it. The clock reset again.

By March 1962, Chemie Grünenthal had withdrawn thalidomide from the West German market. The published teratogenicity evidence — infants born with severe limb reduction defects, phocomelia, hands or feet attached directly to the trunk — was everywhere. Richardson-Merrell withdrew its American application. President Kennedy presented Kelsey with the President's Award for Distinguished Federal Civilian Service on August 7, 1962. The Kefauver-Harris Drug Amendments — requiring proof of efficacy as well as safety, eliminating auto-approval by inaction, mandating informed consent for investigational studies — were signed into law on October 10.

Approximately 10,000 to 20,000 children were born with thalidomide-induced defects outside the United States. A small number of cases occurred in America anyway, because the company had legally distributed the drug to roughly 1,200 American physicians as investigational samples while the NDA was pending — a loophole that required no FDA approval and no mandatory reporting.

The Scientist in this story is not hard to find: Kelsey, working from pharmacological training that included research on fetal drug metabolism, reading a signal in the *BMJ* correspondence that her colleagues did not and inferring a mechanism that had not yet been published. The inference was correct, but it was a heuristic rather than a demonstrated chain. The system worked because one scientist made one correct inference under conditions designed to produce the opposite result. The scientific literature did not lead the regulatory decision; the regulatory decision led the scientific literature.

The Humanist sees what the regulatory record cannot accommodate: that the patients most at risk were being actively recruited. Richardson-Merrell's marketing materials positioned Kevadon for morning sickness in pregnant women. There was no mechanism for those future patients — the unborn — to have any standing in the FDA review process. Kelsey described the company's pressure campaigns not only as scientifically inadequate but as morally uncomfortable: she felt things were being hidden. The Humanist notes that her discomfort was epistemically useful. She was picking up a signal — about the company's confidence in its own data — that did not appear in the pharmacological record.

The Engineer looks at the statutory mechanism and asks who designed this. The 1938 FDCA gave the FDA sixty days to object; if no objection was raised within that window, the drug was approved automatically. The burden of proof was inverted: the agency had to show danger, not the company safety. There was no conditional approval pathway — no regulatory vocabulary for "proceed with monitoring." There was no mandatory reporting from the investigational sample distribution to 1,200 physicians. The company was legally distributing a potentially dangerous drug to American patients while the review was ongoing, generating no evidence that the agency could access. Kelsey's tool — the "incomplete" declaration — restarted the clock but gave her nothing affirmative to do. She was holding a door closed with both hands.

Where is X? The February 1961 letter Kelsey sent to Richardson-Merrell citing the *BMJ* peripheral neuritis correspondence and demanding fetal safety data. That letter changed the nature of the dispute: no longer a paperwork argument about completeness but a scientific argument about mechanism, one the company had no answer for and could not pressure her to withdraw.

What question were the parties answering? Richardson-Merrell was asking: "Is the application compliant with FDA filing requirements?" Kelsey was asking: "Is this drug safe for the patient population the company is targeting?" These were not the same question. The 1938 Act did not require them to be. The company's application was an answer to the first question; Kelsey's review was asking the second.

The 18th camel — the option outside the binary of approve or reject — was conditional approval with mandatory adverse-event reporting from investigational use. This pathway did not exist in 1960 American law. It would have allowed the drug's legitimate uses to proceed, built a real-world evidence base from the company's own physician distribution program, and preserved Kelsey's scientific judgment as a condition rather than a veto. The Kefauver-Harris Amendment built exactly this capacity into the regulatory system. The 18th camel was available; it required statute to put it there.

The decision pathway that was available at the decisive moment was Redesign — not the binary choice of approving or rejecting this particular drug, but a redesign of the regulatory framework so that scientific judgment was embedded institutionally rather than concentrated in individual reviewers. The mechanism was the Kefauver Senate hearings, already underway. The tool was legislative: converting Kelsey's documented scientific concerns into a hearing record that specified the statutory gap and proposed the remedy. Who acts: the FDA Commissioner, in testimony to the Kefauver committee, citing the Kevadon case as evidence of structural failure rather than individual heroism. For whom: future patients with no representative in the regulatory process. Through what process: a new statute that defines "adequate safety data," eliminates auto-approval by inaction, and creates the conditional approval pathway that the law lacked.

The tools the counterfactual pathway required: jointly developed scientific standards for what "adequate safety data" means, so that the question is not negotiated case by case; mandatory adverse-event reporting from investigational use, turning the company's own distribution program into a surveillance system; conditional approval authority, so the FDA can say "proceed under these conditions" rather than being forced into a binary determination. Each tool changes the nature of the interaction — from company-FDA negotiation to shared scientific standard; from black box to reportable; from approve/reject to conditional and reviewable.

(What the episode reveals that principled pragmatism itself does not resolve: the framework can locate X, name the 18th camel, and specify the decision pathway. What it cannot guarantee is that the person at X on any given day is Frances Kelsey. She had the right training, the right supervisor, and the institutional courage to use the same procedural tool four times. Change any one of those and the drug is approved. Regulatory design cannot bank on heroism; it must make heroism unnecessary.)

---

**Problem** — Whether a regulatory framework built for speed can be made to also hold the line on scientific rigor, or whether the two goals require different institutional structures.

**Stakeholders** — Future patients (no standing in the review process); pharmaceutical manufacturers (legal applicants with full institutional access); FDA reviewers (gatekeepers with procedural but not affirmative authority); Congress (statutory architects whose 1938 design had not been updated for a more sophisticated industry).

**Binding constraint** — The 1938 FDCA's inverted burden of proof: the agency had to show danger within a window, not the company safety on a standard. The constraint was statutory, not scientific.

**Decision pathway** — Redesign: use the Kefauver hearings to convert Kelsey's individual scientific determinations into a legislative record specifying the statutory gap and mandating the remedy.

**Tools** — Jointly defined safety data standards (moves the question from negotiation to scientific baseline); mandatory investigational adverse-event reporting (converts physician distribution into surveillance); conditional approval authority (creates the 18th camel the law lacked).

**Metrics** — Time from first adverse-event signal to market withdrawal in each country (benchmark for surveillance system effectiveness); proportion of NDA pharmacological data independently verified vs. company-submitted only; adverse events per 1,000 drug-months of investigational use.

**90-day commitment** — Within 90 days of the February 1961 letter, the FDA Commissioner convenes an independent expert panel to establish minimum teratogenicity data requirements for drugs to be marketed to people capable of pregnancy and submits findings to the Kefauver committee as a statutory amendment proposal.
