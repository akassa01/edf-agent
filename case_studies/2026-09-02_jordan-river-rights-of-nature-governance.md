# The River with No Standing — Jordan River Restoration and the Governance Gap

Stand on the Abdallah Bridge, where the lower Jordan River crosses into the Kingdom of Jordan, and you are looking at what was once described by pilgrims as a wide, swift, sacred river. What you see today is a narrow, turbid channel, running brown and saline, flanked by invasive reeds on both banks. The water level is low enough that children could wade across. The salinity — more than 3,000 milligrams per litre, compared to the 500 mg/litre threshold below which the water is useful for irrigation — has made the river useless to the Bedouin farmers who once depended on it. The Jordan River, by volume, has lost between 90 and 98 percent of its historical flow. The Dead Sea, its terminal basin, is shrinking at approximately one metre per year.

This is not a failure of scientific knowledge. The hydrology is precise: the Jordan River once delivered 1.2 to 1.3 billion cubic metres per year to the Dead Sea. By the 2020s, it delivers 20 to 100 million. The sources of the diversion are documented. The technical levers for restoration are identified and partially operational. The money exists. The political will, in the form of a November 2022 joint declaration by Israeli and Jordanian environment ministers at COP27, is on record. None of this has produced a single additional cubic metre of flow in the lower river. The failure is not technical. It is a failure of the question the governance architecture is designed to answer.

Three states use the Jordan River basin: Israel, Jordan, and the Palestinian territories. Their relationship to it is governed by the Israel-Jordan Peace Treaty of 1994, specifically Article 6 and Annex II, which fix each state's water allocation entitlement. The Palestinian Authority has no formal standing in this treaty and no bilateral water rights to the Jordan River. Oslo II (1995) allocated groundwater in the West Bank without assigning surface flow from the river. Thirty years on, the Oslo annex has not been updated, the PA's exclusion from Jordan River governance has not been corrected, and the 1994 allocations have not been revisited. The treaty answered the question "how do we prevent another war over water?" It was not asked "what does the river need to survive?"

That distinction is the case.

The Scientist sees three actionable restoration levers that are technically available right now. Israel's desalination capacity now exceeds its domestic water demand; the surplus could fund a proportional reduction in Sea of Galilee diversions, releasing flow southward into the Jordan River, without reducing Israel's overall water security. Wastewater reclamation infrastructure in the lower Jordan Valley — partially funded through USAID and partially operational — could be expanded to reduce agricultural demand on the river itself. EcoPeace Middle East's Regional NGO Master Plan, a 2022 comprehensive engineering analysis, estimates that 400 to 600 million cubic metres per year of ecological flow is achievable through a combination of these levers, which is sufficient to stabilise the current situation. The science has been done. The models exist. The target is specific and reachable.

The Humanist sees why the science has not moved any water. The Jordan River is not primarily, for any of the parties, a hydraulic object. For Palestinian communities, it is the western boundary of any future Palestinian state — a line of sovereignty as much as a source of irrigation — and to accept its governance through any framework that excludes the Palestinian Authority is to accept permanent marginality in the region's water future. For Jordanians, the 1994 Peace Treaty's water provisions represent a diplomatic achievement the Hashemite Kingdom's government cannot be seen to dilute; domestic water scarcity is severe, and any reallocation triggers domestic political opposition that no government has the standing to absorb. For Bedouin agricultural communities in the valley, the river's salinity has already ended their livelihoods; restoration is survival, not preference. For the civil society networks that have maintained trilateral cooperation across the conflict divide for two decades — EcoPeace Middle East, the Jordan River Society, local municipalities — the river is the one issue that survives rounds of violence that suspend everything else. The river carries different questions for each party, and the governance architecture answers none of them.

The Engineer sees the structural problem that the Scientist and the Humanist together name: there is no forum in which the three states, the Palestinian Authority, and the river's ecological interests appear simultaneously. The 1994 treaty is bilateral. The Oslo II water annex is bilateral. Every diplomatic venue that exists is structured around sovereign entitlements, not around the ecological unit — the basin — that determines whether there is water to allocate. The doing-mode tools that would restore the river (coordinated reallocation, desalination offsets, joint monitoring) require a governance layer that does not currently exist. The treaties are the obstacle, not because their provisions are wrong but because they address the wrong question.

Where is X? It is the UN Water Conference follow-up process. The 2023 UN Water Conference — the first since 1977 — produced a set of voluntary commitments now being tracked through 2025 and 2026. That process provides a diplomatic occasion at which language can be introduced: not treaty amendment (politically impossible), but a new category of governance instrument — a transboundary ecological guardianship, with co-representatives from all basin parties including civil society, explicitly not modifying bilateral allocations. The moment when that language could be inserted into the preparatory committee documents is the fulcrum. It has not passed.

The 18th camel is not a renegotiation of the 1994 Peace Treaty — neither government has domestic standing to attempt it. It is not a reliance on the Red Sea-Dead Sea Canal to fill the Dead Sea from the south, which would stabilise the Dead Sea's level while leaving the Jordan River itself to die. It is a third thing: a Jordan River Co-Guardianship Council, modeled on New Zealand's Te Awa Tupua framework for the Whanganui River, which designated the river a legal entity co-governed by Indigenous representatives and the Crown in 2017. A Jordan River Council would designate the river a transboundary ecological entity with formal co-guardians drawn from Israeli, Palestinian, and Jordanian civil society — a parallel governance layer operating alongside, and explicitly not superseding, bilateral treaty obligations. The Council would have no power to redistribute water rights. It would have standing to coordinate the use of surplus capacity (desalination offsets, wastewater credits), to commission and publish joint ecological monitoring, and to represent the river's interests in any diplomatic forum where the basin's future is discussed.

The political obstacles are not trivial. The Israeli Water Authority has consistently resisted any governance innovation it interprets as a constraint on diversion discretion. The Palestinian Authority's legitimacy in the West Bank is constrained post-2023; it may not have the political capacity to participate in a new multilateral framework. Any hosting government for a trilateral convening faces domestic pressure around being seen to reduce its own water security. And the single most dangerous risk is indirect: if the Red Sea-Dead Sea Canal secures final funding without a Jordan River ecological flow condition attached, the visual evidence of the governance failure — the shrinking Dead Sea — is neutralised from the south, the political urgency for Jordan River restoration drops, and the window for a governance reframe closes before it is used.

A principled pragmatist at EcoPeace Middle East, or at the UN Economic and Social Commission for Western Asia, has a move available in the next 90 days that does not require governmental co-authorship. A civil society drafting process — trilateral, explicitly framed as supplemental to and not replacing any bilateral treaty — could produce legal text for a Jordan River Co-Guardianship proposal in time for submission to the 2027 UN Water Conference preparatory committee. The proposal would need to be co-signed by Israeli, Palestinian, and Jordanian civil society organisations. It would need to be explicit about what it is not (a treaty renegotiation, a reallocation of rights) and clear about what it is (a governance layer that gives the river standing in forums where it currently has none). The tool required to begin is not legislation. It is a drafting convening.

*(The honest flag: rights-of-nature designations create legal standing for a river but cannot compel the infrastructure changes needed to restore its flow. The Whanganui River has legal personhood in New Zealand and continues to be the subject of water allocation disputes. A guardianship council changes who can argue about the water; it does not change how much water is diverted. A Jordan River Council would improve representation and monitoring but cannot make Israel reduce Sea of Galilee diversions, cannot give the Palestinian Authority treaty standing it does not have, and cannot prevent Jordan from treating its 1994 allocation as non-negotiable. The gap between governance innovation and hydrological change is the unresolved problem, and no governance instrument alone closes it.)*

**Problem** — How to coordinate Jordan River ecological restoration when the governance architecture — bilateral water treaties that exclude Palestine and address sovereign allocation rather than ecological function — prevents the coordination of restoration levers that technically exist and are partially operational.

**Stakeholders** — The lower Jordan River as an ecological entity (no current governance standing); Palestinian communities with no treaty water rights; Bedouin agricultural communities in the Jordan Valley whose livelihoods depend on irrigation-viable river water; EcoPeace Middle East and the trilateral civil society network (20+ years of cross-conflict coordination capacity); the Israeli Water Authority (diversion manager and governance gatekeeper); the Jordanian Ministry of Water and Irrigation (treaty party with domestic water scarcity constraints); USAID and EU (current funders of joint technical infrastructure); the Palestinian Authority (excluded from existing frameworks, contested domestic legitimacy).

**Binding constraint** — The absence of a governance architecture that includes all three major water users and the river's ecological interests in the same forum. The Palestinian Authority lacks treaty standing in any bilateral Jordan River framework, making any trilateral coordination structurally incomplete under the existing architecture.

**Decision pathway** — Reframe: reframe the governance question from "how do we divide the water?" to "how do we co-govern the river?" by establishing a Jordan River Co-Guardianship Council as a parallel legal entity, explicitly not amending bilateral allocation treaties, that gives the river formal standing and coordinates surplus capacity deployment. Who acts: EcoPeace Middle East as civil society convener, with ESCWA providing diplomatic hosting. For whom: all basin parties, including civil society actors currently excluded from intergovernmental water forums. Through what process: a 90-day trilateral drafting convening producing a co-guardianship proposal for the 2027 UN Water Conference preparatory committee.

**Tools** — (1) Jordan River Co-Guardianship Council (Te Awa Tupua model): designates the river a co-governed legal entity with civil society co-guardians — changes interaction by giving Palestinian civil society and Jordanian Bedouin communities formal governance standing without requiring treaty amendment. (2) Desalination Offset Credit mechanism: allows Israel to publicly report Sea of Galilee diversion reductions proportional to its desalination surplus — changes coordination by making a unilateral technical arrangement into a multilateral visible commitment. (3) UN Water Conference Restoration Pledge: non-binding basin-state commitment to annual ecological flow reporting — changes learning by creating international peer-accountability pressure without treaty obligation.

**Metrics** — (1) Annual volumetric flow reaching the Dead Sea from the Jordan River: measure in MCM/year via flow gauging at Damiya Bridge; baseline 30–100 MCM; target 200 MCM by 2030. (2) Dead Sea surface level: measure in metres relative to 1978 baseline via satellite altimetry; target: arrest of annual decline. (3) Number of basin civil society organisations with formal observer or co-guardian standing in any intergovernmental Jordan River body: count via UN Water Conference secretariat registration; current: 0; target: ≥5 by 2027.

**90-day commitment** — EcoPeace Middle East, in coordination with the Jordan River Society and ESCWA, convenes a trilateral civil society drafting process to produce the legal text of a Jordan River Co-Guardianship Proposal for submission to the 2027 UN Water Conference preparatory committee. The proposal explicitly preserves bilateral treaty allocations. Israeli, Palestinian, and Jordanian civil society organisations co-sign the submission, establishing trilateral legitimacy without requiring governmental co-signature.
