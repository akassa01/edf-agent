# The Blood Diamond That Wasn't

On December 5, 2011, a London-based organization called Global Witness released a statement unlike any it had produced in its twenty-year existence. It was not accusing a corporation or a government. It was resigning from a body it had helped create. "A rough diamond used by a government to commit atrocities against its own people is every bit as tainted as a diamond used to fund a rebel army," the statement read. The Kimberley Process, Global Witness concluded, "has become an industry marketing tool." The walkout ended a chapter in international governance and exposed the structural flaw that had been visible from the inside for years: a mechanism built to keep blood off diamonds had been designed so that certain kinds of blood didn't count.

The Kimberley Process Certification Scheme was born out of one of the most successful civil society campaigns in modern history. Through the late 1990s, investigative reporting and NGO advocacy — led by Global Witness and Partnership Africa Canada — had established the link between rough diamond sales and the financing of rebel movements that mutilated civilians across Sierra Leone, Liberia, and Angola. The result was a 2002 agreement signed by 74 governments: no certified diamond could originate from a zone of rebel conflict. Consumer campaigns had put the moral weight of the diamond trade on the line. De Beers, unwilling to lose a luxury market, cooperated. The Kimberley Process was operational, and within a decade it was certifying more than 99 percent of world diamond production as "conflict-free."

The definition at the center of that certification was precise: conflict diamonds were "rough diamonds used by rebel movements or their allies to finance military action in opposition to legitimate and internationally recognized governments." Rebels. Against governments. That clause had been necessary in 2002 — without it, every state-to-state diplomatic dispute involving a diamond-producing nation could have triggered a certification challenge. The drafters were solving a specific problem. They did not anticipate that the problem would mutate.

In October 2008, the Zimbabwe military deployed soldiers to the Marange diamond fields in the eastern part of the country, where an uncontrolled rush of artisanal miners had arrived after the discovery of a major deposit. What followed has been documented by Human Rights Watch, Global Witness, and Partnership Africa Canada in extensive, separately corroborated detail: more than 200 people killed in the initial crackdown, forced labor, torture, the relocation of communities to areas without water or sanitation. Soldiers controlled the mining. The Mugabe government exported the diamonds.

Inside the Kimberley Process, there was no procedural path to disqualification. The KPCS definition required rebel movements. Zimbabwe's government was a recognized government; its military was not a rebel army. The victims were its own citizens, but the mechanism had not been built to adjudicate that relationship. Representatives from civil society observer organizations raised the issue at successive KP Plenaries. They presented photographs, satellite imagery, forensic accounts of mass graves. The KP's joint monitoring missions confirmed the violence but described it as "irregular" and heading toward resolution. A Joint Work Plan between Zimbabwe and the KP was drafted. A rehabilitation process was set in motion.

At the 2011 KP Plenary in Kinshasa, the body voted to certify Zimbabwe's Marange diamonds as conflict-free. The vote was not close. The African and diamond-producing blocs were unified. China, a major trade partner for Marange output, had no interest in a definition expansion. The Western bloc — Europe, the United States, Canada — expressed concern but did not break consensus. The system's architecture required unanimity for any definition change, which meant the states with the strongest commercial interest in a low bar held an effective veto.

The humanist reading of this situation begins not with the vote but with the question of standing. Who had the authority to say what "blood diamond" meant? The survivors of the Marange crackdown — people who had watched soldiers kill their neighbors, who had been forced to dig under guard, who had returned to find their homes replaced by mine infrastructure — had one answer. The drafters of the KPCS had another, and their answer was embedded in a legal text that had been negotiated by states without reference to the people it was meant to protect. The KP had successfully mobilized the moral weight of those victims to build its founding legitimacy, then placed that legitimacy inside a governance structure that systematically excluded their testimony from the definition of harm.

The scientist sees adequate evidence. The HRW and Global Witness reports were precise, cross-verified, and unrebutted on the substance. The documentation was not the problem. The problem was that the documentation mapped onto the wrong category. The engineer sees a compliance system that worked as designed and failed as intended: the architecture was built for rebel financing and performed correctly on that criterion; it had no failure mode for state violence because none had been included. Both readings identify real dimensions of the case. Neither alone explains why Global Witness walked out.

The organization left because of a third dimension: the founding moral claim had been appropriated by the compliance mechanism. The KP used the language and the history of blood diamonds — the images of Sierra Leone, the testimony of amputees — to market its certifications, while building a governance structure that made it impossible to apply that history to new evidence. When Global Witness named this, its name for it was "marketing tool." The engineering phrase for it is system capture.

Where was the intervention? Not in Marange, which was unreachable without Zimbabwe's cooperation. Not in the KP's monitoring process, which had done its job and found the evidence. The intervention was a clause in a 2002 document, and the governance mechanism that clause was embedded in. The 18th camel was neither full certification nor expulsion: it was a parallel Human Rights Concern Designation — a second tier, operable at two-thirds majority rather than unanimity, for systematic state violence in extraction sites, with a defined rehabilitation pathway and sunset. This was not a compromise between the two options on the table. It was a structural change that left the rebel-financing definition intact while creating a second lane. It required persuading the bloc most threatened by human rights scrutiny to accept a governance change that would constrain them. It was not persuaded.

(The flag: consensus governance protects small members from great-power domination — the same feature that made the KP safe for Namibia and Botswana made it incapable of excluding Zimbabwe. A certification body whose amendment procedure requires unanimity ossifies around the interests of its most powerful members, regardless of its founding moral purpose. Principled pragmatism can redesign the mechanism. It cannot change the fact that the redesign requires the consent of those who profit most from the current design.)

---

**Problem** — Whether to certify as "conflict-free" diamonds extracted from state-controlled Zimbabwean fields where the government's own military killed hundreds of civilians, and whether the KP's definition of harm can be amended to cover that violence.

**Stakeholders** — Marange massacre survivors and displaced communities (direct harm; excluded from the definition); Global Witness and Partnership Africa Canada (moral legitimacy providers; formal observer standing only); Zimbabwe's government (producer; effective veto through consensus requirement); African diamond-producing bloc (commercial and precedent interests in narrow definition); China (commercial importer; veto-aligned); Western KP member governments (rhetorical opposition; chose consensus over walkout); global diamond consumers (moral claim; no formal standing in KP governance).

**Binding constraint** — Unanimity requirement: no KPCS definition change or new designation can pass without every member's agreement, including those who benefit from the current definition.

**Decision pathway** — Redesign: create a parallel two-tier certification system in which the rebel-financing "conflict diamond" definition operates under unanimity, but a new Human Rights Concern Designation operates under two-thirds majority, with suspension rather than permanent exclusion, and a defined rehabilitation pathway.

**Tools** — (1) Joint fact-finding panel with civil society co-equal evidentiary standing, replacing advisory status: changes learning by creating a category of facts no member can formally contest. (2) Two-tier designation architecture: changes coordination by allowing majority decisions on human rights suspensions without reopening the foundational rebel-financing definition. (3) Independent civil society evidence standard: changes interaction by giving NGO documentation formal procedural weight rather than moral suasion alone.

**Metrics** — Volume of diamonds under Human Rights Concern Designation as a share of total certified output (baseline: zero; annual reporting). Time from civil society complaint to formal KP review (target: ≤90 days). Count of civil society organizations with active formal KP observer standing (Global Witness exit is the baseline; recovery toward founding engagement levels is the measure).

**90-day commitment** — In the counterfactual (action available before the 2010 Plenary): weeks 1–4, EU, US, and Canada KP chairs jointly propose a Human Rights Concern Designation amendment at two-thirds threshold, explicitly framed as not touching the conflict-diamond definition. Weeks 5–12, coordinate with African bloc to offer a face-saving "transitional zone" framework with rehabilitation benchmarks, framing the tier as protecting all members from impossible standards rather than targeting Zimbabwe. Weeks 13–90, negotiate a phased rehabilitation pathway for Zimbabwe with documented, measurable milestones and a two-year sunset on the designation if benchmarks are met.
