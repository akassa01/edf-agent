The seat has an occupant. That is the problem.

The Russian Association of Indigenous Peoples of the North — RAIPON — holds one of six Permanent Participant seats at the Arctic Council, the intergovernmental body that has governed scientific cooperation and environmental monitoring across the Arctic since 1996. Permanent Participant status is not a technicality. It is a structural recognition, built into the Council's founding Ottawa Declaration, that the peoples who have inhabited the Arctic for millennia are not observers to decisions about their territory but parties to them, with standing equal to that of the eight member states in most deliberations. The Aleuts, the Inuit, the Gwich'in, the Saami — their organizations hold seats because the Council's architects understood that Arctic governance without Arctic peoples was governance without knowledge.

RAIPON's seat, as of 2026, does not represent Russian Arctic indigenous peoples. It represents the Kremlin.

In 2013, Kremlin-aligned actors completed a takeover of RAIPON's board. In April 2022, as Russian forces were advancing into Ukraine, RAIPON's new leadership issued a public statement of support for the invasion — a statement that the 40 indigenous peoples' organizations RAIPON nominally represents had no role in crafting and that many explicitly repudiated from exile. By July 2024, Russia's Ministry of Justice had declared dozens of independent Arctic indigenous organizations as extremist, cutting off any remaining domestic pathway to authentic representation. The people most exposed to the climate consequences of Arctic change in Russia — the Nenets reindeer herders whose pasture is vanishing as permafrost thaws, the Khanty and Even communities whose rivers and fisheries are altering faster than management frameworks can track — now have no authentic voice in the institution built to respond to the emergency they are living inside.

The Scientist at the Council understands what this costs before the moral accounting begins. Russia holds 53 percent of the Arctic coastline and operates the world's largest terrestrial carbon monitoring network over Siberian permafrost. That permafrost is not a geological curiosity; it is the largest single land-based carbon store on the planet, and its thaw — accelerating as surface temperatures in the High Arctic rise four times faster than the global average — is a feedback loop that cannot be modeled without ground-based monitoring data. As of January 2024, researchers at Aarhus University were warning publicly that missing Russian data was causing Arctic climate blind spots that degraded every forecast model the Council's scientific working groups were producing. The AMAP Climate Change Update 2024 carried acknowledged geographic gaps. And in August 2026, NOAA ended administrative support for the Arctic Report Card, eliminating the primary public-facing synthesis of Arctic climate change that had run annually since 2006. The Council is operating two simultaneous data crises — one driven by political isolation, one by administrative withdrawal — at the moment when the data most urgently matters.

The Humanist sees what the data crisis obscures. Indigenous traditional ecological knowledge — TEK, in the Council's taxonomy — is not a supplement to scientific monitoring. It is a distinct epistemological register, capturing decadal and generational patterns that instrument networks cannot reconstruct. Nenets herders have observed permafrost dynamics across migration routes for centuries; their knowledge of freeze-thaw cycles, of where ice bridges form and where they do not, represents a longitudinal record that no AMAP working group can replicate by other means. RAIPON's capture does not merely exclude a community from governance. It cuts the Council off from a knowledge system that exists nowhere else, held by people whose experience of climate change is not an abstraction but a transformation of the landscape they have always read as text.

The Engineer's question is whether anything in the existing institutional machinery can address this without triggering institutional collapse. The answer is not comfortable. The Ottawa Declaration's consensus architecture requires the agreement of all eight member states and all six Permanent Participants to change any formal rule. Russia will not agree to any modification of RAIPON's standing. Denmark's 2025-2027 chairmanship program commits to "strengthening Indigenous Peoples' participation" — a sentence whose content, under examination, turns out to mean improving process inclusion for the five organizations that already function, not addressing the one that doesn't. Denmark's Greenlandic leadership gives the chairmanship authentic moral credibility on indigenous rights; it does not give it a mechanism for doing what the Ottawa Declaration does not permit.

But the engineer also notes what the institution can do without rule changes. Arctic Council working groups already use an "invited expert" mechanism that permits contributions from individuals who are not organizational members. Norway's foreign minister acknowledged in May 2025 that exiled Russian indigenous representatives "can apply and register" for Council meetings — an informal precedent that individual participation is legally permissible. The machinery for a partial fix exists. The institutional culture has avoided using it.

Where is X in a prospective case? Not in a moment that has already passed, but in a decision window that is still open: the Senior Arctic Officials meeting under Denmark's chairship in 2025-2026, where the Chair must decide whether to table any guidance on the invited-expert mechanism before the term ends in 2027. By not issuing guidance, Denmark implicitly extends the status quo for two more years. The intervention point is not the Permanent Participant structure itself — that requires consensus — but the working group chairs' standing guidance, which the chairship controls unilaterally.

The eighteenth camel here is not formal recognition of exiled organizations, which requires Russia's veto. It is a Scientific Heritage Protocol: chairmanship guidance to all six working group chairs specifying that contributions from "indigenous knowledge holders participating in their personal scientific capacity" will be accepted through the existing invited-expert mechanism and labeled as "traditional ecological knowledge submitted to the Arctic scientific heritage" rather than as organizational submissions. RAIPON retains its formal seat. Russian experts from exile communities contribute scientific knowledge through a technical channel that Russia cannot formally veto without blocking its own working group participation — because the mechanism is one Russia itself uses to participate while its financial contributions remain suspended. The rights and representation question stays unresolved; the knowledge-flow question becomes tractable. The eighteenth camel is a bridge over the governance fracture, not a repair of the river.

The honest flag is this: the Arctic Council's Permanent Participant architecture assumed that indigenous organizations would remain genuinely independent of states. The Ottawa Declaration built institutional standing for civil society on that assumption. It contains no mechanism for adjudicating when the assumption fails — no procedure for determining that a Permanent Participant no longer represents its constituency, and no recourse that does not require the consent of the state that captured the organization. The problem Denmark faces is not a design problem it can solve within its two-year chairship. It is a structural failure of the Council's founding logic. What Denmark can do is begin building the parallel infrastructure — knowledge registries, expert-participation channels, informal relationships — that a future governance reform would need to function. That infrastructure will not fix the governance fracture. It will make a fix possible when conditions eventually permit.

**Problem** — Whether Denmark's 2025-2027 chairmanship should establish a working mechanism for authentic Russian Arctic indigenous knowledge contribution outside the RAIPON framework, using existing invited-expert rules that do not require Russian consent.

**Stakeholders** — Russian Arctic indigenous peoples in exile, who hold irreplaceable traditional ecological knowledge and no authentic representation; the five functioning Permanent Participant organizations, who have an interest in the institution's legitimacy and in not destabilizing the consensus architecture; Denmark's Greenlandic leadership, whose moral authority on indigenous rights makes inaction symbolically costly; Russia, which retains veto power over formal rule changes and minimal working-group participation that would be threatened by any mechanism it perceives as politically motivated; the Council's scientific working groups, which need Russian Arctic data to maintain forecast model integrity.

**Binding constraint** — The Ottawa Declaration's consensus requirement. Any formal change to RAIPON's Permanent Participant status requires Russia's agreement, which will not be given.

**Decision pathway** — Reframe: convert the RAIPON legitimacy question (a political problem with no available solution) into a knowledge-contribution question (a technical problem with an existing mechanism).

**Tools** — Working group expert guidance (chairship-issued, no formal consensus required): specifies that TEK from indigenous knowledge holders in personal capacity is welcome through existing invited-expert channels; labels such contributions as "Arctic scientific heritage" rather than organizational submissions. Arctic indigenous knowledge registry: documents TEK contributions as a scientific heritage asset that persists beyond any single chairship. Chairship side-event series: convenes Russian indigenous communities in exile with functioning Permanent Participants for informal knowledge-sharing, building cooperation infrastructure outside the formal structure.

**Metrics** — TEK contribution rate: number of knowledge contributions from Russian Arctic indigenous experts (personal capacity) to AMAP and other working group outputs, tracked annually by working group secretariats. Exiled community contact rate: number of Russian indigenous communities in exile with active, documented data-sharing arrangements with Council working groups, tracked by the chairship secretariat.

**90-day commitment** — Within 90 days of Denmark's first Senior Arctic Officials meeting, the chairship issues guidance to all six working group chairs specifying that "indigenous knowledge holders participating in their personal scientific capacity" may contribute through existing invited-expert mechanisms, and that such contributions will be labeled as "traditional ecological knowledge submitted to the Arctic scientific heritage." Denmark simultaneously convenes an informal consultation with representatives of Russian Arctic indigenous communities in exile to document available knowledge and establish individual-level contacts with relevant working group projects.
