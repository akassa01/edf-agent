# The Settlement They Could Not Accept

New Delhi, February 14, 1989. Chief Justice R.S. Pathak calls the Supreme Court to order for what everyone in the room understands will be a brief session. Five years after the gas leak at Union Carbide's Bhopal plant killed thousands in a single night — the exact number has never been agreed upon — a settlement has been reached. The Indian government will accept $470 million from Union Carbide. All civil claims will be extinguished. All pending criminal proceedings will be quashed. The Chief Justice reviews the terms and approves.

Outside, in Bhopal, the women of the gas-affected neighborhoods have gathered again. The Bhopal Gas Peedit Mahila Udyog Sangathan has been organizing since 1984: documenting cases, accompanying families to hospitals that did not know how to treat methyl isocyanate poisoning, demanding that someone be named responsible. They were not invited to this negotiation. Under the Bhopal Gas Leak Disaster (Processing of Claims) Act, passed by Parliament in 1985, the Government of India holds exclusive authority to litigate on their behalf. They have no standing in their own case. They learn the terms from the newspapers.

The survivor organizations were not without analysis. They understood that the settlement was calculated on Union Carbide's own casualty estimates — not on ICMR studies still underway, not on their own count of the dying. They understood that the per-claimant payout would amount to roughly ₹25,000, approximately $300 at the time. And they understood that for the community, no dollar figure resolved the question the disaster had opened: whether a corporation could kill thousands of people and buy its way clear of the consequences.

The mode the community leaned on — the feeling that money was not justice, that justice required criminal accountability for the men who had made the decisions — was not irrational. It was internally coherent, morally serious, and deeply felt. It was also the mode that crowded out the mode that could have helped the people already dying of chronic respiratory disease in the gas-affected lanes of Bhopal: the doing-mode, which asks what is actually buildable and who can build it now.

The Scientist's reading of February 14, 1989 is uncomfortable. The death toll was genuinely unknown — Union Carbide claimed approximately 3,800 deaths; ICMR data was emerging; Amnesty International would eventually document over 22,000 excess deaths across two decades. The settlement's $470 million was calculated on the lowest defensible estimate, with no adjustment mechanism if the toll proved larger. The chemical exposure — MIC, hydrogen cyanide, and other gases — had no prior industrial literature for its long-term effects. The Scientist sees a settlement priced on the cheapest assumption and stamped final.

The Humanist sees the families who came to the survivor organizations' offices. They were not asking to be compensated; they were asking to be recognized — that what was done to them had a name, that the name included someone's personal responsibility, that the law had a mechanism for saying so. The ₹25,000 the settlement produced per claimant was not recognition. It was a transaction that closed the case without answering the question.

The Engineer asks what $470 million could have built. Respiratory clinics, rehabilitation centers, vocational programs for the thousands left partially disabled, longitudinal health monitoring of the kind that might have caught second-generation effects documented years later. The Engineer watches those needs go unmet and traces the line back to February 1989: the legal dispute over the settlement's adequacy — which the survivors were right to wage, on the facts — effectively froze the money's deployment while people continued to die of treatable conditions. The Engineer does not disagree with the Humanist about what justice required. The Engineer notes what the Humanist's stand cost, in clinics not built, in years, in lives.

Where is X? The Supreme Court hearing of February 14–15, 1989. Chief Justice Pathak's bench had the procedural authority to impose a 90-day consultation period, to invite survivor organizations as formal intervenors, to require a health baseline assessment before approving a settlement figure calculated on disputed data. The leverage point was not the amount — that would have required a different negotiation — but the procedural moment that locked the settlement's structure before survivors could formally contest it.

The binary looked like this: (A) accept the settlement — civil compensation and criminal quashing bundled — or (B) reject it and fight on in courts with no funding flowing and no legal standing in the government's case. The 18th camel was a third thing: accept the civil funds for medical infrastructure while the government formally decoupled the criminal proceedings — issuing a declaration that the settlement covered civil liability only and that criminal charges against Warren Anderson remained an active, separate track under Indian law. The Supreme Court's own ruling in October 1991 — which struck down the criminal quashing provision at survivor petition, while leaving the civil settlement intact — proves this separation was legally achievable. Someone had to ask for it in February 1989, with the standing to be heard.

1+1 produced three separate negotiations. The Indian government was answering: how do we close this diplomatically without destroying the investment climate? Union Carbide was answering: how do we achieve finality and cap aggregate liability? The survivor organizations were answering: how do we hold the corporation criminally accountable and ensure the people dying now get medical care? Three separate questions. Only the first two had seats at the table.

A principled pragmatist, working from 1985: file for intervenor status in the government case under India's public interest litigation doctrine — survivor organizations had the constitutional basis. Commission an independent health baseline from ICMR with survivor-designated epidemiologists before the settlement figure is set. Insist that any settlement explicitly decouples civil from criminal liability. These three acts would have changed the structure of what was on the table on February 14. They required political will from a government under pressure to close the case quickly. That is, precisely, what principled pragmatism asks of someone with standing.

*(The flag: when a corporation's senior officers can reside in a jurisdiction that will not extradite them, criminal accountability is structurally inaccessible regardless of how well the civil settlement is designed. Warren Anderson died in Vero Beach, Florida, in 2014, having never appeared before an Indian court. The Indian Supreme Court issued its warrant; the US courts declined extradition. Principled pragmatism can design the civil/criminal severability mechanism. It cannot create the jurisdictional authority that international corporate law in 1989 did not provide. The gap between the framework's prescription and the law's reach was not a negotiation failure. It was the architecture of the world as it was.)*

---

**Problem** — A survivor community stripped of legal standing by statute and excluded from settlement negotiations must secure a civil settlement that funds urgent medical care without surrendering the right to pursue criminal accountability, when the criminal track is structurally inaccessible regardless of what the settlement says.

**Stakeholders** — Bhopal gas survivors (medical care, criminal accountability, participatory dignity); Indian government (diplomatic closure, investor confidence); Union Carbide (civil finality, limitation of aggregate liability); Indian Supreme Court (constitutional legitimacy of the settlement process).

**Binding constraint** — Survivor organizations had no formal standing in the government case and could not procedurally block settlement approval; only contest it afterward — which they did successfully on the criminal quashing issue, but not until 1991, two years later.

**Decision pathway** — Reframe: not "accept or reject this settlement" but "change the structure of what is on the table by requiring civil/criminal decoupling and a health baseline as prior conditions of any approval."

**Tools** — Public interest litigation petition for intervenor status (changes interaction: survivor organizations gain procedural standing in a case that excluded them by statute); health baseline commission with survivor-designated epidemiologists (changes learning: puts a jointly produced epidemiological floor under any settlement figure); civil/criminal severability declaration by the Attorney General (changes coordination: makes the legal architecture reflect what the 1991 ruling confirmed was always achievable).

**Metrics** — Criminal proceedings status: whether criminal case against Anderson remains procedurally active post-settlement; medical infrastructure: clinics and rehabilitation centers operating within 36 months of settlement approval; survivor representation: formal intervenor status in settlement hearing recorded in Supreme Court case record.

**90-day commitment** — Counterfactual, beginning late 1985: survivor organizations file for intervenor status under Indian PIL doctrine; ICMR commissions baseline health study with survivor-designated co-investigators; the Attorney General publicly states that any civil settlement will not extinguish the criminal case. Each act costs nothing except the political will to name the distinction between civil and criminal accountability before the negotiation closes.
