# The River That Cannot Wait

On April 23, 2025 — the day after militants killed 26 tourists in Pahalgam, Kashmir — India's Ministry of External Affairs declared that the 1960 Indus Waters Treaty would be held "in abeyance" pending Pakistan's "credible and irrevocable" abandonment of cross-border terrorism support. The Permanent Indus Commission, which under Article VIII of the treaty is required to meet at least annually to exchange hydrological data and issue flood warnings, did not meet. The gauge stations on the Jhelum and Chenab stopped sending their numbers south. The treaty had survived two India-Pakistan wars and the 1971 Liberation War. It had not survived the April announcement.

The treaty's architects spent nearly a decade designing a split precise enough to look mechanical: Pakistan receives the three western rivers (Indus, Jhelum, Chenab), supplying roughly 80% of the system's annual flow; India receives the three eastern rivers. India may build hydroelectric projects on the western rivers, but only as run-of-river plants within strict design limits. The Permanent Indus Commission — one commissioner per country, meeting annually — would manage day-to-day questions of a shared hydraulic system. For 65 years, it did.

What the architects could not anticipate was Kishanganga (330 MW, diverting Jhelum tributary water through a 23-km tunnel) and Ratle (850 MW, on the Chenab) — projects Pakistan argued exceeded the treaty's design envelopes. In August 2016, Pakistan filed at the Permanent Court of Arbitration in The Hague. India refused to participate. On July 6, 2023, the Court unanimously rejected India's jurisdictional objections and proceeded in absentia. The August 2025 general-interpretation award ruled that India must allow western river waters to flow for Pakistan's unrestricted use. India rejected it as null and void. A final merits award is still pending, with no enforcement mechanism for any of it.

The Scientist's reading begins with the river, which does not wait for litigation. The Indus system drains the Hindu Kush-Karakoram-Himalaya glaciers — retreating at documented rates, initially elevating flows before a sharper long-term decline. The 1960 treaty was negotiated using mid-century flow data and contains no provision for climate variability. With data-sharing suspended, both countries are operating on satellite estimates where they once had gauge readings. Precision and accuracy are not synonyms: the PCA proceedings address the precise design parameters of the Ratle reservoir; the accuracy problem — is there enough water for Sindhi farmers this season? — is addressed by nobody.

The Humanist asks who lives at the end of the irrigation canals. Approximately 80% of Pakistan's cultivated land — 16 million hectares — draws from the western rivers. In Sindh Province, downstream of Punjab's own overallocation, farmers at the tail ends of irrigation channels are the most food-insecure and the furthest from the proceedings. Neither they nor Kashmiri communities beside the disputed dams were present in the 1960 negotiations or at the PCA. Pakistan's government declared any withholding of water "an act of war" — a formulation with a political address in Islamabad and a humanitarian impact address in rural Sindh. These are different places.

The Engineer asks: what is actually broken? The treaty's allocation of rivers has not changed. The dispute is about hydroelectric design parameters — a legal question the PCA is already answering. What has broken is something smaller and more immediately consequential: the PIC, which measured the river and told both countries what it read. Suspending the PIC as a political response to the Pahalgam attack is like switching off a shared weather radar after a border incident. The radar has no national loyalty. Its absence hurts both sides, and it hurts the downstream population that caused nothing far more than it hurts anyone in either capital.

This is the 1+1 problem precisely. India asks: how do we respond to state-sponsored terrorism while maintaining our development rights? Pakistan asks: how do we enforce our water rights under a treaty India is extralaterally suspending? They believe they are in the same negotiation. They are not. India is negotiating about Kashmir. Pakistan is negotiating about water. They share a document. They do not share a question.

Where is X? Not the Court, which will issue its merits award regardless. Not the modification notice India served in January 2023 under Article XII(3) — a request with no legal mechanism for unilateral enforcement. The intervention point is Article VIII: the PIC meeting obligation, which the treaty's architects placed precisely because they understood that a river requires continuous management that no court proceeding can substitute for. Restarting the PIC on a strictly technical mandate — "we meet to manage the river, not to negotiate the treaty" — costs neither party its legal position.

The apparent binary is: India maintains the abeyance until Pakistan makes security concessions, or Pakistan agrees to talks outside the PCA and thereby undermines its legal claims. The 18th camel is an Indus Basin Climate-Technical Protocol — a limited-scope instrument, not a treaty modification and not a concession in the arbitration — that restores four PIC functions: annual data exchange, flood warning, glacier monitoring (which the 1960 treaty lacks entirely), and scheduled inspection of treaty-relevant works. The Protocol explicitly excludes all questions of allocation, pondage limits, and modification. Its political value is its narrowness: neither party concedes anything about the Kishanganga design rules by agreeing to share discharge readings. The World Bank, as treaty guarantor, has the institutional standing to convene it.

*(The flag: the 18th camel here requires a political prerequisite — that India's abeyance is tactical rather than strategic. If the abeyance is a strategic tool for renegotiating a treaty India has already declared inadequate, rather than a security response that ends when security conditions change, there is no political window for the Protocol. Principled pragmatism can design the Protocol and name the World Bank as the convener with treaty standing. It cannot determine whether the political condition exists — because that condition is inside a security calculation that India rationally does not make transparent. Engineering diplomacy correctly identifies the move. It cannot force the move when a party's strategic interest is to keep the technical and political fused.)*

**Problem** — Given that India has placed the Indus Waters Treaty in extralegal abeyance — suspending the joint technical cooperation that 200 million downstream Pakistanis depend on — while the PCA issues binding awards India refuses to implement, is there a pathway to restart hydrological data-sharing and flood-warning cooperation that neither party must frame as political concession?

**Stakeholders** — Downstream Pakistani communities in Sindh and Punjab (most exposed; zero standing in proceedings); Kashmiri communities on both sides of the Line of Control (live beside the disputed dams; unrepresented in treaty or arbitration); India's water engineers (understand the technical case for data-sharing; politically constrained); Pakistan's Ministry of Water Resources (pressing the legal case while the river goes unmonitored); the World Bank (treaty guarantor; publicly declined to intervene, May 2025)

**Binding constraint** — India's decision to link technical cooperation to political security concessions, making any re-engagement readable domestically as capitulation — mirrored by Pakistan's constraint that partial cooperation without PCA implementation reads as abandoning its legal claims

**Decision pathway** — Redesign: not restore the 1960 PIC mechanism as-is (contested and climate-inadequate), not negotiate a comprehensive new treaty (politically impossible), but redesign the technical cooperation architecture under a narrowly scoped new instrument — the Indus Basin Climate-Technical Protocol — that separates hydrological management from the legal dispute and adds glacier monitoring the 1960 treaty lacks

**Tools** — Indus Basin Climate-Technical Protocol (changes interaction: converts the PIC from a political hostage into a professional technical service, explicitly without prejudice to either party's legal position); World Bank-convened Technical Working Group (changes learning: produces shared hydrological data both countries need for domestic water planning); UNEP-funded glacier monitoring under a neutral institutional umbrella (changes coordination: creates an agreed-in-advance data record that reduces the scope of future technical disputes)

**Metrics** — PIC or equivalent technical meeting frequency (target: at least annual, within 6 months of Protocol adoption); hydrological data exchange completeness (target: 80% of pre-April 2025 data flows restored within 12 months, UNEP-audited); flood warning lead time for downstream communities (target: maintain or improve pre-abeyance capacity); formal exploratory talks on climate adaptation to the treaty (timeline: within 18 months of Protocol adoption)

**90-day commitment** — By November 5, 2026: the World Bank, invoking its treaty guarantor role and Article VIII's mandatory PIC meeting requirement, convenes a Technical Restoration Meeting in Vienna or Geneva, with a standing agenda covering flood-warning data exchange on the Jhelum and Chenab (90-day trial), commission of a joint climate hydrology review (12-month deliverable), and a written agreement that the meeting is "without prejudice" to both the PCA proceedings and India's abeyance declaration. Neither party concedes its legal or political position. Both parties restore flood-warning capacity before the next monsoon season.
