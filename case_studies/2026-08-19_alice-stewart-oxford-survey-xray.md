# The Survey Nobody Read: Alice Stewart and the Governance of Contested Data

In the autumn of 1956, Alice Stewart stood before a room of British obstetricians and radiologists to present what she believed was the most important finding of her career. She was fifty years old, a senior researcher in social medicine at Oxford, and she had spent four years building the most methodologically careful case-control study of childhood cancer that British medicine had assembled. The finding was this: a single diagnostic X-ray during pregnancy roughly doubled a child's risk of dying of leukemia before the age of ten. The room's response was not scientific curiosity. It was professional anger. "The antagonism was from the medical profession," she later said. "Obstetricians and radiologists didn't like being told what they could do by me or by anyone else."

Within months, the Medical Research Council — Britain's premier medical research institution — had made its decision. It commissioned a counter-study to be conducted by Michael Court-Brown, Richard Doll, and Austin Bradford Hill: the three most credentialed epidemiologists in the country. The MRC was not commissioning a study to evaluate whether Stewart's findings warranted a change in clinical practice. It was commissioning a study to determine whether those findings were true — a subtle but decisive difference. Until the study reported, the existing guidance would stand. While the guidance stood, the practice continued.

Stewart was not an outsider to the British medical establishment. She had been the first woman elected to the Executive Committee of the Medical Research Council. She had run clinical trials during the Second World War. Her Oxford Survey of Childhood Cancers — 1,299 cancer deaths in children under ten, matched against 1,299 healthy controls, traced back through their mothers' medical histories — was a piece of work that her methodology fully supported. She knew how to run a case-control study. She had done the calculations from multiple angles and the signal held.

The problem was not her method. It was her framework.

The International Commission on Radiological Protection, the body that set global radiation safety standards, had built its models from a different dataset: acute, high-dose exposures — Hiroshima survivors, radium dial painters, early therapeutic radiation cases. From these it derived thresholds: below a certain dose, safe; above it, harmful. The obstetric X-ray exposures Stewart studied fell well below every threshold in the table. To the ICRP's physicists and radiobiologists, "below threshold" meant "safe." To Stewart's epidemiology, it meant a doubled risk of childhood leukemia. Both called the result "radiation safety." Neither party had noticed that they were computing answers to different questions using the same vocabulary.

The Scientist's reading of this episode notices the framework mismatch with precision. Two valid methods — physics threshold modeling and population epidemiology — produced genuinely incommensurable answers to a question that only looked like one question. The threshold model said: the dose is below the known harm level. The epidemiological model said: the population data show elevated risk at this dose in this specific exposure window. To resolve them required acknowledging that the ICRP's founding model had not been designed for low-dose in utero risk — a category the model had no column for. This was not a failure of either method; it was a failure to recognize that the two methods were not answering the same question.

The Humanist's reading notices who paid the cost of the institutional delay. The children who died of leukemia through the 1960s were born after the evidence existed. They were dispersed across thousands of hospitals and GP lists, invisible in aggregate to any individual practitioner who would have needed to change a single habit. No obstetrician saw enough cases to feel the pattern. The Humanist observes that harm dispersal is not accidental — it is a structural feature of how statistical risk distributes across a population, and it reliably makes inaction feel tolerable in ways that concentrated harm never could. No one harmed enough people in a single practice to feel responsible.

The Engineer's reading notices what was missing from the governance architecture. No mechanism existed in 1956 for a published epidemiological finding — however rigorous — to compel the ICRP to formally review its guidance. The ICRP was a private commission; it had no external accountability structure; it had not been designed to respond to findings that challenged its founding methodology. The MRC counter-study was, from an engineering perspective, the most institutionally efficient response available in a system with no more direct pathway: deploy the maximum credentialed authority to occupy the field for five years, during which the question would be treated as "awaiting scientific resolution" rather than "requiring interim precautionary action." The governance machine worked precisely as designed. The design itself was the problem.

Where is X? Not in The Lancet and not at the ICRP's annual meeting in Geneva, though both matter. The precise intervention point is the ICRP's obstetric sub-committee agenda in 1957 — the first formal opportunity after the September 1956 Lancet publication to add a mandatory review of Stewart's findings to the commission's official work programme. A single item on that agenda, requiring a written formal response within eighteen months, would have obligated the commission to publicly engage with the challenge rather than quietly defer to the counter-study. All the clinical practice consequences — every routine pelvimetry performed in every uncomplicated pregnancy in Britain and beyond through 1970 — sat downstream of that agenda item. One line, at one meeting, that was never added.

The question 1+1 produced: the ICRP computed zero — the dose is below threshold, therefore safe. Stewart computed two — the population risk is doubled. Neither realized until Brian MacMahon's 1962 Harvard confirmation study (covering 734,000 births, finding a 42% elevated cancer risk) that the frameworks had produced incompatible answers to the same clinical situation. By then the counter-study was complete, the findings were broadly confirmed, and the practice had been allowed to continue for six more years while the "awaiting confirmation" posture held.

The 18th camel was present but invisible. The binary as presented was: accept Stewart's data and reverse obstetric X-ray guidance entirely, or reject her data and continue. The option outside that binary: a precautionary interim recommendation restricting routine pelvimetry to clinically indicated cases — not a ban on diagnostic X-ray in pregnancy, but a distinction between routine (every uncomplicated pelvic measurement) and indicated (specific clinical concern) — pending a multi-center replication study with a defined completion timeline. This was executable with the tools at hand. It required no new science, no biological mechanism, no certainty. It required only the institutional decision to treat "our threshold model was not designed to cover this exposure type" as sufficient grounds for interim precaution. No one proposed it because no one with standing to do so was in the room. Stewart was not at the ICRP table. The governance architecture had no seat for the person with the most relevant data.

The pathway that was available at the decisive moment in 1957 was Reframe: not "convince the ICRP that the data are valid" — the MRC counter-study would do that, eventually — but "change the governance rule so the ICRP has a mandatory response obligation." An external review mandate, requiring formal acknowledgment of published challenges within eighteen months, combined with a joint notice from the Royal Colleges to practitioners recommending precautionary restriction pending review, and a funded multi-center replication protocol with a completion deadline. None of these required waiting for certainty. All of them required someone with institutional standing to propose them, in a room where Stewart had no standing at all.

Brian MacMahon's Harvard study reported in 1962: 42% elevated risk, 734,000 births, unmistakably significant. The British and American medical establishments received it quietly. By 1970, routine pelvimetry in uncomplicated pregnancies had largely stopped. Fourteen years after the original finding. One cohort of children later.

(The flag: scientific governance bodies that simultaneously set practice standards and control the review process for those standards carry a structural conflict of interest that the quality of an individual scientist's data cannot resolve. Stewart's data were sound enough from the beginning; they were never the problem. The problem was a governance architecture designed by physicists to evaluate physics-based evidence, with no mandatory pathway for epidemiological challenges and no accountability to the populations bearing the unreviewed risk. Principled pragmatism can identify the intervention — the mandatory review rule, the precautionary interim guideline — but cannot install it without access to the political authority that designed the governance in the first place. That installation is not a scientific act.)

---

**Problem** — When published epidemiological evidence contradicts the dominant physics-based radiation safety framework, how can clinical practice change when the governing body has no mandatory response pathway and can defer indefinitely by commissioning counter-studies?

**Stakeholders** — Alice Stewart (rigorous findings, no seat at the governing table); ICRP physicists and radiobiologists (custodians of a framework the data challenge); obstetricians and radiologists (professional identity at stake); pregnant women and their children (bearing the risk without representation in the governance); the nuclear industry (with a structural interest in preserving the threshold-only model of radiation safety across all applications).

**Binding constraint** — The absence of a mandatory response pathway in the ICRP's governance. No rule required a formal review of published epidemiological challenges within any defined period. This converted the MRC counter-study from a scientific instrument into an institutionally sanctioned deferral mechanism.

**Decision pathway** — Reframe: from "convince the ICRP that the data are valid" (a strategy that requires the commission's voluntary cooperation) to "change the governance rule so that the ICRP must formally respond" (a strategy that creates an obligation the commission cannot avoid).

**Tools** — Mandatory external review protocol (ICRP required to formally respond to any peer-reviewed epidemiological challenge within eighteen months); precautionary interim clinical guideline issued by the Royal Colleges (restricting routine pelvimetry to indicated cases pending formal review); multi-center replication protocol with a defined reporting deadline (creating a scheduled resolution date rather than an open-ended wait for "consensus").

**Metrics** — Rate of routine pelvimetry in uncomplicated pregnancies (hospital records audit, direct measure of practice change); childhood leukemia incidence in birth cohorts following guideline change (cancer registry, 3–5 year lag expected between exposure reduction and measured outcome); ICRP formal response rate to published peer-reviewed challenges to existing guidance (committee minutes, measure of governance accountability).

**90-day commitment** — Within 90 days of the September 1, 1956 Lancet publication: a joint notice from the Royal College of Obstetricians and Gynaecologists and the Royal College of Radiologists, acknowledging the Stewart findings, recommending precautionary restriction of routine pelvimetry in uncomplicated pregnancies pending a formal review panel report due in six months, and naming a three-person review panel independent of both the MRC counter-study team and the ICRP commission.
