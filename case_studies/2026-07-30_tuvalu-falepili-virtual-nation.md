# The Nation Without Ground

On November 9, 2023, in Rarotonga, Cook Islands, Prime Minister Kausea Natano signed the Falepili Union with Australian Prime Minister Anthony Albanese. Natano chose the treaty's name from the Tuvaluan concept of neighborly obligation — falepili, the duty of proximity and reciprocal care. He framed the signing as choosing survival over pride. Former Prime Minister Enele Sopoaga, who had governed Tuvalu for six of the previous nine years, called it an auction of sovereignty.

Both descriptions were accurate. They were also describing different things.

The treaty's structure is straightforward: Australia recognizes Tuvalu's continuing statehood and provides up to 280 Tuvaluan nationals per year the right to live, work, and study in Australia, with access to welfare and healthcare. In exchange, Article 4.4 requires Tuvalu to mutually agree with Australia any security or defence arrangement it makes with any other state. The first visa ballot opened June 16, 2025. Migration has begun. The question that the treaty did not answer — and that the current Tuvalu government must now answer, under PM Feleti Teo, against a clock — is what Tuvalu is after its people have gone.

The Scientist notes that "after its people have gone" is itself a contested projection. Sea level rise in the central Pacific runs at approximately 5mm per year, roughly twice the global average, and most of Funafuti lies below two meters of elevation. IPCC AR6 projects significant inundation risk by 2100 under medium emissions scenarios. But Kench and colleagues published findings in Nature Communications in 2018 showing that Tuvalu's total land area actually increased by 2.9 percent between 1971 and 2014, as wave dynamics and sediment deposition partially counteracted sea-level rise. The timeline for uninhabitability is not a fixed engineering parameter. It depends on coral reef health, storm surge frequency, freshwater lens stability, and emissions trajectories that have not yet resolved. A 50-year planning horizon is scientifically defensible alongside a 10-year one. The Falepili Union was signed without a public joint assessment of these competing scenarios — without establishing what trigger conditions would shift governance architecture from "adaptation mode" to "dispersal mode." The science was clear enough for urgency. It was not clear enough to supply the design specifications.

The Humanist sees the treaty as a story about dignity and erasure simultaneously. Foreign Minister Simon Kofe delivered his 2021 COP26 address standing knee-deep in water on a submerged reef, in a suit and tie, in a image of refusal to be made invisible. Natano's signing carried the same register: Tuvalu will not quietly disappear. But the humanist imperative — keep our people safe — does not automatically preserve what makes Tuvaluans a people rather than a diaspora community. The Falepili Union grants a visa pathway. It does not answer who will vote in Tuvaluan elections from suburban Brisbane. It does not specify how a parliament functions when its constituency is dispersed across Sunshine Coast, Auckland, and Funafuti. It does not address what happens to Tuvaluan language, kinship networks, land tenure records, and navigational knowledge when the physical places those practices were anchored in are empty. The humanist urgency — survival now — crowded out the harder and slower question of what form survival takes.

The Engineer reads the treaty as mechanism design with a sequencing flaw. The mobility provisions are administratively tractable; the Digital Ark and Future Now initiatives (digital twins of all 124 islands, cloud-stored cultural records, an UNCLOS campaign to preserve EEZ rights without physical territory) demonstrate Tuvaluan technical ambition. What is not architecturally resolved is diaspora governance — the machinery by which a territorially dispersed Tuvaluan community exercises political self-determination. Estonia built digital governance infrastructure for an intact state with an intact territory and a population of 1.3 million, and it took a decade. The Basque diaspora maintained cultural institutions across borders without ever holding a seat in the UN. Neither model maps cleanly onto a legally recognized sovereign state governing a population primarily living on another state's soil. The architecture problem is real and tractable. It was simply not designed before migration began.

Where is X in this case? It is the implementation window: 2025–2026, when the first visa cohort is being selected and the governance architecture of diaspora statehood is still malleable. Governance infrastructure for a dispersed people — digital electoral rolls, remote voting platforms, cultural registries, constitutional frameworks for diaspora participation — takes years to build and test and constitutionalize. Once ten thousand Tuvaluans are established in Australian schools and hospitals and labor markets, the question of whether they participate in Tuvaluan governance is no longer a design question. It is a fait accompli shaped by inertia rather than intention. The window is the design phase. It is open now.

The 1+1 problem runs through the treaty's central word. Australia meant "statehood" as formal international recognition — Tuvalu retains its UN seat, its EEZ claims, its legal personality in bilateral agreements. Sopoaga meant "statehood" as genuine foreign policy independence — the capacity to make security arrangements without requiring another state's consent. The Falepili Union satisfies the first definition while qualifying the second. Both sides can describe the treaty as sovereignty-preserving. They are preserving different objects.

The 18th camel is a Tuvaluan Digital Commons: a legally recognized governance platform, funded through the FAIP's development commitment, that enables Tuvaluan nationals in Australia and globally to vote in Tuvaluan elections, access consular services, register cultural and genealogical records, and participate in community governance. This is not digital administration for efficiency. It is national infrastructure for a distributed people. The platform needs constitutional standing in Tuvaluan law before the diaspora exceeds the voting threshold at which its exclusion becomes politically irreversible. The precedent draws on Estonian digital governance and Basque diaspora institutions without mapping onto either. It is a new instrument for a new situation.

The decision pathway here is Implement: design and constitutionalize the governance architecture now, using the treaty's development commitment as the financing vehicle, before the dispersal makes design path-dependent. PM Teo's government has already demonstrated the relevant capacity — it ratified the FAIP and secured an interpretive memorandum on Article 4.4 from Australia without reopening the treaty. That is exactly the institutional competence the next move requires. The specific moves: a Tuvaluan Digital Governance Bill introduced within twelve months, establishing the legal basis for diaspora participation; a proposal to the Pacific Islands Forum for a multilateral climate mobility framework that converts bilateral FAIP-style agreements into a regional standard and removes the bilateral security-concession structure; and an immediate pilot opening the Tuvaluan Cultural Registry to FAIP diaspora members, with a target of 500 registrations in the first 90 days.

The stress test reveals the limits. Australia has limited incentive to support governance infrastructure that increases Tuvaluan institutional independence from Australian civic life — the FAIP's geopolitical value is partly the security alignment it purchases, and a strong diaspora governance model reduces assimilation pressure. The Pacific Islands Forum has seventeen members with different major-power relationships; multilateral reform of the bilateral FAIP template faces genuine collective action barriers. And there is a harder uncertainty: some Tuvaluans in Australia will prefer Australian civic identity over Tuvaluan governance participation, particularly second-generation diaspora members who grew up on the Gold Coast. Governance infrastructure serves those who want it. It cannot compel the national identification it is designed to support.

*(International law has no framework for a state whose physical territory is made uninhabitable through the emissions of other nations. Australia's bilateral recognition of Tuvaluan statehood is meaningful and insufficient: recognition by one state does not create universal recognition, and the Vienna Convention and UN Charter both assume territorial sovereignty as the foundation for legal personality. A diaspora Tuvaluan government elected digitally, governing from distance, holding an EEZ over waters above submerged atolls — this is unprecedented. Principled pragmatism can design the governance architecture and the international campaign for multilateral recognition. It cannot resolve the foundational question of whether a stateless people whose territory has been destroyed by others' choices retains the same standing in international law as a territorially grounded sovereign. Thirteen other Small Island Developing States are watching. The precedent matters more than Tuvalu.)*

**Problem** — Given that the Falepili Union is in force and migration has begun, how does Tuvalu design the governance, legal, and cultural infrastructure that allows a territorially dispersed community to exercise meaningful self-determination — before diaspora assimilation and institutional inertia make redesign impractical

**Stakeholders** — Tuvaluan nationals in Funafuti (current residents; full formal participation; declining population); FAIP diaspora (in Australia; legal pathway holders; no current formal mechanism for Tuvaluan civic participation); PM Teo's government (ratified treaty; demonstrated capacity to negotiate within existing framework); Australia (development financing; geopolitical interest in security alignment; limited incentive to support diaspora governance autonomy); Pacific Islands Forum (13 other SIDS watching; potential vehicle for multilateral reform); former PM Sopoaga and sovereignty critics (legitimate domestic counterweight)

**Binding constraint** — Article 4.4 of the FAIP constrains Tuvalu's foreign policy independence and must be worked within; international law has no established framework for diaspora statehood, making every governance design choice simultaneously a policy decision and a legal precedent

**Decision pathway** — Implement: design and constitutionalize the Tuvaluan Digital Commons governance architecture now, using the FAIP's development commitment as the financing vehicle, before the diaspora exceeds the threshold at which its exclusion from Tuvaluan governance becomes politically irreversible

**Tools** — Tuvaluan Digital Governance Bill (establishes constitutional basis for diaspora participation in elections, consular services, and cultural registry — changes the political incentive structure so diaspora members have voice in Tuvaluan governance); Pacific Islands Forum multilateral climate mobility proposal (converts bilateral FAIP precedent into regional standard, removing the security-concession quid pro quo for future climate-vulnerable states — changes coordination architecture); Tuvaluan Cultural and Genealogical Registry pilot (enables diaspora members to access and contribute to national records from anywhere — changes what "being Tuvaluan" means when separated from the physical territory)

**Metrics** — Percentage of FAIP diaspora members registered on the Tuvaluan electoral roll (measured via FAIP visa holder registry cross-referenced with electoral commission enrollment); frequency of Tuvaluan parliamentary sessions with digital participation quorum (measured via parliamentary records); number of other SIDS bilateral climate-mobility treaties that include security concessions (measured via treaty registry audit, annually — tracks whether the FAIP template is being adopted or reformed)

**90-day commitment** — Live recommendation: By October 2026, the Tuvalu government (1) introduces a Tuvaluan Digital Governance Bill establishing the legal basis for diaspora participation in Tuvaluan civic and political life; (2) proposes to the Pacific Islands Forum Secretariat a multilateral climate mobility framework that converts FAIP-style bilateral agreements into a Forum-wide standard; (3) opens the Tuvaluan Cultural Registry to FAIP diaspora members as a pilot participation mechanism, with a target of 500 registrations within 90 days
