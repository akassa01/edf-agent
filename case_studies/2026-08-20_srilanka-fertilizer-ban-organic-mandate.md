# The Overnight Transition: Sri Lanka's Fertilizer Ban and the Idealism That Ate the Harvest

On April 22, 2021, in a televised address, President Gotabaya Rajapaksa announced that Sri Lanka would ban the import of chemical fertilizers, herbicides, and pesticides — with immediate effect. Five days later, the Cabinet formalized it. On May 6, it became law. Two weeks between announcement and legal enforcement, with the maha rice crop already sown into fields conditioned by chemical inputs, and with two million farmers who had not been consulted about the change. Rajapaksa framed the decision as a public-health rescue: the North Central Province had for years suffered an epidemic of chronic kidney disease of unknown etiology, and some Sri Lankan researchers had linked it to arsenic contamination in imported phosphate fertilizers. The country, he said, would become the world's first fully organic farming nation.

The decision was politically legible in a way that a phased transition never would have been. It fulfilled a campaign promise. It relieved foreign-exchange pressure at a moment when Sri Lanka's reserves were collapsing under COVID's decimation of tourism. It offered a nationalist and ecological victory in a single sentence. It required no coordination, no pilots, no data. What it lacked was any operational apparatus to actually replace 700,000 tonnes per year of chemical fertilizer with the domestic organic supply chain that did not yet exist.

By August, the yala planting season was in trouble. By November, the ban was reversed. By early 2022 Sri Lanka, which had been rice-self-sufficient since the 1970s, was importing $450 million worth of rice from India and paying about fifty percent more for the staple of its national diet. The country's tea exports — the largest single foreign-exchange earner, and the ban's most immediate carve-out in July — recovered slowly and incompletely. By July 2022, amid mass protests over food and fuel shortages, Rajapaksa fled the country and later resigned. In April 2021 the ban had been a nationalist triumph. Fifteen months later, it had become one of the visible failures around which a government collapsed.

The Scientist's reading notices that the epidemiological premise was contested from the start. The CKDu-fertilizer causal link cited by proponents rested on studies (Jayasumana and colleagues, 2015) showing arsenic contamination in triple superphosphate, at concentrations up to 31 mg/kg. This was a real finding. It did not, in the WHO's 2013 assessment or in subsequent cross-country comparisons, translate into a monocausal explanation for CKDu. Countries with far higher chemical fertilizer application per hectare — India at 175 kg/ha, the United Kingdom at 245, China at 393, against Sri Lanka's approximately 138 — did not report the same kidney disease epidemic. If chemical fertilizers were the mechanism, the epidemiology should have followed the intensity gradient. It did not. This did not disprove the link, but it should have prevented a policy pretending certainty. The Scientist also notices that no published agronomic model supported a six-month organic transition on Sri Lankan soils. The domestic supply of certified organic amendments was an order of magnitude below the substitution requirement. When the government tried to bridge the gap by importing organic fertilizer from China in the autumn of 2021, the shipment was rejected as microbially contaminated. The empirical objections were on record before the ban. They were not answered; they were bypassed.

The Humanist's reading notices whose voices were invoked and whose were ignored. The CKDu-affected communities of the dry zone had real grievance, and their kidney disease was a legitimate public-health concern. The moral vocabulary of the ban — healing Sri Lanka's soils, protecting farmers' kidneys, restoring traditional practice — placed those communities at the emotional center of the policy. But no CKDu-affected farming community was consulted about the transition's timing, and the two million farmers whose livelihoods would collapse in the first season had not been at any table. When food inflation ran to fifty percent, it was the rural landless and the urban poor who paid it, and rural farming households who lost income while the government waited to see whether the yields would recover. A rice grower in eastern Sri Lanka, interviewed by Reuters months later, said what had been said to no one on his behalf: "The ban came without consultation or time for us to prepare. Farmers couldn't get a proper harvest using only organic inputs, and many became desperate." A humanist justification, in short, does not become humanist policy by invocation; it becomes humanist policy by consultation.

The Engineer's reading notices what was absent from the implementation architecture. No cross-ministry planning document exists for the transition. The Ministry of Agriculture had no extension service capacity to convert two million farms; no soil-testing infrastructure to certify readiness; no compensation mechanism for the yield gap; no phased regional pilot to identify failure modes before scaling. The 2014 Sirisena manifesto had envisioned a ten-year gradual transition with parallel organic subsidies — a policy on record, at cabinet level, that the 2021 decision effectively erased in a fortnight. The engineering diplomacy question of who can act, in what sequence, with what coordination, was not asked in any documented planning process. The transition was declared before the system that would execute it existed.

Where is X? Not the campaign promise. Not the underlying decision, which was politically reversible until the moment it was signed. The intervention point is the two-week window between the April 22 announcement and the May 6 effective date. Fourteen days in which the government could have converted a ban into a five-year phased transition with pilot districts, subsidized organic supply build-out, and a defined yield-gap compensation mechanism. All the downstream damage — the yala collapse, the emergency rice imports, the food inflation, the erosion of legitimacy that fed into the 2022 protests — sits on top of those two weeks. That is the fulcrum.

The question 1+1 produced: the government was answering "how do we deliver a campaign promise while conserving foreign exchange?" Farmers were answering "how do I grow this season's rice?" Agronomists were answering "over what timescale can this transition even work?" The word "agriculture" was in all three. When 1+1 was computed, the government got a policy triumph, the farmers got no fertilizer for a sown crop, and the agronomists received no reply. The same policy meant a triumph, a catastrophe, and a category error simultaneously.

The 18th camel was on the table before the ban was signed, and it is a specific policy, not a vague call for gradualism. A five-year phased transition beginning with three pilot districts — one wet-zone, one intermediate, one dry — each with parallel chemical and organic plots, farmer training programs, guaranteed minimum-yield price supports funded by a small fraction of the foreign-exchange savings, and public monthly reporting of results. Paired with immediate independent research funding for CKDu causation and immediate investment in domestic organic fertilizer capacity. This is not "delay the ban" and it is not "implement the ban." It is a structurally different program that fulfills the moral commitment (the transition has begun, publicly and visibly), provides the political theater (a signature program with a defined name and reporting cadence), addresses the public-health concern with actual evidence-building rather than assertion, and preserves rice production while the transition is proven. No one proposed it publicly with enough political weight because it would have surrendered the legibility of the sudden announcement — a phased plan reads, in nationalist media, as capitulation to foreign fertilizer companies. The 18th camel was defeated not on merit but on acoustics.

The Solomon Paradox applies at institutional scale. Sri Lankan agronomists asked to advise a neighboring country contemplating the same overnight ban would almost certainly have counseled the phased pilot approach. Applied to their own government's decision, several of those same agronomists remained publicly cautious in April and May 2021 — partly out of political prudence, partly because the moral framing of the policy narrowed the space for technical objection. The pragmatism they would have offered a foreign counterpart was not directed inward until the harvest had already failed, at which point the objection carried the sound of second-guessing rather than the weight of prevention.

The pathway available in April 2021 was Redesign. Convert the announcement into a phased pilot program. Fund domestic supply chain and independent CKDu research. Guarantee farm income floors in transitioning districts. Preserve the political achievement (the transition is real and visible) while removing the collateral catastrophe. All executable with the technical and institutional capacity Sri Lanka already had. None executed because the political system read the phased plan as retreat and the sudden ban as strength.

(The flag: when an ideologically committed leader invokes a genuine public-health concern to justify a policy that also serves an urgent budget imperative, technical objections are heard through three filters at once — insufficient patriotism, insufficient environmental commitment, and insufficient loyalty to a suffering constituency. Principled pragmatism can specify the phased pathway that would achieve the same eventual goal without the collateral catastrophe. It is silent on how to make that specification audible in a political system that reads decisive announcements as strength and phased plans as delay. The framework identifies the 18th camel and says nothing about how to change the acoustics of the room in which it is proposed.)

**Problem** — When a government facing a foreign-exchange crisis wants to reduce a costly agricultural import and can frame the reduction as public-health protection, how should it distinguish between a policy that pursues a defensible long-term transition and a policy that uses a defensible long-term goal as cover for a short-term budget move — when the second policy will predictably immiserate the population whose health the first was invoked to protect?

**Stakeholders** — President Gotabaya Rajapaksa and the Presidential Secretariat (custody of the campaign promise and the foreign-exchange problem); the Ministry of Agriculture (with technical capacity that was not engaged); two million small-holder farmers (bearing the transition's immediate cost, unrepresented in the decision); CKDu-affected communities of the North Central Province (invoked as beneficiaries, not consulted as decision-makers); the Sri Lanka Tea Research Institute and Sri Lankan agronomists (with objections that were on record but politically muted); the rural landless and urban poor (paying fifty percent food inflation on rice); the IMF and India (later underwriting the rescue that a phased transition might have avoided requiring).

**Binding constraint** — The political imperative to demonstrate decisive fulfillment of the campaign promise, combined with the foreign-exchange crisis's demand for immediate import reduction, closed the political space for a phased alternative. Every day of visible transition read as strength; every day of phased planning would have read as retreat.

**Decision pathway** — Redesign: from "ban all chemical fertilizer imports effective immediately" (a policy that satisfies political legibility at the cost of agricultural collapse) to "launch a five-year phased transition with three pilot districts, minimum-yield price supports, domestic organic supply chain investment, and an independent CKDu research fund" (a policy that satisfies the moral commitment through evidence-building rather than assertion, and preserves the food system while the transition is proven).

**Tools** — Phased district-pilot transition (three representative zones with parallel chemical/organic plots and monthly public reporting); minimum-yield price support for transitioning farmers (state-underwritten insurance for the individual farmer's downside risk); domestic organic fertilizer capacity investment (build the supply system before the ban lands into it); independent CKDu research fund (separate the public-health claim from the ideological claim with defined reporting milestones).

**Metrics** — Rice yield per hectare in pilot districts (kg/ha per season, parallel chemical vs. organic plots, monthly public reporting); farmer income parity in pilot districts (mean farm-gate income relative to prior-year baseline); domestic organic fertilizer output (tonnes per year of certified amendment production by district); CKDu incidence in North Central Province (age-standardized incidence per 100,000 tracked over five years via national kidney registry).

**90-day commitment** — Within 90 days of April 22, 2021: the government publicly reframes the announcement as a five-year phased transition with reversibility criteria; the Ministry of Agriculture, in consultation with farmer federations, designs the pilot program with parallel plots and monthly reporting; a minimum-yield price support scheme is announced for pilot districts; an independent CKDu research fund is established with international partnership and defined milestones; the 2021 maha crop is planted using existing chemical inputs to preserve food security, with the transition beginning at the 2022 yala season in pilot districts only.
