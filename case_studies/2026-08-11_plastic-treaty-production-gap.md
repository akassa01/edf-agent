# The Production Clause That Wasn't

Busan, South Korea. December 1, 2024. Luis Vayas Valdivieso, the Ecuadorian diplomat who has chaired five rounds of negotiations toward the world's first legally binding instrument on plastic pollution, faces a room that will not agree. The "Like-Minded Group" — led by Saudi Arabia, Russia, Iran, and Kuwait, with China and India in loose alignment — has spent two weeks refusing any treaty language that obliges member states to reduce primary plastic production. More than 100 countries support some form of production cap; the blocking bloc is smaller but uses the session's consensus decision rule with precision. Vayas Valdivieso can call a vote. He decides not to. He adjourns.

This moment — a chair choosing process integrity over outcome adequacy, at the point of maximum leverage — is what the plastic pollution crisis looks like through the Engineering Diplomacy frame. Not a villain and a victim. A technically correct position (production must be reduced) that has not yet been translated into a politically executable pathway, meeting a blocking coalition that knows the rules better than the science.

The scientific case is not in dispute. UNEP's flagship 2023 report, "Turning Off the Tap," modeled the options: even if every proposed recycling and waste management improvement were fully implemented, 100 million metric tonnes of plastic would still enter the environment annually by 2040. Achieving plastic neutrality — getting the flow to match the cleanup capacity — requires a 40% reduction in primary production alongside ambitious recycling. Global production is currently 430 million tonnes per year. Only 9% of all plastic ever produced has been recycled. Waste management alone is, by the science, insufficient. The Scientist looks at this situation and asks: what else is there to discuss?

A great deal, as it turns out. The Humanist sees what the Scientist's clean model misses. Plastic production is not evenly distributed in its costs. The communities absorbing the waste — fisher families in Southeast Asia, coastal neighborhoods in West Africa, informal waste workers across the Global South — did not design the production system and bear its harms without meaningful representation in Geneva or Busan. Meanwhile, the blocking states frame production caps as a sovereignty question: the right of a developing petrochemical economy to build its own industrial base, without a treaty designed by wealthier nations who industrialized freely and now ask others not to follow. Both framings have internal coherence. Neither has found a pathway to the other's interests, because neither has been seriously looking for one.

The Engineer asks what is actually buildable given the rules of the room. The INC process operates on consensus — not a formal voting rule, but a diplomatic norm strong enough that the Chair would not break it even with a majority in hand. The petrostates' blocking power is not scientific; it is procedural. The Engineer sees that the High Ambition Coalition — 71 countries co-chaired by Norway and Rwanda, including the EU and most of the small island states — arrived in Busan with a scientifically correct position and no concrete transition pathway for the fence-sitting developing nations who care about plastic pollution but cannot afford to alienate their petrochemical trade partners. The HAC was answering the question "what does the science require?" The developing nations were answering "what happens to our industries if we sign?" Nobody had tabled an answer to the second question. So the treaty stalled.

1+1 produced three separate negotiations. The High Ambition Coalition was answering: what does the evidence require? The Like-Minded Group was answering: what protects our sovereign industrial development? The middle-group developing nations were answering: what do we get if we support production caps? Three separate questions. The INC Chair's Text from Busan preserved production language as an option, but options without coalition math are editorial.

Where is X? Two moments, in sequence. The first is the session in which the HAC decided not to table a concrete transition finance package alongside its production cap proposal — separating the scientific case from the implementation architecture. That decision was made quietly, in consultations before Busan, and it determined the coalition math. A $5 billion transition facility, with automatic eligibility tied to a country's submitted production reduction plan, would have changed what developing nations had to gain by joining the HAC. The second is the closing plenary on December 1, when the Chair chose not to call a vote he had the procedural authority to call. Both are specific, locatable, and reversible in the next session.

The binary framing has been: (A) binding global production caps, opposed by the petrostate bloc; or (B) a waste-management treaty with no production obligations, opposed by the HAC and scientifically inadequate. The 18th camel is a nationally determined production trajectory mechanism — modeled on the Paris Agreement's NDC structure. Each country submits a national plan specifying its plastic production levels and a reduction pathway, tied to sustainable consumption benchmarks. Plans are subject to peer review by a UNEP-convened technical body. There is no agreed global cap — only agreed transparency, review, and ratcheting. Petrostates can participate without conceding a global number. Small island states and HAC members get binding accountability. Developing nations without petrochemical sectors get a structure that links plan ambition to eligibility for concessional transition capital. The NDC mechanism was not popular in Paris in 2015 either. It produced a framework that has since set the architecture for every major climate commitment.

A principled pragmatist at the next session: table the national trajectory mechanism as a draft article with three defined options for the scope of "production" — resin-level, pellet-level, product-level — each with its distributional implications made explicit. Simultaneously, have HAC core members announce the transition finance facility with a concrete capitalization figure and eligibility criteria. This sequence changes the question from "will you accept a global cap?" to "will you submit a national plan and qualify for financing?" It does not guarantee success. It addresses the second question in the room that nobody answered in Busan.

*(The flag: the treaty text will eventually contain a definition of "plastic production" — and that definition is the treaty's most consequential and least-discussed design decision. Accountability attaches to whoever is defined as the producer: crude oil exporters, resin manufacturers, polymer pellet producers, or consumer-product companies. Each definition allocates the economic burden of compliance to a different set of countries and industries. Principled pragmatism can map the options and their distributional consequences. It cannot resolve which distribution is just — because that is the policy question, and scientific analysis alone cannot provide it. The definitional choice will be made by negotiators under time pressure. The right time to design that choice carefully was before the next session convened. That window is now.)*

**Problem** — How should the INC process secure binding plastic production reduction obligations when a blocking coalition of petrostates uses the consensus rule to prevent them, and when the High Ambition Coalition's science-first framing has so far failed to build the transition pathway that would bring developing nations firmly to its side?

**Stakeholders** — High Ambition Coalition states (scientific adequacy, environmental credibility); Like-Minded Group petrostates (sovereign industrial development, treaty non-binding on production); middle-group developing nations (environmental commitment plus industrial transition risk); coastal and fishing communities globally (the stakeholders most harmed by plastic pollution, not directly represented in negotiations); the INC Chair (process legitimacy, treaty adoption before mandate exhaustion).

**Binding constraint** — The INC's consensus decision norm gives the petrostates' blocking coalition veto power over any specific production obligation, and the HAC has not yet designed the institutional package (transition finance, nationally determined mechanism) that would move the middle-group developing nations to its side.

**Decision pathway** — Redesign: replace the "agree on a global cap" frame with a "nationally determined production trajectory" mechanism that decouples the accountability structure from the global-cap political impasse.

**Tools** — National Plastic Production Trajectory mechanism (changes interaction: replaces binary cap/no-cap negotiation with country-by-country transparency and ratcheting; modeled on Paris NDC structure); Transition Finance Eligibility Framework (changes incentives: links NPPT ambition level to concessional capital eligibility, making it costly for developing nations to remain outside the system); without-prejudice definitional protocol (changes learning: surfaces the distributional consequences of each "production" definition explicitly, before a political choice is made implicitly).

**Metrics** — NPPT coverage: percentage of global plastic production covered by submitted national trajectory plans, tracked via UNEP registry; transition finance activation: USD disbursed under the framework within 24 months of treaty adoption; definitional clarity: whether an agreed definition of "plastic production" for treaty purposes is adopted at the next session.

**90-day commitment** — Live recommendation: the INC Secretariat convenes an intersessional technical working group to produce a draft NPPT mechanism design with three structural options; HAC core members (Norway, Rwanda, EU) announce a concrete transition finance facility ($5 billion over five years, OECD-funded) with published eligibility criteria linked to NPPT ambition; both outputs are tabled for the next INC session before its agenda is finalized. Both moves can be made without consensus. Only the political will to make them is required.
