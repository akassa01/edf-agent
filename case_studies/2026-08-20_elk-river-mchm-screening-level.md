# The Number That Ended the Do-Not-Use: CDC's 1 ppm and the Speech-Act of a Safe Level

On the evening of January 9, 2014, Dr. Vikas Kapil, chief medical officer at the CDC's National Center for Environmental Health, took a call from West Virginia. The state Bureau for Public Health had a request that could not wait. About 10,000 gallons of crude 4-methylcyclohexanemethanol — a coal-processing chemical almost no one outside the industry could name — had leaked from a corroded 76-year-old storage tank at Freedom Industries in Charleston, into the Elk River, a mile and a half upstream of the region's primary drinking water intake. West Virginia American Water had issued a do-not-use order to more than 300,000 customers across nine counties. Bathe with it, no. Cook with it, no. Drink it, no. Boiling won't help. It was OK for flushing toilets and fighting fires. That was the entire list.

Kapil needed to give the state a number. Below what concentration of MCHM in tap water could residents safely resume normal use? He had until roughly the following night to say.

What he had to work with was thin. The chemical had received almost no toxicological study. The manufacturer, Eastman Chemical, had a Material Safety Data Sheet built from a rat oral lethality study. There was a proprietary 28-day repeated-dose rat study — pure MCHM, not the crude mixture actually spilled — that identified a No Observable Adverse Effects Level (NOAEL) of about 100 milligrams per kilogram of body weight per day. Kapil's team divided that by roughly a thousand-fold uncertainty factor (ten for interspecies extrapolation, ten for individual variability, ten for the study's limitations), yielding a reference dose near 0.1 mg per kilogram per day. They modeled a ten-kilogram child drinking a liter of water per day. The arithmetic came out clean: one milligram per liter. One part per million. On January 13, the West Virginia Bureau for Public Health adopted 1 ppm as the screening level. The utility began flushing zones in sequence. By January 17, eight days after the spill, the do-not-use order was lifted.

The math is defensible. Every step is what a competent toxicologist would do with the data available in the hours available. Later work supported it: the National Toxicology Program's studies, running through 2016, concluded that exposure at or below the 1 ppm level was not likely to be associated with known adverse health effects. On the narrow question the CDC was asked — will people be acutely poisoned above this concentration — the answer was probably correct.

The problem was not the number.

The problem was what happened to the community after the order lifted. MCHM has a distinctive licorice-anise odor, detectable at roughly two micrograms per liter — about five hundred times below the CDC's screening level. Residents whose water was now officially "safe" continued to smell it coming out of their taps, particularly in hot showers, particularly from bathtubs and washing machines where the chemical had absorbed into pipe biofilm and off-gassed on rewarming. West Virginia Poison Control logged hundreds of exposure calls in the weeks after the lift. Rashes appeared on children who had just bathed. Households in the poorer counties, unable to sustain the cost of bottled water for months, faced a choice between the odor at their faucet and the reassurance from the press conference. An independent screening protocol led by Andrew Whelton of Virginia Tech — later Purdue — reanalyzed the same NOAEL with more conservative exposure assumptions and arrived at 120 micrograms per liter, eight times more stringent than the CDC number. Both were peer-defensible. Only one had been declared during the emergency.

The Scientist's reading notices that the toxicological derivation was one exercise and the community's exposure surface was another, and that the two were not connected by any instrument the response system contained. In-home tap concentrations were not being measured. Pipe biofilm was not being characterized. The odor detection threshold sat orders of magnitude below the "safe" number without any advisory framework acknowledging that gap. The number was correct for the exposure model it assumed. The exposure model did not resemble what people were actually experiencing.

The Humanist's reading notices the durable damage. Trust in advisory systems is expensive to build and cheap to break. When a community's own senses contradict a government reassurance during an ongoing exposure event, the reassurance loses future authority in exchange for present operational convenience. The 300,000 people who lived through the eight days and the weeks after did not lose faith in one advisory; they became skeptical of the next one. This cost is real and it is paid in a future emergency by an agency that may not know what it inherited.

The Engineer's reading notices what the response system did not have. No infrastructure for in-home water sampling at scale. No tiered advisory template distinguishing drinking, bathing, and cleaning as different exposure routes with different toxicological profiles. No cleanup guidance for pipe biofilm, water heaters, or appliance filters. No protocol for treating community odor detection as a formal input to advisory updates. The Chemical Safety Board's 2017 report identified the physical causes — tank corrosion, no secondary containment. The harder finding beneath it is that the regulatory apparatus was built around one threshold, not around the several things a household actually does with water.

Where is X? Not the tank. Not the NOAEL calculation. The intervention point is the framing of the number on January 13, 2014 — the choice between "safe level" as a declarative sentence and "screening threshold below which we know little about long-term effects" as a conditional one. That single framing carries the whole downstream story: whether the odor complaints get treated as data or as nuisance, whether residents' trust in the next advisory survives, whether the operational apparatus that should have been built during the crisis is built at all. Everything sits on top of that sentence.

The question 1+1 produced: CDC was answering "at what concentration are known acute effects unlikely?" Residents were answering "is it safe for my child to bathe?" Both used the word safe. To the agency the water was under threshold; to the household it still smelled wrong. The parties never agreed on which question they were jointly answering.

The 18th camel: not the binary of "declare a safe number and reopen use" versus "keep the do-not-use order until definitive long-term data arrive." A tiered, use-specific advisory — safe for flushing at any detectable level; safe for showering when odor is not detected at the tap; safe for drinking only below 120 μg/L, the more conservative independent threshold — paired with a public commitment that resident odor detection is a legitimate reason to defer use, paired with rapid deployment of in-home sampling. This is not a compromise between the two stated options. It separates the exposure routes the body actually encounters, gives residents a formal role in the assessment, and treats odor as evidence rather than as a complaint to be discounted. Executable in January 2014 with a phone tree, a sampling kit, and a two-page advisory template. No one deployed it because the response apparatus was built for a single threshold, and building anything else in eight days was operationally impossible.

The pathway available at the decisive moment was Reframe. Not "compute a better number" — the number was already the number — but "replace declarative language with conditional language, replace single-threshold guidance with route-specific guidance, replace plant-outflow monitoring with in-home sampling, replace odor as noise with odor as trigger." All executable with the science already in hand. None executed because the pressure to give the state a single sentence overwhelmed everything else.

(The flag: a screening threshold is a scientific object and a public speech act at the same time. Toxicology gives the number, but the announcement is a promise, and the promise persists in the community's memory long after the confidence interval has ceased to be a live consideration. Principled pragmatism can specify the tiered structure and the conditional language that would keep the two separate; it does not tell you how to prevent the operational pressure of an ongoing crisis from collapsing that structure back into a declaratory sentence. Every emergency reference dose is, in this sense, a political artifact wearing scientific clothes — and there is no framework for keeping the two costumes distinct when a governor's chief of staff wants to know when the restaurants can reopen.)

---

**Problem** — When toxicological data are sparse, the exposure event is ongoing, and the community's lived experience contradicts the calculated safety threshold, how should a public health agency communicate a screening level so that it does not become a speech act that overwrites the community's own evidence of continuing exposure?

**Stakeholders** — CDC and ATSDR toxicologists (custody of the number and of the agency's future advisory credibility); West Virginia American Water (custody of a shut-down system whose downtime cost accrues daily); the West Virginia Bureau for Public Health (accountable for the state's public health response and for future advisories); 300,000 residents across nine counties (bearing the exposure and paying, in trust, for the framing of the number); Freedom Industries (bankrupt within eight days, exiting the accountability structure); the poorer households of Kanawha, Boone, and Clay counties (least able to sustain bottled-water alternatives, most exposed to the choice between advisory compliance and household function).

**Binding constraint** — The operational imperative to end the do-not-use order collided with the absence of in-home testing infrastructure, tiered advisory templates, and biofilm cleanup guidance. Every day of shutdown had visible cost; the cost of a premature "safe" was invisible for years.

**Decision pathway** — Reframe: from "compute a single safe threshold to reopen the system" (a strategy the operational pressure demanded) to "issue a tiered, route-specific advisory in conditional language, deploy in-home sampling, and treat odor as data" (a strategy that preserves scientific honesty and future advisory credibility at the cost of decisive-looking action in the moment).

**Tools** — Tiered use-specific advisory template (route-specific thresholds for drinking, bathing, cleaning, replacing single-threshold guidance); in-home sampling deployment protocol (bringing household-level exposure data into the evidence base within 48 hours); odor-detection escalation clause (converting resident reports of the characteristic odor into automatic resample triggers); conditional communication protocol (replacing declarative "safe" with structured language about what is and is not known at each level).

**Metrics** — In-home MCHM concentration distribution across 200 sampled homes, updated weekly; advisory trust index at 30, 90, and 365 days post-lift (would residents comply with a hypothetical next order); odor-triggered resampling frequency and time-to-resample; symptom-report incidence through Poison Control and emergency departments, pre- and post-lift.

**90-day commitment** — Within 90 days of January 9, 2014: CDC and WVBPH jointly publish a tiered use-specific advisory replacing the single "below 1 ppm is safe" framing; an in-home sampling program deploys door-to-door across the nine counties with results on a public dashboard; a standing community liaison panel including North Charleston and Boone County residents is convened before advisory changes rather than after; the independent WVTAP-style reanalysis is commissioned from the outset as a mandatory second opinion rather than emerging months later as an academic paper.
