# The Airlift as Alibi: Biafra, Humanitarian Neutrality, and the Unasked Question

Geneva, July 1968. Auguste Lindt, a former UN High Commissioner for Refugees now serving as the ICRC's special commissioner for Nigeria, was briefed on a choice that had no good options. The Nigerian federal government (FMG) would not authorize daylight food flights into Biafra, citing sovereignty and the accusation—sometimes true—that night flights mixed relief cargo with weapons. The Joint Church Aid consortium of Catholic and Protestant organizations was already flying in from São Tomé island at night without authorization, delivering 200 to 300 tons of food and medicine per sortie to the Uli airstrip. The ICRC, whose mandate required consent from both parties, was caught: join the unauthorized flights and lose Lagos's cooperation, or keep negotiating and watch people die faster than the authorized channels could feed them.

Lindt chose negotiation. It was the right answer to the wrong question.

By late 1968, an estimated 6,000 to 8,000 people were dying each day in Biafra—from kwashiorkor, from conflict, from the intersection of blockade and broken agricultural infrastructure. The images taken by Don McCullin, Gilles Caron, and others—children with distended bellies and eyes clouded by protein deficiency—had catalyzed unprecedented public mobilization across Europe and North America. Churches held emergency collections. Governments were besieged with demands to act. For humanitarian organizations, the moral clarity was absolute: visible mass starvation demands visible mass response.

The feeling mode driving that response was not wrong. People were dying and they could be fed. But it was doing two things at once: driving action and foreclosing inquiry. The question that the imagery made impossible to ask—and that the funding model made financially lethal to answer—was whether more food delivery was the lever that would end mass starvation, or whether the lever was somewhere else entirely.

The Scientist looks at the numbers. At peak capacity, JCA was delivering 200 to 300 tons per night. WHO caloric need estimates for the Biafran population placed adequate supply at two to four times that volume. The airlift was never going to be sufficient. What was producing the starvation—and what would end it—was the blockade, not the delivery shortfall. The war ended on January 15, 1970, three years after it began. The famine ended within weeks. This confirmed what the numbers had already implied: the famine was sustained primarily by the blockade, not by agricultural collapse alone. An operation designed to feed people through a political instrument, rather than to change the instrument, was always going to be fighting the wrong variable.

The Humanist, looking at the same situation from inside it, sees what made the political analysis impossible to voice. JCA's donors were mobilized by photographs of dying children. The organizational narrative—we are flying food to starving people—was what made the next donation arrive. An organization that publicly concluded "our airlift is delivering 25 to 50 percent of what is needed, and the blockade cannot be airlift-defeated" would have destroyed its donor base and grounded its aircraft. The feeling that drove the response was real; it was also, structurally, a constraint on the thinking that the response required.

The Engineer asks the doing question: what was actually controllable? The airlift logistics were improvised from nothing into an extraordinary operation. But the constraint binding mass mortality was not logistical. The FMG's blockade was a deliberate political instrument—General Gowon's government had calculated that the political cost of the blockade, internationally, was acceptable. Ojukwu's Biafran leadership had calculated the opposite: that prolonged international humanitarian attention extended the independence project. Both calculations were correct. Neither the ICRC nor JCA factored them into their operational logic.

Where is X? It is not the volume of food flown into Uli. It is the moment when JCA committed to flying from São Tomé—to the Biafran side only, without engaging the FMG's terms—in late 1968. That commitment was logistically rational; São Tomé was geographically accessible and the FMG was refusing daylight corridors anyway. But it was also the moment at which JCA foreclosed a different intervention: one directed at the mechanism producing the blockade rather than at the consequence it was producing. X is the negotiating table that was never convened.

The 1+1 that produced three separate answers: aid organizations answered "how do we get food to starving people?" Ojukwu answered "how do we keep Biafra visible to the world?" Gowon answered "how do we end the war fastest?" The airlift was an answer to the first question that accidentally served the second question and delayed the resolution of the third. No one in the room was answering all three simultaneously.

The 18th camel was not a larger airlift or a different route. It was a WHO-convened, Sweden-brokered joint fact-finding commission on food access and blockade effects—with findings published publicly, available to all parties. Sweden had diplomatic access to both Lagos and Biafra; Olof Palme had a channel to Ojukwu. A commission whose published findings made the blockade politically expensive for the FMG—by disaggregating blockade-caused mortality from conflict-caused mortality and publishing the numbers under WHO letterhead—would have done more for caloric access than a doubled airlift. It would have changed the question being answered: not "how do we get food in?" but "what would end the condition that is making food impossible to deliver at scale?" It was proposed, in various forms, by Sweden and others. It never happened, because neither the ICRC nor JCA had the institutional mandate to support it, and the FMG had every incentive to resist it.

The counterfactual pathway—Reframe—required an institution with both the political analysis capacity and the financial independence to say: the problem is the blockade, and feeding is not a solution to a blockade. No institution in this crisis had both.

(The limitation the episode reveals that principled pragmatism does not resolve: humanitarian organizations that depend on donor attention generated by images of suffering cannot publicly conclude that their relief operation is counterproductive. The funding model that makes the airlift possible is the same funding model that makes the political analysis financially impossible. Principled pragmatism can describe a different pathway; it cannot redesign the institution that would need to walk it. The organization that would perform the analysis is the organization that would be destroyed by its conclusions.)

---

**Problem** — How international humanitarian organizations should respond when the mechanism producing mass starvation is a deliberate political instrument, and when the relief operation itself may be extending the conflict sustaining the famine.

**Stakeholders** — Biafran civilians (direct mortality); ICRC and Joint Church Aid (operational actors with donor-constrained mandates); Nigerian federal government (FMG, controlling the blockade); Biafran leadership under Ojukwu (using famine visibility strategically); Sweden and other neutral states (with potential mediation capacity); Britain and Soviet Union (great-power backers of FMG, with Security Council veto).

**Binding constraint** — The humanitarian funding model: ICRC and JCA raised money from donors mobilized by images of dying children; any public statement that the airlift was insufficient or counterproductive would collapse the donor base. Financial survival required the narrative that the operation was working.

**Decision pathway** — Reframe: instead of optimizing the airlift, convene a WHO-Sweden-brokered joint fact-finding commission on food access and blockade effects, with public reporting, that changes the question being answered from "how do we get food in?" to "what would end the conditions making mass feeding impossible?"

**Tools** — Joint fact-finding commission on blockade-caused vs. conflict-caused mortality (changes learning: produces a publicly verifiable causal account that makes the blockade politically expensive); structured bilateral negotiation on food corridor access brokered by a neutral state with ICRC as observer (changes coordination: aid organizations become background pressure rather than primary actors); public disaggregated mortality reporting under WHO letterhead (changes interaction: makes the FMG's political cost of maintaining the blockade visible internationally).

**Metrics** — Caloric delivery as percentage of estimated daily need in Biafra (target: ≥80%, requiring a political solution, not better logistics); time from commission findings to opening of a daylight food corridor; number of humanitarian deaths per day attributable to blockade vs. conflict (tracked by joint commission).

**90-day commitment** — Within 90 days of the famine's becoming internationally visible (August 1968): convene a WHO-Sweden-ICRC joint fact-finding mission with access to both sides and a public reporting mandate; request an emergency Security Council session addressing the blockade as a famine-causing instrument; publish preliminary findings showing blockade-caused mortality as a distinct category from conflict-caused mortality. (Counterfactual: the pathway that was available and was not taken.)
