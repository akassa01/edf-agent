# The Dam That Could Not Be Wrong

At 22:39 on October 9, 1963, approximately 270 million cubic meters of rock slid into the Vajont reservoir in northeastern Italy in about forty-five seconds. The wave rose 230 meters above the dam's crest and raced down the gorge toward the town of Longarone at roughly ninety kilometers per hour. Longarone had a population of approximately 1,300 people. Within four minutes, 1,269 of them were dead. The dam itself — 261 meters of thin-arch concrete, one of the tallest in the world, built by the electricity company SADE — survived intact. It had been designed to withstand everything. Except the question no one in a position of authority had been willing to formally answer: what if the design capacity was unachievable?

The answer was known. It had been in SADE's own files since 1960.

Edoardo Semenza was a trained geologist and the son of Carlo Semenza, the engineer who had designed the dam. In summer 1959, after early slope alarms during initial test filling, the younger Semenza found the morphological signature of an ancient prehistoric landslide mass on Monte Toc — the curved scar of displaced rock that had never fully re-settled. Working with colleague Franco Giudici, he estimated the unstable volume at approximately 200 million cubic meters, enough to send a wave over the dam. According to Semenza's own later account, the resulting 1960 report was not transmitted to Italian regulatory authorities by SADE. The finding was in the company's own files.

Austrian rock mechanics specialist Leopold Müller, commissioned around the same time, warned of large-scale failure potential and proposed a strategy of cyclic filling and drawdown to gradually test the slope. This was partially adopted through 1961 and 1962. The slope moved; displacement correlated with water level. The official external consultant, Giorgio Dal Piaz, had examined the valley in 1928 and 1958 and found "no concerns regarding the stability of either side." His reassuring assessments were the ones SADE cited in regulatory communications. The Giudici-Semenza finding was not.

By October 4, 1963, the slope was moving at approximately twenty centimeters per day. A partial drawdown — roughly ten meters — was initiated. Hendron and Patton's 1985 geotechnical analysis established that arresting the failure would have required a much more aggressive reduction. The slide was past the point where a managed drawdown was possible. What was being measured precisely was not what was being understood accurately.

The Scientist observes the gap between precision and accuracy. The monitoring data were precise — displacement readings accurate to millimeters, plotted regularly by SADE's own team. The conclusion drawn from them was not accurate: that ten meters of drawdown constituted a sufficient safety measure rather than an insufficient gesture. The data generated a question — "Is this slope moving toward catastrophic failure, or can it be managed?" — that was resolved in the interpretation most compatible with continued commercial operation. Precision and accuracy are not synonyms.

The Humanist looks at the valley below. The communities of Longarone, Pirago, Rivalta, Villanova, and Faè were not party to any of the decisions being made above them. They did not know the slope was moving. They did not know SADE's engineers were debating whether ten meters was enough. The village of Erto, higher on the reservoir margin, had experienced the 1960 test-fill landslide firsthand and had filed safety complaints with SADE. A journalist, Tina Merlin, had been publishing warnings in l'Unità since February 1961 that a landslide could send a wave over the dam. SADE's response was to charge her with "spreading false and misleading news to disturb public order." She was acquitted. She continued publishing. Mario Pancini, SADE's director of dam works, did not face trial for the disaster: on November 24, 1968 — the eve of the criminal proceedings' opening in L'Aquila — he died by suicide. He reportedly told an investigating judge before his death: "Guilty or not guilty, there are two thousand dead."

The Engineer reads the governance structure. In December 1962 — ten months before the disaster — SADE was nationalized into ENEL, Italy's new state electricity authority, with SADE personnel continuing to manage the dam. There was no independent dam safety board, no external regulator with operational power, no institutional actor whose sole mandate was the safety of the communities downstream. The safety judgment and the commercial judgment were being made by the same people with the same reputational stakes.

1+1 produced three questions that looked like one. SADE's engineers were answering: how do we manage the slope while maintaining commercial operations? The residents of Erto were asking: are we safe? The monitoring data were generating a third question nobody was formally authorized to ask: is this consistent with continued operation, or with imminent catastrophic failure? Only the first question had an institutional home.

Where is X? The early October 1963 decision to initiate a partial drawdown rather than an emergency full drawdown. That decision was made by the people with the most to lose from ordering a full drawdown — the engineers whose design capacity required near-maximum impoundment, inside an institution whose contracts required energy delivery. The threshold that mattered — twenty centimeters per day of slope movement — was visible in the data. The institution was not built to act on it.

The apparent binary was: fill to design capacity or admit the slope was permanently unstable and concede the project could not deliver what its builders had promised. The 18th camel: operate the reservoir permanently at a reduced level that Semenza's and Müller's analysis suggested the slope could tolerate — less power than designed, contractual renegotiation, reputational damage, Longarone alive. This option was technically available. It required the engineers who built the dam to say, in public, that its design capacity was not achievable. No institutional structure in 1963 was built to let anyone say that sentence.

*(The flag: sunk cost is not merely psychological; it is institutional, contractual, and reputational in a way that no individual engineer can dissolve by being more principled. SADE's engineers were not fools or villains — they were inside a structure in which admitting the design capacity was unachievable would simultaneously destroy their company's commercial case, their professional reputations, and the political argument for the dam already made to government regulators. Principled pragmatism correctly identifies the independent safety board and the pre-agreed displacement thresholds as the governance fix. But those institutions needed to exist before construction began, before the contracts that made the project financially viable were signed. The lesson arrives in a form that can only be applied to the next Vajont, not this one.)*

**Problem** — When SADE's internal monitoring showed accelerating slope movement through September and October 1963, and the finding of an ancient failure mass had been in SADE's own files since 1960, who structurally had the standing to order an emergency full drawdown over the operator's commercial and contractual objections — and what governance architecture would have created that actor?

**Stakeholders** — Residents of Longarone and four neighboring villages (1,917 dead; no knowledge of the risk; no standing in any decision); residents of Erto and Casso (experienced 1960 landslide; filed safety complaints; not informed of slope creep data); SADE/ENEL engineers (held the monitoring data; inside a structure with no mechanism for emergency action over commercial objections); Italian dam safety regulator (nominal authority; no independent operational power); ENEL's commercial contracts (mandated energy delivery that required near-full reservoir operation)

**Binding constraint** — The absence of an independent dam safety authority — separate from SADE/ENEL's commercial management — with legal standing to order emergency drawdown over the operator's objections; the safety judgment and the commercial judgment were made by the same people with the same reputational stakes, with no external actor empowered to break the conflict

**Decision pathway** — Redesign: not rebuild the dam (it performed as designed), not change the monitoring (it existed), but redesign the safety governance architecture — separate the safety monitoring function from commercial management, establish pre-agreed displacement thresholds for mandatory emergency action, and create an independent authority empowered to override the operator on life-safety grounds

**Tools** — Independent dam safety review board (changes interaction: creates an institutional actor whose mandate is entirely safety, empowered to order emergency drawdown without the operator's consent); pre-agreed emergency trigger thresholds (changes learning: converts a judgment call vulnerable to motivated reasoning into a rule triggered by the data itself at critical displacement rates); community risk disclosure protocol (changes coordination: gives downstream communities knowledge of current risk and standing to demand intervention)

**Metrics** — Time from monitoring threshold breach to emergency action (days, tracked against pre-agreed criteria and an independent review board's records); reservoir level vs. displacement rate correlation (continuous monitoring by an independent team, separate from the operator's engineers); community hazard notification completeness (percentage of downstream residents formally informed of current risk level, audited annually)

**90-day commitment** — Counterfactual: by January 1, 1960 — 90 days after the 1959 test-fill landslide alarm — SADE constitutes an Independent Dam Safety Panel with three external geologists and geotechnical engineers, no financial stake in the project, with contractual authority to order reservoir drawdown over SADE's commercial objections. Pre-agreed thresholds are established and made binding: if slope displacement exceeds 10 cm/day for three consecutive days, level reduces within 14 days; if it exceeds 20 cm/day, emergency full drawdown commences within 72 hours. Residents of Longarone, Erto, and Casso receive formal disclosure of the monitoring program and trigger criteria.
