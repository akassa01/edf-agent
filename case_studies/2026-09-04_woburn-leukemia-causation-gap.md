# The Woburn Causation Trap

In 1972, Anne Anderson sat at her kitchen table in east Woburn, Massachusetts, and began drawing. On a neighborhood map she had bought at a pharmacy, she marked each child she knew to have been diagnosed with leukemia — one here, two doors down, another across the street, too many clustered near the Aberjona River and the two large municipal wells that served the east side of town. She had no epidemiological training. She had a sick son named Jimmy and a pattern she could not talk herself out of. By the time Jimmy died in 1981, she had mapped twenty-one cases.

Her mapping was, by the standards of scientific evidence, anecdotal. It was also correct.

The east Woburn leukemia cluster is the most thoroughly documented case in American environmental health law of a community knowing something that its legal system could not verify. Anne Anderson's map predicted — with striking accuracy — what the Harvard School of Public Health would confirm a decade later: that exposure to drinking water from Wells G and H in east Woburn was statistically associated with childhood leukemia incidence at rates far above any plausible baseline. The contamination source was eventually traced to industrial solvents, principally trichloroethylene (TCE), leaching from properties operated by W.R. Grace and Beatrice Foods. EPA testing in 1979 confirmed toxic concentrations and the wells were closed. By then, the families had been drinking the water for at least a decade.

What followed was, simultaneously, a landmark environmental lawsuit and a systematic demonstration of why the legal system cannot process epidemiological truth.

The Scientist in this story is not a villain — or not only one. The Harvard biostatisticians Marvin Zelen and Steven Lagakos spent two years designing a community-health study that was epidemiologically sound by any peer review standard. Working with 250 volunteers who interviewed five thousand Woburn families about their health histories and water use patterns, they produced a 1986 study in the Journal of the American Statistical Association finding a statistically significant association between exposure to the contaminated wells and childhood leukemia. The science worked exactly as it was supposed to: it identified a population-level risk elevation that no individual-case analysis could produce. That was precisely its limitation in court.

The Humanist is the community itself, which had conducted its own survey, organized FACE (For A Cleaner Environment), mapped cases, and kept their own records long before any academic study existed. Their knowledge was both scientifically productive — the Lagakos study used it — and legally inert, because American tort law requires individual proof of causation, and epidemiology proves nothing about any individual case. The families were testifying to the very thing the science confirmed, in a language the court could not receive. The courtroom treated their lived knowledge as emotional testimony to be discounted and the Harvard study as a scientific finding to be challenged on methodological grounds. Both experiences of Woburn were real; only one was legally actionable.

The Engineer's question — which institutional mechanism could actually deliver cleanup and accountability? — had a cleaner answer than anyone pursued. Under CERCLA (the Superfund Act, passed in 1980), the EPA had authority to compel industrial remediation at contaminated sites without requiring proof of individual causation. The EPA's toxicological trigger standard — association between contamination and health outcomes — is not the same as the legal causation standard. This distinction mattered: the Industri-Plex site in Woburn was eventually designated a Superfund site and remediated under EPA authority. The cleanup happened. The tort compensation, effectively, did not.

Where is X in this case is unusually precise: it is the structure of the trial itself. Judge Walter Skinner bifurcated the case into Phase 1 (contamination — could the plaintiffs prove TCE reached the wells from the defendants' properties?) and Phase 2 (causation — could the plaintiffs prove that TCE in the water caused their children's specific leukemias?). Phase 1 ran for seventy-nine days. After the jury found W.R. Grace liable and Beatrice not liable, Skinner threw out the verdict on procedural grounds, and W.R. Grace settled for eight million dollars before Phase 2 began. It is Phase 2 that mattered — and it was never reached, because it never could have been. No epidemiological study, however careful, can answer a Phase 2 question as courts define it. 1+1 here produced two separate proceedings: one answering "did the contamination reach the wells?" and one attempting to answer "did this specific contamination cause this specific leukemia?" — a question that population-level science cannot address, by design.

The 18th camel was not another lawsuit. It was a consent decree: a negotiated EPA-facilitated agreement, separate from the tort action, that used the Phase 1 contamination finding as the trigger for remediation. This mechanism existed under CERCLA in 1982, when the suit was filed. It requires no proof of individual causation — only a documented association between contamination and health outcomes sufficient to trigger the agency's remediation authority. The families were simultaneously pursuing the only mechanism that could give them attribution (the lawsuit) and neglecting the only mechanism that could give them cleanup (the Superfund pathway). These could have been pursued in parallel, with EPA as a mediating force rather than a bystander. They were not.

A principled pragmatist in 1982 would have identified the Superfund consent decree as the first move, not the last resort. They would have recognized that the tort system was asking epidemiology to do something it cannot do, and found the institutional pathway that asked it to do what it actually does. The remediation eventually happened — through EPA, decades later. The families are still waiting for the legal acknowledgment.

*(Honest flag: American tort law requires individual proof of causation that population-level epidemiology cannot provide by design — these are two different questions that share a name. No framework of principled pragmatism resolves this mismatch without legislative reform. The gap is architectural: baked into the legal system, not addressable by joint fact-finding or better science.)*

---

**Problem** — How a community with strong epidemiological evidence of harm gains accountability when adversarial courts require individual causation proof that epidemiology cannot deliver.

**Stakeholders** — Twenty-one Woburn families; W.R. Grace and Beatrice Foods; EPA Superfund program; the nationwide precedent for community contamination liability.

**Binding constraint** — The adversarial civil liability system's requirement for individual causation proof, structurally incompatible with probabilistic epidemiological evidence.

**Decision pathway** — Redesign: Separate the remediation obligation (triggered by epidemiological association + contamination finding, via EPA consent decree) from the compensation question, pursuing both simultaneously through the appropriate institutional mechanism rather than forcing a single adversarial proceeding to carry both.

**Tools** — Joint fact-finding between EPA, defendants, and community, replacing adversarial expert battle and reducing the evidentiary uncertainty that made Phase 1 so protracted; epidemiological trigger standard for remediation, decoupling cleanup from tort; community health registry from the moment of contamination identification, producing longitudinal data that strengthens future attribution.

**Metrics** — Time from contamination identification to full remediation completion; fraction of affected households receiving legal acknowledgment or compensation of any form; whether the Lagakos/Zelen findings were replicated by an independent team within five years of the 1986 publication.

**90-day commitment** — Within 90 days of the Phase 1 contamination verdict (July 1986), EPA convenes a CERCLA consent decree process with all parties, using the jury's contamination finding as the remediation trigger — separate from and parallel to any Phase 2 tort proceeding, so cleanup does not wait on individual causation proof.
