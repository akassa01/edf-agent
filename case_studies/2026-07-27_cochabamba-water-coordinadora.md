# The Well They Already Built

On the morning of April 10, 2000, Óscar Olivera walked out of the Cochabamba prefecture building and stood before a crowd that had spent four months shutting down Bolivia's fourth-largest city. He was carrying a piece of paper. Aguas del Tunari, the private consortium that had held the water concession for Cochabamba's municipal system for less than five months, was withdrawing. The water law was being repealed. The government was canceling the contract. Olivera read the announcement aloud into the crowd and wept.

The Coordinadora de Defensa del Agua y de la Vida — the Coalition in Defense of Water and Life — had just won. But Cochabamba still did not have the water it needed.

The setup took less than a year. In September 1999, the Bolivian government awarded a forty-year concession for Cochabamba's municipal water utility (SEMAPA) to Aguas del Tunari, a consortium controlled by Bechtel's subsidiary International Water and Spain's Abengoa. The concession was one instrument of a World Bank structural adjustment package. Bolivia needed a $600 million loan; privatization of urban water utilities was a condition. The engineers were right that SEMAPA couldn't fund the Misicuni dam project — a tunnel and reservoir that would bring water from the Cochabamba valleys and significantly expand coverage — without private capital. That part of the analysis was accurate. The model had a different problem.

It erased the community wells.

Roughly forty percent of Cochabamba's 800,000 residents did not receive SEMAPA service. They had built their own water systems: community wells dug collectively, irrigation cooperatives managed through consensus, water committees (comités de agua) that allocated access during dry months. These systems existed because SEMAPA had never reached the peri-urban slopes, and they worked — imperfectly, unevenly, but on terms the communities controlled. When the economic model identified the forty percent as a "service gap to be filled," it was treating existing governance as absence of service. When Law 2029 was rushed through the Bolivian Congress in October 1999 to establish the legal basis of the concession, Article 50 extended Aguas del Tunari's territory of exclusivity to include "all water resources" within the concession area. In a single clause, the wells that communities had built with their own labor became private property they now technically owed royalties to use.

The Coordinadora understood this before most observers did. The protests that began in December 1999 were not simply about price increases, though the prices were shocking — some SEMAPA users saw bills reach twenty dollars a month, equivalent to a full month's minimum wage, with no improvement in service. They were about something harder to quantify: who decides who controls water. The Coordinadora's unusual breadth — factory workers' unions, farmers' cooperatives, professional associations, civic organizations — gave it a technical base alongside a moral one. Its lawyers identified Article 50 as the hinge. Its first demand was not "cancel the contract." It was: repeal Article 50.

The Scientist in this case is working from a model with a missing column. The World Bank's analysis was internally coherent and the SEMAPA data was accurate, but the baseline labeled "no service" was not no service. It was a different kind of service, organized on different principles. What cannot be measured does not appear in the model; what does not appear in the model does not appear in the contract. The World Bank's privatization analysis would have been identical if the community wells had been counted — only the conclusions would have changed.

The Humanist sees the wells as governance. In Cochabamba's peri-urban zones, the comités de agua were the institutions through which communities exercised a form of collective self-determination that formal politics did not offer. Criminalizing those wells — even if enforcement was unlikely — was experienced as expropriation of something that had never been for sale. The Coordinadora's humanist claim was that water governance was not a service delivery problem. It was a democracy problem. And democracy problems require different instruments than concession redesign.

The Engineer sees a genuine infrastructure dilemma that the Water War resolved politically but not technically. SEMAPA still could not fund Misicuni after the protests. The contract was gone but the problem remained. An engineering solution to Cochabamba's water scarcity required investment that the public utility could not self-finance. The camel — the option outside the apparent binary of full privatization or full public control — was technically available and was eventually enacted: Bolivia's 2004 Law 2066 distinguished between municipal networks and community-managed systems, creating a legal category that Law 2029 had refused. A concession covering only SEMAPA's existing formal connections and new Misicuni-funded infrastructure, with Article 50 amended to explicitly exempt community-built systems, would not have eliminated the financing challenge. It would have removed the clause that made the community systems legally illegal, and it might have survived.

Where is X in this case? It is Article 50 of Law 2029, and the specific moment in October 1999 when the Bolivian Congress passed it without meaningful debate. The provision was not an engineering necessity — Aguas del Tunari did not need jurisdiction over community wells to fund the Misicuni tunnel. It was a legal over-reach, a clause whose downstream effects were not modeled because the communities who depended on the wells were not at the table where the contract was written. Remove Article 50 and you substantially change the conflict. Keep it and the conflict cannot be resolved short of the company's withdrawal.

The 18th camel was a hybrid governance model: a regulated private operator responsible for SEMAPA's existing formal connections and all new Misicuni infrastructure, combined with legally recognized community management of existing independent water systems, with tiered pricing creating cross-subsidies from higher-consumption formal connections to new peri-urban connections. This is not a theoretical construction. It is what Bolivia built after the Water War, slowly, imperfectly, in Law 2066 and subsequent water governance reforms. The camel existed; it was simply not visible to anyone at the concession negotiation in September 1999, because no one at that table represented the communities for whom it would have been designed.

The Coordinadora did not take the camel when it was eventually offered in modified form. By December 1999, after the first round of protests and a failed government negotiation, the coalition had hardened to full contract cancellation. Some of this was strategic; some was the accumulated distrust of a government that had passed the law without debate and a company that had sent police to disperse protesters. When Victor Hugo Daza, seventeen years old, was shot in the face by a sharpshooter in civilian clothes on April 8, 2000, any remaining space for a modified offer closed. Four days later, the company left.

The camel was rejected. The outcome was vindicated. Olivera wept.

The counterfactual pathway — the one available at the decisive moment in September 1999 — was a Redesign of Article 50 before the contract was signed, in a process that included community water committee representatives on the drafting panel. The Bolivian water ministry could have commissioned a legal opinion on whether the clause covered existing community wells; it would have found that it did, and that this was not necessary for the concession to function; it could have amended the language before the law went to Congress. Within ninety days of the concession agreement, that specific redesign was available. It did not happen because community water committee leaders had no formal standing to demand it, because the World Bank's conditionality pressures created urgency around finalization, and because the government of Hugo Banzer had no domestic political interest in slowing down a process that was already moving.

The stress test reveals why the camel might have failed even if offered. The World Bank could have conditioned the loan on non-exemption of community systems. SEMAPA management could have resisted the mapping process that would have made the exemption legible. And there is a harder truth: the Coordinadora's humanist conviction — water as sovereignty, not service — might have been incompatible with any version of Aguas del Tunari in Cochabamba, regardless of what Article 50 said. The community water committees were not primarily a service delivery mechanism. They were a form of democratic self-governance. A private concession that exempted their wells might still have been a private concession in a space the Coordinadora believed should not be occupied.

*(The case leaves unresolved the deepest challenge it surfaces. When the humanist refusal to accept a modified offer is vindicated by the counterparty's exit, the 18th camel becomes retroactively unnecessary. But that vindication was contingent on Aguas del Tunari's decision to leave rather than fight — a decision the Coordinadora could not have known in advance. Engineering diplomacy can name the camel and design the hybrid model. It cannot tell you, in April 2000, whether your counterparty will negotiate or exit. When the outcome is vindicated by the other side's departure, the case teaches the hardest lesson: the decision under uncertainty was made correctly, and for reasons not fully available at the moment of decision.)*

---

**Problem** — How to structure a water concession that funds necessary infrastructure without extinguishing the community water governance that serves the population the municipal utility does not reach

**Stakeholders** — Peri-urban water committees (existing governance; no formal standing); SEMAPA customers (formal connections; facing rate increases); Aguas del Tunari (concession holder); World Bank (financing conditionality); Bolivian government (loan dependency; no domestic political base to resist); the Coordinadora (organized resistance; technical competence)

**Binding constraint** — World Bank loan conditionality tied the financing structure to privatization; without an alternative financing vehicle for Misicuni, modifying the concession reduced revenue projections the Bank required

**Decision pathway** — Redesign: amend Article 50 of Law 2029 before enactment to explicitly exempt existing community-built water systems from the concession's territory of exclusivity, through a joint drafting process including community water committee representatives

**Tools** — Joint legal review of Article 50 language with community water committee representatives (to identify the boundary between SEMAPA's formal network and independent community systems); community water system mapping (to enumerate and legally recognize existing cooperative structures, changing the concession baseline); tiered pricing feasibility study (to demonstrate that cross-subsidy from formal connections can offset community exemption without compromising Misicuni bankability)

**Metrics** — Number of community water systems legally exempted / total operating community systems; SEMAPA formal connection expansion rate in peri-urban areas, measured quarterly; real water cost differential between community system users and SEMAPA formal customers, tracked annually

**90-day commitment** — Within 90 days of the September 1999 concession agreement: (1) Bolivian water ministry commissions a legal opinion on whether Article 50 covers existing community wells, with Coordinadora representatives on the review panel; (2) joint working group with water committee leaders maps existing community systems and their relationship to SEMAPA's service boundary; (3) revised Article 50 draft is submitted to the World Bank, explicitly exempting community-built systems
