# The Genocide Fax

At 8:32 in the evening on January 11, 1994, Brigadier-General Roméo Dallaire, force commander of the United Nations Assistance Mission for Rwanda, sent a cable from Kigali to New York. The fax ran to four pages. It named an informant with direct access to the Interahamwe leadership — the Hutu militia being trained and armed for an organized campaign of mass killing. It listed weapons cache locations. It described the training methodology and the stated intent: to kill Tutsi civilians at a rate that would overwhelm any response. Dallaire had checked his informant's credibility. He proposed a raid on the caches within the next thirty-six hours. He requested authorization.

Iqbal Riza, deputy to Kofi Annan in the Department of Peacekeeping Operations, replied the next day. The proposed operation exceeded UNAMIR's Chapter VI mandate. Dallaire was not to execute a raid. He was, instead, to share the intelligence with Rwandan President Habyarimana — the head of the government whose militia the informant had just described.

The genocide began on April 7, 1994, twelve weeks later. Within one hundred days, approximately 800,000 people were killed, mostly Tutsi and moderate Hutu, at a rate that exceeded the Holocaust's daily pace at its height. Dallaire watched it happen with 2,500 troops, a mandate that did not allow him to protect the people he was watching die, and the knowledge of what the fax had said.

The scientist's reading of this case is precise and, in a narrow sense, sympathetic to Riza's decision. Dallaire's informant intelligence was specific, but the UN Secretariat applied an evidential threshold: it could not independently verify the intelligence before acting, and acting on unverified intelligence — raiding weapons caches in a country nominally at peace — carried its own risks. The scientist asks: at what confidence level was the fax confirmed, and what are the false-positive costs of military action on an erroneous intelligence assessment? These are legitimate questions. They are also, in this case, the wrong frame. Waiting for certainty was itself a choice with a body count. The scientist's threshold was applied to prevent error. It produced catastrophe.

The humanist sees something the scientist cannot compute. The 800,000 people who would be killed had no seat at the table where the decision about their fate was made. The Security Council members — processing the Genocide Fax as an institutional question about mandate language — were not computing human loss. They were computing political risk. The United States State Department had issued explicit guidance to its officials: do not use the word "genocide" in press briefings. The reason was legalistic but precise: the 1948 Genocide Convention required signatories to take action to prevent genocide once it was recognized. If no one named it, no one was obligated to stop it. The manufactured ambiguity between "genocide" and "civil war" was not a scientific failure. It was a political decision to keep moral obligation in suspension.

The engineer sees a gap between capacity and authorization. Dallaire had 2,500 troops in-country and specific intelligence about where the weapons were. He believed, and later wrote, that he could have executed a targeted raid on the caches with what he had. The Security Council could have amended his mandate in a session of hours. France and Belgium — both with rapid-deployment capacity — were separately capable of bilateral reinforcement. The binding constraint was not military: it was the absence of a member state willing to lead a mandate expansion. Every P5 member was calculating post-Somalia exposure. No one wanted to go first.

The X is January 11, 1994, at the moment Riza responded to the fax. The UN Charter's Article 99 gives the Secretary-General the explicit authority to "bring to the attention of the Security Council any matter which in his opinion may threaten the maintenance of international peace and security." This authority — which bypasses the need for a member state to raise the issue — had not been invoked since 1979. Boutros-Ghali had it. He did not use it. Had the Genocide Fax been forwarded to Security Council members directly by the Secretary-General under Article 99, the decision would have been made where it belonged: by the people with the authority to authorize action, in public, on the record.

The 18th camel was not military intervention. It was an emergency Security Council session under Article 99, called within forty-eight hours of the fax, to issue a targeted, geographically limited, time-bounded mandate amendment authorizing Dallaire to raid the weapons caches — not to take sides in a civil war, but to prevent a specific, documented, named plan. Dallaire's own proposal was essentially this. The Secretary-General had the standing to convene it. The session was never called.

The Solomon Paradox is explicit here. Security Council members who would never have hesitated to invoke Article 99 for an equivalent intelligence assessment about a European city — specific, named informant, verifiable cache locations, stated genocidal intent — declined to use it for Rwanda. The distance that produces wisdom in the Solomon Paradox was, in this case, replaced by the calculation that African lives could be managed at lower political cost than European ones.

(What this case does not resolve: the 18th camel — the targeted mandate amendment via Article 99 — was genuinely available and was not used. But a reframe tool can only be used by someone with standing. When the Secretary-General's office itself declines to use its own authority, principled pragmatism has no escalation path. There is no mechanism in the UN Charter that compels an institution to use its own power when doing so would embarrass the members who fund it. The tool exists. The willingness to use it is the missing variable, and no framework produces that willingness.)

---

**Problem** — Given a specific, verified intelligence warning of organized genocide in Rwanda, the UN Secretariat chose mandate compliance over preventive action because no member state was willing to lead an authorization and the Secretary-General did not invoke his Article 99 authority to convene an emergency Security Council session.

**Stakeholders** — Gen. Dallaire and UNAMIR (in-country capacity, no authorization); Tutsi and moderate Hutu civilians (affected population, no representation); Kofi Annan's office and Iqbal Riza (mandate interpretation, procedural gatekeepers); Security Council P5 members, especially the US and France (authorization authority, managing post-Somalia risk exposure); Belgium (troops deployed, withdrew after 10 paratroopers killed April 7); Secretary-General Boutros-Ghali (unused Article 99 authority).

**Binding constraint** — The absence of a member state willing to lead a mandate expansion, combined with the Secretary-General's office choosing not to invoke Article 99.

**Decision pathway** — Reframe: the Secretariat asked "does our current mandate allow this?" The move that was available: "what is the minimum mandate amendment that would allow Dallaire to act — and who has standing to convene the session to authorize it?" This redirects from rules-compliance to purpose-fulfillment and locates the tool: Article 99, unused since 1979, within the Secretary-General's unilateral authority.

**Tools** — Emergency Security Council session under Article 99 (changes interaction: elevates the decision to the authorization level, bypasses Secretariat caution); targeted mandate amendment with sunset and geographic limits — Kigali, 30 days (changes coordination: gives Dallaire specific authorization without permanent commitment from risk-averse member states); public intelligence disclosure if Security Council stalls (changes learning: makes the failure to act visible in real time, activates Convention accountability in the open rather than in a classified cable).

**Metrics** — Days between receipt of Genocide Fax and Security Council emergency session (target: ≤ 5 days; method: UN meeting records); proportion of weapons cache locations in the fax confirmed raided within mandate window (target: ≥ 80%; method: UNAMIR after-action report); civilians in UNAMIR-protected zones per week (method: commander field reports) — tracks whether the mandate amendment achieves its stated purpose.

**90-day commitment** — [Counterfactual] Within 90 days of January 11, the Secretary-General invokes Article 99 and convenes an emergency Security Council session by January 16. Dallaire is authorized a targeted 72-hour operation against named cache locations. A public early warning statement is released, naming the Interahamwe's documented capability and stated intent. By February 15, the mandate is expanded to civilian protection within Kigali. France and Belgium are formally requested to contribute one additional battalion each.
