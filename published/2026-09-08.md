# The Only Check: Frances Kelsey and the Thalidomide Decision

In September 1960, Frances Oldham Kelsey received her first major assignment at the U.S. Food and Drug Administration: a new drug application for Kevadon, a sedative-hypnotic already approved across Europe and marketed in West Germany under the name Contergan. She was new to the job, a pharmacologist who had spent her career studying how drugs behave in biological tissue, including fetal tissue. The company that submitted the application, Richardson-Merrell of Cincinnati, expected routine processing. By the standards of the day, they were not wrong to expect it.

Kelsey opened the file and found it wanting.

The application contained the pharmacological data the company had gathered — clinical reports, animal studies, summaries of European use — but the data was company-generated and, in Kelsey's judgment, insufficient to establish safety across the patient population that mattered. She declared the application "incomplete," which under the 1938 Federal Food, Drug, and Cosmetic Act reset the sixty-day review clock rather than rejecting the drug outright. It was a procedural move, not a veto. Richardson-Merrell submitted additional materials. She declared it incomplete again. Over the next sixteen months, this sequence repeated four times.

The pressure did not remain polite. Richardson-Merrell sent delegations to the FDA. They wrote to Kelsey's supervisors characterising her review as capricious delay. They threatened legal action. A new hire resisting a drug already approved across most of the developed world was not, institutionally, on solid ground.

In February 1961, she read a letter in the *British Medical Journal* from a British physician reporting cases of peripheral neuritis — damage to nerves in the extremities — among patients taking thalidomide. The connection she drew from this report was not in the published literature: if the drug's mechanism affected peripheral nerve cells, it might affect developing fetal tissue. She wrote to Richardson-Merrell demanding evidence that Kevadon was not harmful to the fetus. The company could not produce it. The clock reset again.

By March 1962, Chemie Grünenthal had withdrawn thalidomide from the West German market. The published teratogenicity evidence — infants born with severe limb reduction defects, phocomelia, hands or feet attached directly to the trunk — was everywhere. Richardson-Merrell withdrew its American application. President Kennedy presented Kelsey with the President's Award for Distinguished Federal Civilian Service on August 7, 1962. The Kefauver-Harris Drug Amendments — requiring proof of efficacy as well as safety, eliminating auto-approval by inaction, mandating informed consent for investigational studies — were signed into law on October 10.

Approximately 10,000 to 20,000 children were born with thalidomide-induced defects outside the United States. A small number of cases occurred in America anyway, because the company had legally distributed the drug to roughly 1,200 American physicians as investigational samples while the NDA was pending — a loophole that required no FDA approval and no mandatory reporting.

The Scientist in this story is not hard to find: Kelsey, working from pharmacological training that included research on fetal drug metabolism, reading a signal in the *BMJ* correspondence that her colleagues did not and inferring a mechanism that had not yet been published. The inference was correct, but it was a heuristic rather than a demonstrated chain. The system worked because one scientist made one correct inference under conditions designed to produce the opposite result. The scientific literature did not lead the regulatory decision; the regulatory decision led the scientific literature.

The Humanist sees what the regulatory record cannot accommodate: that the patients most at risk were being actively recruited. Richardson-Merrell's marketing materials positioned Kevadon for morning sickness in pregnant women. There was no mechanism for those future patients — the unborn — to have any standing in the FDA review process. Kelsey described the company's pressure campaigns not only as scientifically inadequate but as morally uncomfortable: she felt things were being hidden. The Humanist notes that her discomfort was epistemically useful. She was picking up a signal — about the company's confidence in its own data — that did not appear in the pharmacological record.

The Engineer looks at the statutory mechanism and asks who designed this. The 1938 FDCA gave the FDA sixty days to object; if no objection was raised within that window, the drug was approved automatically. The burden of proof was inverted: the agency had to show danger, not the company safety. There was no conditional approval pathway — no regulatory vocabulary for "proceed with monitoring." There was no mandatory reporting from the investigational sample distribution to 1,200 physicians. The company was legally distributing a potentially dangerous drug to American patients while the review was ongoing, generating no evidence that the agency could access. Kelsey's tool — the "incomplete" declaration — restarted the clock but gave her nothing affirmative to do. She was holding a door closed with both hands.

Where is X? The February 1961 letter Kelsey sent to Richardson-Merrell citing the *BMJ* peripheral neuritis correspondence and demanding fetal safety data. That letter changed the nature of the dispute: no longer a paperwork argument about completeness but a scientific argument about mechanism, one the company had no answer for and could not pressure her to withdraw.

What question were the parties answering? Richardson-Merrell was asking: "Is the application compliant with FDA filing requirements?" Kelsey was asking: "Is this drug safe for the patient population the company is targeting?" These were not the same question. The 1938 Act did not require them to be. The company's application was an answer to the first question; Kelsey's review was asking the second.

The 18th camel — the option outside the binary of approve or reject — was conditional approval with mandatory adverse-event reporting from investigational use. This pathway did not exist in 1960 American law. It would have allowed the drug's legitimate uses to proceed, built a real-world evidence base from the company's own physician distribution program, and preserved Kelsey's scientific judgment as a condition rather than a veto. The Kefauver-Harris Amendment built exactly this capacity into the regulatory system. The 18th camel was available; it required statute to put it there.

The decision pathway that was available at the decisive moment was Redesign — not the binary choice of approving or rejecting this particular drug, but a redesign of the regulatory framework so that scientific judgment was embedded institutionally rather than concentrated in individual reviewers. The mechanism was the Kefauver Senate hearings, already underway. The tool was legislative: converting Kelsey's documented scientific concerns into a hearing record that specified the statutory gap and proposed the remedy. Who acts: the FDA Commissioner, in testimony to the Kefauver committee, citing the Kevadon case as evidence of structural failure rather than individual heroism. For whom: future patients with no representative in the regulatory process. Through what process: a new statute that defines "adequate safety data," eliminates auto-approval by inaction, and creates the conditional approval pathway that the law lacked.

The tools the counterfactual pathway required: jointly developed scientific standards for what "adequate safety data" means, so that the question is not negotiated case by case; mandatory adverse-event reporting from investigational use, turning the company's own distribution program into a surveillance system; conditional approval authority, so the FDA can say "proceed under these conditions" rather than being forced into a binary determination. Each tool changes the nature of the interaction — from company-FDA negotiation to shared scientific standard; from black box to reportable; from approve/reject to conditional and reviewable.

(What the episode reveals that principled pragmatism itself does not resolve: the framework can locate X, name the 18th camel, and specify the decision pathway. What it cannot guarantee is that the person at X on any given day is Frances Kelsey. She had the right training, the right supervisor, and the institutional courage to use the same procedural tool four times. Change any one of those and the drug is approved. Regulatory design cannot bank on heroism; it must make heroism unnecessary.)

---

**Problem** — Whether a regulatory framework built for speed can be made to also hold the line on scientific rigor, or whether the two goals require different institutional structures.

**Stakeholders** — Future patients (no standing in the review process); pharmaceutical manufacturers (legal applicants with full institutional access); FDA reviewers (gatekeepers with procedural but not affirmative authority); Congress (statutory architects whose 1938 design had not been updated for a more sophisticated industry).

**Binding constraint** — The 1938 FDCA's inverted burden of proof: the agency had to show danger within a window, not the company safety on a standard. The constraint was statutory, not scientific.

**Decision pathway** — Redesign: use the Kefauver hearings to convert Kelsey's individual scientific determinations into a legislative record specifying the statutory gap and mandating the remedy.

**Tools** — Jointly defined safety data standards (moves the question from negotiation to scientific baseline); mandatory investigational adverse-event reporting (converts physician distribution into surveillance); conditional approval authority (creates the 18th camel the law lacked).

**Metrics** — Time from first adverse-event signal to market withdrawal in each country (benchmark for surveillance system effectiveness); proportion of NDA pharmacological data independently verified vs. company-submitted only; adverse events per 1,000 drug-months of investigational use.

**90-day commitment** — Within 90 days of the February 1961 letter, the FDA Commissioner convenes an independent expert panel to establish minimum teratogenicity data requirements for drugs to be marketed to people capable of pregnancy and submits findings to the Kefauver committee as a statutory amendment proposal.

# The Blank Article: The WHO Pandemic Agreement and the Mechanism It Cannot Finish

On May 20, 2025, the gavel came down in Geneva and the World Health Assembly adopted the WHO Pandemic Agreement. Precious Matsoso, the South African pharmacist and health official who had co-chaired the three-year negotiation, called it a landmark. Tedros Adhanom Ghebreyesus called it historic. In the chamber, delegates from nearly 170 member states applauded. The United States, which had withdrawn from WHO under the Trump administration months earlier, was not among them.

What the agreement contained was substantial: 35 articles on surveillance, laboratory networks, health system financing, and a commitment that the world would not repeat the equity failures of COVID-19. What it did not contain was Article 12's Annex — the operational document specifying precisely how pathogen samples and genomic data would be shared with WHO, how vaccine and therapeutic production built on those samples would flow back to the countries that contributed them, and what legal mechanism would enforce that flow when a new pandemic arrives and the political pressure to hoard begins. The Annex was formally deferred to the 2026 World Health Assembly. The Agreement could not open for signatures without it.

The negotiating body — the Intergovernmental Negotiating Body, or INB — had spent months on exactly this problem. By November 2024, of the agreement's 35 articles, roughly 17 were fully agreed and 12 were substantially agreed, operating under the principle that nothing is agreed until everything is agreed. The sticking points were the same two they had been for two years: Article 11, on technology transfer, and Article 12, on Pathogen Access and Benefit Sharing. Rich countries and the pharmaceutical industry wanted both to be voluntary, on mutually agreed terms. The bloc of approximately 100 low- and middle-income countries wanted both to be mandatory, legally binding, with a defined set-aside — ten percent of pandemic product production donated at no cost, another ten percent at affordable prices — as the explicit quid pro quo for rapidly sharing pathogen samples with WHO.

The impasse was not a negotiating failure. It was a diagnosis. Both sides were right about different things.

The Humanist reads the LMIC position not as a negotiating stance but as a lived record. COVID-19 vaccines were developed using viral genomic data shared by dozens of countries, including the low-income ones most likely to be devastated by a pandemic. When the vaccines arrived, rich countries pre-purchased at scale, triggered export bans, stockpiled far beyond what they needed, and argued through surrogates at the World Trade Organization that intellectual property protections were non-negotiable. COVAX — the international mechanism designed to ensure equitable distribution — delivered its first doses eight months after the wealthiest countries had begun mass vaccination. The feeling mode governing the LMIC bloc's position is not sentiment; it is evidence. The mandatory set-aside is not an aspirational demand. It is a minimum condition for the agreement to mean anything.

The Scientist sees a mechanism design problem. The PABS Annex is blank not because countries failed to agree on the aspiration — they all endorsed equity — but because they could not agree on the operational definition of benefit-sharing. "Ten percent at no cost" sounds like a clear number. It is not. Ten percent of what production, when, from which manufacturers, through which logistics channel, allocated by which formula to which countries, enforced by which dispute mechanism? Each of these questions is a separate negotiation, and the draft agreement answers none of them. The Scientist also notes that the mechanism must function during a PHEIC — a declared public health emergency — when factories are running at capacity, governments are imposing export controls, and the political environment is the opposite of a peacetime equity conversation. A mechanism that assumes peacetime behavior will fail at exactly the moment it is needed.

The Engineer sees where the machinery is missing. The COVAX failure was not primarily a funding failure; it was an architecture failure. Equity was operationalised as a charitable purchase program — rich countries choosing how much to buy for others — rather than as a pre-committed legal obligation. When the political pressure intensified domestically, the charitable frame collapsed immediately. The Engineer asks: what architecture survives domestic political pressure? The answer is pre-committed contracts — obligations created at the moment of pathogen sharing, not at the moment of production, when the emergency is already underway and the incentive to defect is highest.

Where is X? The clause in the PABS Annex that defines when benefit-sharing obligations are triggered and how they are calculated. This clause does not yet exist. The intervention point is the drafting of this clause in the WHA-79 negotiation — and specifically, whether the obligation is ex ante (committed at the moment of sharing) or ex post (negotiated at the moment of production). Ex ante commitments survive political crises. Ex post ones do not.

What question were the parties answering? LMIC negotiators were asking: "What legal guarantee ensures our populations receive vaccines during the next pandemic?" High-income negotiators were asking: "What political commitment demonstrates solidarity without legally binding our pharmaceutical sector during an emergency we cannot predict?" Both called their answer "benefit-sharing." 1+1 produced two different documents that needed to be a single Annex.

The 18th camel is a pandemic preparedness credit system. When a country shares pathogen samples and genomic sequence data with WHO, it earns denominated credits in a WHO-administered ledger — valued in product-equivalent units calibrated to the severity of the potential pandemic. When a declared PHEIC uses those materials to develop vaccines or therapeutics, credit-holders receive product allocations that mature from the ledger automatically, without a new political negotiation at the moment of crisis. This is neither the mandatory percentage the LMIC bloc demands nor the voluntary goodwill the pharma bloc offers. It is a pre-committed contractual claim, created at the moment of sharing. It makes the equity obligation legible to manufacturers — a known future liability, not an open-ended exposure — and it makes the LMIC entitlement a legal claim, not a donor request. It also creates an incentive for rapid pathogen sharing: more sharing, more credits, more entitlement. The structure is insurance, not charity.

The decision pathway is Reframe. The negotiation has been locked in a binary — mandatory percentage or voluntary goodwill — that both sides reject in the other's hands. Reframing the mechanism as a pre-committed contract changes the question from "how much will you give us during a crisis?" to "what is the legal value of the sharing contribution you are already making?" WHO Director-General Tedros and Co-Chair Matsoso table specific Annex language — three fully specified alternative mechanisms — before WHA-79, so that member states are choosing among operational options rather than negotiating in the abstract.

(What the episode reveals that principled pragmatism itself does not resolve: any mechanism pre-committing rich-country vaccine production to LMIC distribution during an emergency will face the same political pressure that broke COVAX. Governments that signed the Paris Agreement have also missed their emissions targets. Legal commitments survive political crises when the costs of violation exceed the costs of compliance; in a pandemic, the domestic cost calculus runs the other way. The PABS Annex can be more precisely drafted than COVAX was designed. It cannot be more legally binding than the governments that signed it choose to be when their own populations are dying. That gap — between legal commitment and political survival — is the limitation this framework does not resolve.)

---

**Problem** — How to design a PABS mechanism that is specific enough to distribute vaccines during an actual PHEIC, and politically acceptable enough to retain high-income country signatures and pharmaceutical industry participation, knowing that the mechanism will face maximum political pressure at exactly the moment it must function.

**Stakeholders** — LMIC governments (primary beneficiaries, primary pathogen-sharers, minimum leverage); high-income country governments (pharmaceutical sector principals, maximum leverage); pharmaceutical manufacturers (mechanism participants or non-participants, depending on whether participation is commercially viable); WHO Secretariat (mechanism administrator, no enforcement authority).

**Binding constraint** — The Agreement cannot open for signatures without the Annex. The political constraint is that any mechanism specific enough to actually distribute vaccines during an emergency will face domestic political opposition in high-income countries at exactly the moment of an emergency.

**Decision pathway** — Reframe: shift from mandatory-percentage vs. voluntary-goodwill binary to a pre-committed credit system that makes benefit-sharing a contractual claim at the moment of pathogen sharing, not a political negotiation at the moment of production.

**Tools** — Pandemic preparedness credit ledger (converts sharing into pre-committed entitlement; changes interaction from charity to contract); joint technical panel on Annex operationalisation (produces shared factual basis for what "10% at no cost" means; changes learning dynamic from adversarial to joint modelling); manufacturer participation agreement with liability clarity (converts open-ended exposure to defined obligation; changes coordination between HICs and their pharmaceutical sectors).

**Metrics** — Proportion of global vaccine and therapeutic manufacturing capacity signed into PABS at WHA-79; time from PHEIC declaration to first PABS product delivery to LMIC (benchmark: COVAX baseline of approximately eight months); preparedness credit redemption rate within 90 days of eligibility during a declared PHEIC.

**90-day commitment** — Within 90 days of WHA-78's adjournment (by August 2025), WHO Secretariat tables a draft Annex with three fully specified alternative mechanisms — preparedness credit model, tiered purchase obligation model, manufacturer participation agreement model — each with complete operational detail, so that WHA-79 chooses among options rather than negotiating in the abstract.
