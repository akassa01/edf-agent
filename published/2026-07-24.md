# The Conviction That Couldn't Convince

In the microbiology laboratory at Royal Perth Hospital, on a morning in July 1984, Barry Marshall swallowed a beaker of liquid containing ten million bacteria and waited.

He had tried everything else. The abstract he and his colleague Robin Warren had submitted to the Gastroenterological Society of Australia the previous year had been rejected without presentation — twelve of sixty-eight abstracts turned away, theirs among them, the committee finding no reason to give floor time to the claim that a bacterium caused peptic ulcers. The full paper they published in The Lancet in June 1984, documenting bacterial presence in the stomachs of one hundred patients with gastritis and peptic ulcer, was read and dismissed: the bacteria were most likely contaminants, or a consequence of the ulcers rather than their cause. What Marshall had was correlation. What the profession required, before it would revise forty years of antacid prescriptions and the surgical careers built on removing portions of hyperacid stomachs, was proof of causation. And so Marshall drank.

By day eight, endoscopy confirmed acute gastritis. The biopsy was positive. The hypothesis — that the curved bacterium Warren had noticed on biopsy specimens in 1979, later named Helicobacter pylori, was the primary cause of peptic ulcer disease — had been tested against the only body in the experiment Marshall could unambiguously control. He treated himself with antibiotics and bismuth, and recovered. Koch's postulates, the gold standard of infectious disease causation, were satisfied. Marshall was right.

And for the next decade, almost nothing changed.

The Scientist's reading of this episode is that the evidence was complete and correct. Warren had been observing the curved bacterium since 1979, living in the acid environment bacteriology textbooks said was sterile. Marshall joined the project in 1981 as a registrar and spent three years building the clinical case. The Lancet paper was rigorous. The self-experiment was unambiguous. The small double-blind trial that followed demonstrated antibiotic eradication of H. pylori and ulcer remission. By the mid-1990s, multiple FDA-approved regimens existed; a CDC survey in 1997 — thirteen years after the Lancet paper — documented that a substantial fraction of US physicians still did not associate peptic ulcer disease with the bacterium. In 2005, Marshall and Warren received the Nobel Prize in Physiology or Medicine. The Scientist sees a discovery correctly made and eventually adopted.

The Humanist sees the decade between them.

The gastroenterology profession in 1984 was not a collection of dullards refusing evidence out of spite. It was a community whose clinical identity was built on the hyperacid-stress model of peptic ulcer disease — a model that had produced Tagamet, the first drug ever to exceed one billion dollars in annual global sales, and Zantac, the world's best-selling pharmaceutical by 1988. Accepting Marshall's bacterium meant not just adding an item to the diagnostic checklist. It meant revising the foundation on which careers, curricula, surgical practices, and commercial relationships rested. The profession's resistance was not anti-scientific. It was the immune response of a knowledge community to a finding that, if true, implicated the knowledge itself. Marshall treated this response as an obstacle to be overcome by more evidence. He was correct that it was irrelevant to the science. He was wrong that it was therefore irrelevant.

The Engineer's reading: the pathway from a heterodox finding to changed clinical practice runs through specific gatekeeping institutions — medical society endorsement, NIH consensus development, pharmaceutical licensing, medical education revision. The NIH Consensus Development Conference on peptic ulcer disease, held in 1985, reviewed the evidence and found it insufficient for recommending antibiotic therapy. This was not a scientific error; it was an institutional verdict shaped by whose testimony was weighted most heavily, which in turn reflected the standing of the witnesses. Marshall was a hospital registrar with no department chair behind him, no US academic network, no standing at the NIH. The pathway for a finding to change practice required institutional standing Marshall did not have. The self-experiment was the opposite of what the engineering situation required: it proved causation to Marshall's own satisfaction while leaving the institutional engineering entirely undone.

Where is X? The September 1984 International Symposium on Campylobacter Infections in Brussels, where Marshall presented the self-experiment data to an international audience for the first time. A room of the world's gastroenterological researchers. Marshall at the front with endoscopy results showing he had infected and cured himself. This was the most precise moment where he could have converted "here is what I proved" into "here is what we should prove together." A proposal from that floor — Marshall and two skeptical European co-investigators designing a multi-site double-blind trial — would have produced evidence the profession could not claim was idiosyncratic to one registrar in Perth.

The 18th camel was not a different experiment. It was a different kind of invitation.

The apparent binary: the profession accepts Marshall's individual evidence and revises the ulcer model, or the profession rejects the evidence and waits. Both options positioned Marshall as the sole generator of truth, asking the profession to adopt it or refuse it. The third option: propose a joint replication study in which the skeptics design the experiment. Not "here is my evidence, now believe me" but "here is my hypothesis, let us test it together." When a skeptical department chair in Cleveland runs the protocol and H. pylori appears in her biopsy specimens, the finding belongs to her evidence base as much as to Marshall's. The resistance is structural — the professors most capable of sponsoring such a trial were also the professors with the most to lose if Marshall was right. But finding one willing collaborator and working through that collaborator's institutional standing was the engineering problem Marshall never solved.

What 1+1 actually produced: Marshall was answering "how do I make this evidence undeniable?" The profession was answering "who is this registrar, and what does it mean for us if he is right?" These are not the same question. Marshall's demonstrations answered the first with growing force. The second, which was actually blocking adoption, required a different action: not more proof, but an invitation to co-produce the proof. The Solomon Paradox: any gastroenterologist in that profession would have advised a colleague facing disbelief for a heterodox finding to propose a multicenter trial, get the skeptics as co-investigators, make the evidence theirs as well as yours. Marshall gave exactly this advice to no one, because no one had come to him with this problem. He was inside it. Inside it, he could see only how to make the proof more overwhelming. Not how to make it more adoptable.

(The flag: a correct finding arrived at by an institutionally marginal figure faces a structural double-bind that principled pragmatism cannot dissolve from within it. The professors who could have sponsored a collaborative trial were also the professors with the most to lose if the finding was true. The 18th camel required a collaborator with institutional standing. Neither Marshall nor Warren had enough standing to compel that collaboration. The framework can identify the pathway; it cannot grant you the position from which to walk it.)

---

**Problem** — Barry Marshall had sufficient evidence that H. pylori causes peptic ulcers; his strategy for making that evidence matter treated professional adoption as a scientific problem rather than a social and institutional one.

**Stakeholders** — Marshall and Warren (the discoverers, without institutional standing to convene multi-site trials or set the NIH agenda); the gastroenterology profession (the gatekeeper community, initially resistant); pharmaceutical companies (commercial interest in the antacid model); NIH and academic medicine (the institutions setting evidence standards for clinical practice change); approximately 4 million Americans with active peptic ulcer disease at any given time (affected parties with no standing in the room).

**Binding constraint** — Marshall's position as a hospital registrar. He could generate evidence; he could not convene the institutional process that would make that evidence belong to the mainstream.

**Decision pathway** — Reframe: shift from "how do I prove that I am right?" to "how do I make the skeptics co-produce the evidence that changes their own practice?" Who acts: Marshall, approaching a senior gastroenterologist willing to sponsor a multi-site replication trial as a genuine test rather than a validation exercise. Through what process: skeptics design the inclusion criteria and run the endoscopies; the finding emerges from within the profession's own evidence-generating system.

**Tools** — Co-investigator replication trial (converts the relation from adversarial to collaborative; makes the evidence belong to the mainstream, not to a peripheral figure; changes learning); explicit scoping of professional implications at presentations (separates "is the bacterium there?" from "what would we have to change if it is?" — creates space to evaluate the science before confronting the institutional consequences; changes interaction).

**Metrics** — Number of independent labs confirming H. pylori in blinded multi-site study (measures adoption of the finding by the profession's own evidence system; method: published trial results, coordinated by the European H. pylori Study Group established in 1987); time to NIH consensus endorsement compared against the actual 1984–mid-1990s timeline (measures the institutional lag; method: historical analysis against the modeled co-investigator-trial timeline).

**90-day commitment** — October 1984: Marshall approaches a senior gastroenterologist at University of Melbourne or University of Edinburgh to sponsor a co-investigator replication trial. November 1984: protocol drafted with skeptic input on inclusion criteria. December 1984–January 1985: trial begins at three sites; skeptics run their own endoscopies with blinded pathology.

# The Invisible River

On December 9, 2024, a Syrian transitional government took control of Damascus. The man who had governed Syria for twenty-four years, and whose regime had managed the country's relationship with Turkey, Iraq, and the Euphrates River as an instrument of political leverage, was gone. His successor — a coalition whose diplomatic slate was essentially blank — inherited a state built on the banks of a river that was running out of water.

The Euphrates enters Turkey in the highlands of eastern Anatolia. By the time it crosses into Syria at the Jarabulus border station, it carries approximately sixty percent of the flow it carried in the 1970s, reduced by Turkey's Southeastern Anatolia Project (GAP), a network of twenty-two dams and nineteen hydroelectric plants that now captures and regulates the river before it leaves Turkish territory. By the time the Euphrates reaches Iraq — crossing the Syrian border near Albukamal, passing through Anbar and Babil and Najaf toward the southern marshes — it carries less still. Iraq's farmers in the Euphrates valley are managing an agricultural crisis with no diplomatic mechanism to address its upstream cause.

The engineering of this situation is not ambiguous. Turkey built what it built. The GAP project, approximately eighty-two percent complete as of 2023, has transformed the headwaters of the Euphrates and Tigris from seasonally variable rivers into dam-regulated systems with approximately 5,500 megawatts of installed hydroelectric capacity. The 1987 Protocol between Turkey and Syria — the "Protocol on Matters Pertaining to Economic Cooperation" signed 17 July 1987 — commits Turkey to maintain a minimum average annual flow of 500 cubic meters per second at the Syrian border. Whether Turkey is meeting this commitment, and what that flow means for downstream Iraq, is unknown to any party with certainty. There is no shared monitoring mechanism that all three countries trust.

This is the river that is invisible. Not the physical river — that exists, diminished. The data river: the jointly-verified, commonly-agreed measurement of what is flowing where, at what rate, under what conditions. Turkey's water ministry publishes its own dam operations data. Iraq's Ministry of Water Resources publishes its own measurements. Syria's data infrastructure was badly damaged in the civil war and is being rebuilt. Where the data does not agree, negotiation cannot be grounded.

The Scientist's reading of the basin's current state is a study in contested causation. Peer-reviewed literature estimates a long-run forty-percent decline in Euphrates flows to Iraq from historical baselines; drought years have brought flows at specific downstream gauging points seventy to ninety percent below seasonal averages. But how much of Iraq's water deficit comes from Turkey's dams, how much from climate change reducing headwater precipitation, how much from Syria's own agricultural consumption, and how much from Iraq's own irrigation inefficiency — estimated at twenty-five to thirty percent water use efficiency — is genuinely disputed in the academic literature. A joint fact-finding exercise is not a precondition for a pleasant negotiation. It is a precondition for any negotiation. Right now the three countries are not arguing about the same river; they are each arguing about a nationally-constructed estimate of a river that no one has jointly measured.

The Humanist's reading is about who is not in the room. Iraq's approximately 4.6 million farmers in the Euphrates and Tigris valleys have no standing in the bilateral Turkey-Iraq financial framework signed in November 2025, which channeled oil payments into water infrastructure built by Turkish firms but contained no binding flow guarantees. The Marsh Arabs — whose ecosystem Saddam Hussein drained in the 1990s and who saw partial restoration after 2003 when the drainage works were dismantled — face renewed stress from reduced inflows and rising salinity. Syria's Euphrates valley farmers, in Deir ez-Zor and Raqqa and Aleppo, are the constituency of a government too new and too fragile to have negotiated anything yet. The urgency these communities feel is precisely inverted with their diplomatic representation: maximum urgency, minimum voice.

The Engineer's reading notices what the Humanist describes but names it differently: this is a governance design problem. Turkey can modulate its reservoir releases; the Atatürk, Karakaya, and Keban dams all have operational flexibility. The question is not whether Turkey can release more water. It is whether any mechanism tells Turkey what downstream conditions require, what the 1987 protocol commits it to provide, and whether it is providing it. The 1989 Syria-Iraq bilateral agreement allocates fifty-eight percent of what Syria receives to Iraq. The Joint Technical Committee established by Turkey, Iraq, and Syria in 1980 held sixteen sessions and collapsed in 1993 without reaching an allocation agreement. It has not been reconvened. In its place: two bilateral deals, no shared data, and three countries each operating on a different river.

Where is X? The window is the present — July through September 2026. Syria's transitional government is less than two years old. It has signed a bilateral data-sharing memorandum of understanding with Iraq in 2025, committing both to daily flow data exchange at the Jarabulus border station. It is re-engaging Turkey diplomatically for the first time in decades. Syria is the middle riparian: upstream of Iraq, downstream of Turkey, the country through whose territory the Euphrates flows between them. Syria is the only party that can propose a trilateral extension of the existing bilateral monitoring compact and be taken seriously by both Turkey and Iraq simultaneously. The Iraq-Syria MoU is the architecture. Syria can propose to Turkey that it join.

The 18th camel is not a water allocation treaty. Turkey will not sign one — it would require acknowledging prior use as a binding legal principle and would constrain GAP operations, which are politically untouchable domestically. The 18th camel is a Tigris-Euphrates Hydrological Transparency Compact: a data-sharing and early-warning protocol that all three countries can join without conceding their legal positions. Turkey does not have to acknowledge prior use; it shares flow data at dam outfall stations. Iraq and Syria do not have to accept Turkey's development rights arguments; they share their consumption data. The compact is not about who gets what. It is about what is actually happening, so that when the three countries eventually argue about who gets what, they are arguing about the same river.

What 1+1 currently produces: Turkey is answering "how do we operate our dam system for our national development?" Iraq is answering "how do we get enough water for our farmers?" Syria is answering "how do we rebuild our agricultural economy?" Three separate questions. Each refers to a different river, because each country's data describes a different river. The Solomon check: Turkey's water ministry officials know exactly what they would argue if the positions were reversed — if an upstream state were operating dams that reduced Turkey's flow by forty percent without sharing monitoring data. The wisdom of joint fact-finding is available to every party in the room. None can direct it inward, because each is simultaneously the problem and the diagnostician.

The decision pathway is Expand — take the existing Iraq-Syria bilateral data-sharing MoU and extend it to include Turkey as a third party. The architecture exists. The mechanism was built between Iraq and Syria. Syria proposes; UNEP hosts the secretariat; Turkey joins under the explicit framing that data sharing is not rights adjudication. The principled pragmatist's move: find the architecture that already works and build outward from it, before the window closes.

(The flag: a transparency compact creates a shared data baseline. It does not change the physical fact that twenty-two dams have been built upstream. Even with perfect monitoring, Iraq's water deficit in severe drought years is not addressable through data: addressing it would require reducing Turkish reservoir retention, restructuring Syrian irrigation, improving Iraqi water use efficiency, or some combination — all of which require enforceable commitments no party is currently willing to make. The engineering diplomat can build the diagnostic instrument. They cannot compel the fever to break. The compact is necessary; it is not sufficient. And the populations most in need of the sufficient solution are not the populations with the standing to demand it.)

---

**Problem** — Turkey, Syria, and Iraq share the Tigris-Euphrates basin under incompatible legal frameworks and no functioning joint monitoring mechanism, making meaningful negotiation on flows impossible. The live question: can Syria's transitional government broker a trilateral hydrological transparency compact before the current water crisis hardens into entrenched positions?

**Stakeholders** — Syria's transitional water ministry (potential convener; middle riparian; diplomatically fresh; party to the existing Iraq bilateral MoU); Turkey (upstream dam operator; will join only if framed as monitoring, not constraint); Iraq (most affected; most urgency; least leverage; signed the Iraq-Syria MoU); downstream farming communities in all three countries (maximum stake, minimum voice); UNEP (potential neutral technical secretariat).

**Binding constraint** — Turkey's unwillingness to join any mechanism that could be used as evidence in a future water allocation dispute, combined with Syria's limited institutional bandwidth as a new transitional government. The compact is explicitly scoped to data and early-warning, not rights adjudication — designed to remove the first constraint. The second requires an external convener.

**Decision pathway** — Expand: take the existing Iraq-Syria bilateral data-sharing MoU (2025) and extend it to include Turkey as a third party. Syria formally proposes; UNEP hosts the independent technical secretariat.

**Tools** — Trilateral data-sharing protocol extending the Iraq-Syria MoU (converts three national river narratives into a jointly-verified fact; changes interaction from incompatible national claims to a common data object; changes coordination); real-time drought early-warning system (makes drought costs visible to Turkey before political escalation; generates the dataset on which future adjustment protocols can be built; changes learning and interaction); independent technical secretariat hosted by UNEP or Arab League (resolves technical disputes before they become diplomatic ones; changes coordination).

**Metrics** — Shared monitoring station coverage (percentage of key Euphrates-Tigris gauging stations providing real-time data to all three parties; method: UNEP annual audit); drought-trigger activation and response rate (triggers activated vs. releases modulated; method: joint committee log verified by secretariat); water use efficiency in all three countries (agricultural output per unit water; indexed against 2024 baseline; method: FAO assessments shared with secretariat).

**90-day commitment** — July 2026: Syria formally proposes to Turkey the extension of the Iraq-Syria MoU to a trilateral compact, explicitly scoped to monitoring. August 2026: technical working group convenes on data parameters. September 2026: UNEP agrees to host independent secretariat. October 2026: Compact signed in principle, with 12-month pilot at Jarabulus border station including Turkish upstream data from Atatürk Dam outfall.
