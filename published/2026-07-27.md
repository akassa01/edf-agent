## The Well They Already Built

On the morning of April 10, 2000, Óscar Olivera walked out of the Cochabamba prefecture building and stood before a crowd that had spent four months shutting down Bolivia's fourth-largest city. He was carrying a piece of paper. Aguas del Tunari, the private consortium that had held the water concession for Cochabamba's municipal system for less than five months, was withdrawing. The water law was being repealed. The government was canceling the contract. Olivera read the announcement aloud into the crowd and wept.

The Coordinadora de Defensa del Agua y de la Vida — the Coalition in Defense of Water and Life — had just won. But Cochabamba still did not have the water it needed.

The setup took less than a year. In September 1999, the Bolivian government awarded a forty-year concession for Cochabamba's municipal water utility (SEMAPA) to Aguas del Tunari, a consortium controlled by Bechtel's subsidiary International Water and Spain's Abengoa. The concession was one instrument of a World Bank structural adjustment package. Bolivia needed a $600 million loan; privatization of urban water utilities was a condition. The engineers were right that SEMAPA couldn't fund the Misicuni dam project — a tunnel and reservoir that would bring water from the Cochabamba valleys and significantly expand coverage — without private capital. That part of the analysis was accurate. The model had a different problem.

It erased the community wells.

Roughly forty percent of Cochabamba's 800,000 residents did not receive SEMAPA service. They had built their own water systems: community wells dug collectively, irrigation cooperatives managed through consensus, water committees (comités de agua) that allocated access during dry months through consensus. These systems existed because SEMAPA had never reached the peri-urban slopes, and they worked — imperfectly, unevenly, but on terms the communities controlled. When the economic model identified the forty percent as a "service gap to be filled," it was treating existing governance as absence of service. When Law 2029 was rushed through the Bolivian Congress in October 1999 to establish the legal basis of the concession, Article 50 extended Aguas del Tunari's territory of exclusivity to include "all water resources" within the concession area. In a single clause, the wells that communities had built with their own labor became private property they now technically owed royalties to use.

The Coordinadora understood this before most observers did. The protests that began in December 1999 were not simply about price increases, though the prices were shocking — some SEMAPA users saw bills reach twenty dollars a month, equivalent to a full month's minimum wage, with no improvement in service. They were about something harder to quantify: who decides who controls water. The Coordinadora's unusual breadth — factory workers' unions, farmers' cooperatives, professional associations, civic organizations — gave it a technical base alongside a moral one. Its lawyers identified Article 50 as the hinge. Its first demand was not "cancel the contract." It was: repeal Article 50.

The Scientist in this case is working from a model with a missing column. The World Bank's analysis was internally coherent and the SEMAPA data was accurate, but the baseline labeled "no service" was not no service. It was a different kind of service, organized on different principles. What cannot be measured does not appear in the model; what does not appear in the model does not appear in the contract.

The Humanist sees the wells as governance. In Cochabamba's peri-urban zones, the comités de agua were the institutions through which communities exercised a form of collective self-determination that formal politics did not offer. Criminalizing those wells — even if enforcement was unlikely — was experienced as expropriation of something that had never been for sale. The Coordinadora's humanist claim was that water governance was not a service delivery problem. It was a democracy problem.

The Engineer sees a genuine infrastructure dilemma that the Water War resolved politically but not technically. SEMAPA still could not fund Misicuni after the protests. The contract was gone but the problem remained. An engineering solution to Cochabamba's water scarcity required investment that the public utility could not self-finance. The camel — the option outside the apparent binary of full privatization or full public control — was technically available and was eventually enacted: Bolivia's 2004 Law 2066 distinguished between municipal networks and community-managed systems, creating a legal category that Law 2029 had refused.

Where is X in this case? It is Article 50 of Law 2029, and the specific moment in October 1999 when the Bolivian Congress passed it without meaningful debate. The provision was not an engineering necessity — Aguas del Tunari did not need jurisdiction over community wells to fund the Misicuni tunnel. It was a legal over-reach, a clause whose downstream effects were not modeled because the communities who depended on the wells were not at the table where the contract was written. Remove Article 50 and you substantially change the conflict. Keep it and the conflict cannot be resolved short of the company's withdrawal.

The 18th camel was a hybrid governance model: a regulated private operator responsible for SEMAPA's existing formal connections and all new Misicuni infrastructure, combined with legally recognized community management of existing independent water systems, with tiered pricing creating cross-subsidies. This is not a theoretical construction. It is what Bolivia built after the Water War, slowly, imperfectly, in Law 2066 and subsequent water governance reforms. The camel existed; it was simply not visible to anyone at the concession negotiation in September 1999, because no one at that table represented the communities for whom it would have been designed.

The Coordinadora did not take the camel when it was eventually offered in modified form. By December 1999, the coalition had hardened to full contract cancellation. When Victor Hugo Daza, seventeen years old, was shot in the face by a sharpshooter in civilian clothes on April 8, 2000, any remaining space for a modified offer closed. Four days later, the company left.

The counterfactual pathway — the one available at the decisive moment in September 1999 — was a Redesign of Article 50 before the contract was signed, in a process that included community water committee representatives on the drafting panel. Within ninety days of the concession agreement, a legal review commissioned by the water ministry would have found that the clause covering community wells was not necessary for the concession to function; a revised Article 50 could have been submitted to the World Bank before the law went to Congress. That specific redesign was available. It did not happen because community water committee leaders had no formal standing to demand it, and the World Bank's conditionality pressures created urgency around finalization.

*(The case leaves unresolved the deepest challenge it surfaces. When the humanist refusal to accept a modified offer is vindicated by the counterparty's exit, the 18th camel becomes retroactively unnecessary. But that vindication was contingent on Aguas del Tunari's decision to leave rather than fight — a decision the Coordinadora could not have known in advance. Engineering diplomacy can name the camel and design the hybrid model. It cannot tell you, in April 2000, whether your counterparty will negotiate or exit. When the outcome is vindicated by the other side's departure, the case teaches the hardest lesson: the decision under uncertainty was made correctly, and for reasons not fully available at the moment of decision.)*

---

**Problem** — How to structure a water concession that funds necessary infrastructure without extinguishing the community water governance that serves the population the municipal utility does not reach

**Stakeholders** — Peri-urban water committees (existing governance; no formal standing); SEMAPA customers (formal connections; facing rate increases); Aguas del Tunari (concession holder); World Bank (financing conditionality); Bolivian government (loan dependency); the Coordinadora (organized resistance; technical competence)

**Binding constraint** — World Bank loan conditionality tied the financing structure to privatization; without an alternative financing vehicle for Misicuni, modifying the concession reduced revenue projections the Bank required

**Decision pathway** — Redesign: amend Article 50 of Law 2029 before enactment to explicitly exempt existing community-built water systems, through a joint drafting process including community water committee representatives

**Tools** — Joint legal review of Article 50 language with community water committee representatives; community water system mapping (to enumerate and legally recognize cooperative structures); tiered pricing feasibility study (to demonstrate cross-subsidy viability without compromising Misicuni bankability)

**Metrics** — Number of community water systems legally exempted / total operating systems; SEMAPA formal connection expansion rate in peri-urban areas, measured quarterly; real water cost differential between community system users and SEMAPA formal customers, tracked annually

**90-day commitment** — Within 90 days of the September 1999 concession agreement: (1) water ministry commissions a legal opinion on whether Article 50 covers existing community wells, with Coordinadora representatives on the review panel; (2) joint working group with water committee leaders maps existing community systems; (3) revised Article 50 draft is submitted to the World Bank, explicitly exempting community-built systems

## Abu Simbel Was Saved

In November 1959, representatives of Egypt and Sudan sat down in Cairo to divide the Nile. The talks had been running for years, their backdrop the construction financing of the Aswan High Dam — a project that had become, after the United States and the World Bank withdrew their funding offer in 1956, a declaration of Egyptian sovereignty funded by the Soviet Union. The agreement they reached allocated 18.5 billion cubic meters of annual Nile flow to Egypt and 7.5 billion to Sudan, and it addressed, in a brief clause, the population transfer required by the dam's reservoir: Egypt and Sudan would settle compensation terms bilaterally. The clause was technically adequate and historically unprecedented in its indifference to the people it described.

No Nubian representative was in the room.

The territory that Lake Nasser would inundate was not simply a geographic zone along the southern Nile. It was Nubia — a civilization with continuous settlement documented to at least 3800 BCE, older than the Egyptian dynasties whose monuments would be the focus of the international rescue campaign that followed. Nubia had its own language families (Nobiin and Kenzi-Mattoki), its own architectural vocabulary — the decorated courtyard compounds that gave its villages their distinctive form — its own oral histories, its own agricultural system built around Nile silt cycles, date palm cultivation, and community construction practices specific to the valley's microclimate and soil. The 120,000 Nubians who would be displaced were not a population in transition. They were a civilization in place.

The dam's engineers had calculated that approximately 500 kilometers of Nile Valley would be inundated, destroying forty-four Egyptian Nubian villages and a larger number in Sudan. Their calculations were accurate. The parallel analysis of what the affected communities needed to remain viable — what soil conditions Nubian farming practices required, what social organizational units needed to be preserved in the new sites, what transition support a community of subsistence farmers moving to different land would require — was not undertaken. The Egyptian government commissioned soil surveys of Kom Ombo, the agricultural land fifty kilometers north of Aswan designated for Egyptian Nubians, and determined it suitable. Later analyses found soil quality in the northern areas of Kom Ombo substantially below the assessment. The surveys had measured chemistry. They had not asked who would farm the land, with what knowledge, starting from what condition.

Between October 1963 and June 1964, approximately 70,000 Egyptian Nubians were transported to Kom Ombo. Fifty thousand Sudanese Nubians were relocated to Khashm el Girba — the New Halfa project — 800 kilometers from their homeland. They had been given forty days' notice. Compensation was cash at assessed land value: a figure that captured the market price of agricultural productivity and nothing else. It did not capture the social value of water committees that had allocated irrigation access through centuries of reciprocal obligation. It did not capture what it means for a community to be a community.

While the communities were being relocated, something extraordinary was happening on the same river. UNESCO launched the International Campaign to Save the Monuments of Nubia in 1960. Over eight years, the campaign raised $80 million from fifty countries, mobilized archaeologists, engineers, and governments across the Cold War divide, and executed what remained for decades the largest artifact relocation in history. The Temple of Abu Simbel — four seated colossi of Ramesses II, sixty-five feet high, carved into sandstone cliff — was dismantled into 807 numbered blocks, moved sixty-five meters above its original position, and reassembled with millimeter precision on an artificial hill. Thirty-two other monuments were saved.

The Scientist sees both campaigns with the same eye. The monument campaign was technically ambitious because the technical requirements were clear, measurable, and verifiable: the temple must maintain structural integrity, spatial orientation, aesthetic proportions. Progress could be tracked against those specifications. The community relocation had no equivalent specification. No one had written down what a successful resettlement looked like in terms the affected communities would have recognized as success. The surveys measured soil chemistry and water table depths. They did not measure community cohesion, agricultural knowledge, or social unit integrity — not because those things are unmeasurable, but because measuring them would have required asking the communities what they needed. That question was never asked.

The Humanist sees a contrast that is not accidental. Monuments can be internationalized as cultural heritage. Living communities remain subjects of sovereign state authority. The temples were beautiful and irreplaceable. So were the communities. The difference is jurisdictional. UNESCO could mobilize for Abu Simbel because Egypt and Sudan invited it, because the temples were classified as heritage belonging to all humanity, because the campaign generated international prestige for the Egyptian government. The Nubian communities were Egyptian and Sudanese citizens. Their welfare was a domestic matter that neither international organization nor bilateral agreement was structured to contest. The category "internationally protected community welfare in development-induced displacement" did not exist in 1960. The absence of that category was not oversight. It was architecture.

The Engineer, looking at the resettlement as a logistics problem, finds it was solved as a logistics problem. Move the people. Build the houses. Pay the compensation. Provide the land. These tasks were completed. The logistical frame systematically excluded a different kind of question: what did these communities need to maintain their productive capacity at the new sites? Nubian agriculture was specialized for soil conditions, water table depths, and growing seasons specific to the Nile valley south of Aswan. Farmers being relocated to Kom Ombo's different soil and microclimate were not generic subsistence cultivators. They were specialists. No knowledge inventory was taken. No extension program matched community expertise to new conditions.

Where is X in this case? It is the November 1959 negotiating room in Cairo — not the dam, not the construction decisions, but the room where Egypt and Sudan addressed the population transfer in a brief clause without any provision for Nubian community participation in the planning. Everything downstream of that room was determined within a framework set there. The intervention point is not the decision to build the dam. It is the clause that said Egypt and Sudan would settle population transfer terms bilaterally, without specification of consultation, co-design, or community participation requirements.

The 18th camel is a co-design process for resettlement in which Nubian community leaders were principal authors of the relocation plan: mapping existing farming knowledge and social structures to determine site requirements, not just land area; preserving clan groupings (kabilas) in new site layouts rather than dispersing them across individual household assignments; establishing a knowledge-transition program matching Nubian farmers with agricultural extension workers familiar with the new land types. Partial versions of this camel were implemented in Sudan's New Halfa resettlement more than in Egypt's Kom Ombo. The more compact social organization of New Halfa communities produced better long-term cohesion outcomes than Kom Ombo's dispersed layouts. The camel existed and worked where it was partially tried.

The counterfactual pathway is an Expand: in the ninety days following the November 1959 Agreement, Egypt and Sudan jointly appoint twelve Nubian community leaders to an Advisory Commission with formal review rights over site plans. An agricultural knowledge survey is commissioned. Community-led spatial layout review begins. None of these actions requires delaying the dam. They require beginning the co-design process in 1960 rather than as an afterthought when the water is already rising.

*(Abu Simbel was saved. Principled pragmatism can mandate community co-design, require knowledge inventories, and insist on veto power for affected communities over resettlement plans. What it cannot do is resolve the prior question: why does stone reliably generate the international mobilization that living communities reliably do not? The answer is not ignorance. The answer is standing. Monuments can be internationalized as cultural heritage belonging to all humanity; living communities remain subjects of sovereign state authority accountable to no external body. As long as "internationally protected cultural heritage" applies to stone and not to the communities that gave that stone meaning, engineering diplomacy will be designing the camel for a game whose rules it did not set.)*

**Problem** — Egypt and Sudan must relocate 120,000 Nubians within a dam construction timeline, in a bilateral framework with no mechanism for community consent and an international legal environment that protects monuments but not displaced communities

**Stakeholders** — Nubian communities (displaced, no formal standing, no international advocate); Egyptian and Sudanese governments (sovereign authority; bilateral negotiating framework); UNESCO (mandate for cultural heritage, not community welfare; demonstrated organizational capacity); Soviet Union and international donors (financing interests; no human displacement mandate)

**Binding constraint** — The bilateral political framework excluded community representation as a legal category; construction timeline compressed co-design into days rather than months; Cold War non-intervention norms removed any international pressure point for Nubian community rights

**Decision pathway** — Expand: add Nubian community leaders as co-designers of the resettlement plan with formal review rights over site selection and community grouping, within the 90-day window following the November 1959 Agreement

**Tools** — Community social mapping (to preserve kabila groupings in new site layouts, changing land allocation from individual household to community unit); agricultural knowledge inventory (to document Nubian farming techniques and match them to candidate site conditions); participatory site selection (to give community leaders formal review power over sites whose soil and microclimate are incompatible with existing knowledge)

**Metrics** — Percentage of kabilas resettled with their primary social unit intact; agricultural yield per feddan at new sites relative to historical homeland yields, measured 5 years post-resettlement; community water access hours per household relative to Nile baseline, tracked annually for 5 years

**90-day commitment** — In the 90 days following the November 1959 Nile Waters Agreement: (1) Egypt and Sudan jointly appoint a twelve-member Nubian Advisory Commission with formal review rights over site plans; (2) commission an agricultural knowledge survey documenting Nubian farming methods and knowledge-transfer gaps for candidate sites; (3) present New Halfa and Kom Ombo site plans to the Advisory Commission for community-organized spatial layout review
