# Engineering Diplomacy — 2026-08-28

## The Aberfan Certainty

In March 1965, Glyn Owen, Area Surveyor for the National Coal Board's South Western Division, completed a survey of Tip No. 7 above the village of Aberfan, in the Taff Valley south of Merthyr Tydfil. The survey documented what Owen had suspected for some time: the tip had been constructed over a natural underground spring. In autumn and winter, when the water table rose, the spring saturated the base of the tip from below. Owen's report recommended drainage works to relieve the pressure. He submitted it up the NCB hierarchy. It was acknowledged. It was not acted upon.

Tip No. 7 stood 111 feet above the village. On the morning of October 21, 1966, the half-term holiday had just ended and children were filing into Pantglas Junior School at the foot of the slope. At 9:15 am, following heavy rain and a rapid spring rise, the tip liquefied and moved. In under two minutes, 140,000 tons of coal slurry descended on the school and on the houses surrounding it. Of the 144 people killed, 116 were children. One hundred and nine of them were in the same building.

The NCB's mining operations in South Wales had produced coal and men confident in coal for a century. The engineers who managed the tipping regime were competent, experienced, and wrong — not about the mechanical engineering of coal extraction but about what the word "stable" meant. In the NCB's operational vocabulary, a tip was stable if it was not visibly failing under current conditions. Stability was a binary, assessed in the present, and assessed by the same people responsible for keeping production moving. This was the doing mode at its most characteristic: run what is running, note concerns in the margin, keep the lorries turning.

What the doing mode had no room for was Owen's question, which was a different question entirely. Owen was not asking whether the tip was stable now. He was asking what happens when the spring rises this autumn. The answer to that question required a different kind of thinking — geotechnical, probabilistic, seasonal — and it required someone with standing to compel a response rather than file a recommendation. Owen had neither.

The Scientist looks at Aberfan and sees a knowledge-to-action gap. The data was in the system. Owen's report existed. The drainage concern was documented. What failed was not the generation of knowledge but the institutional mechanism for converting knowledge into mandatory action. In the absence of a binding classification — "this survey finding requires engineering-order response within 90 days" — the report became advice, and advice in a hierarchy drifts toward the bottom of the pile.

The Humanist looks at Aberfan and sees a community that raised the alarm and was not heard. The Merthyr Tydfil Borough Council had formally written to the NCB about tip safety in 1963 and again in 1965. Both letters were answered with reassurance. No mechanism existed for the council to escalate those concerns to HM Mines Inspectorate, because tips were classified as surface features, outside the Inspectorate's jurisdiction under the 1954 Mines and Quarries Act. The community's petitions were routed directly to the institution they were petitioning. This is not a communication failure. It is a structural one.

The Engineer looks at Aberfan and finds the 18th camel: the eastern escarpment of Merthyr Mountain. The Lord Justice Edmund Davies Tribunal, which investigated the disaster in 1967, confirmed that coal waste could have been tipped down the east face of the mountain, away from any inhabited valley, without disrupting production. The district engineer had considered this option and rejected it as "operationally inconvenient" — the haul distance was longer. The option was not infeasible. It was inconvenient, and inconvenience, in the NCB's operational calculus, was equivalent to infeasibility.

Where, precisely, was X? Owen's March 1965 survey report is the fulcrum. At that moment — a document in a filing system — the intervention was both cheap and sufficient. Had Owen's recommendation been classified as a mandatory engineering notice, it would have triggered escalation within the NCB hierarchy, a geotechnical review, and drainage works before the autumn of 1965. The tip would have been assessed as stable subject to drainage completion, and the spring rise in October 1966 would have found a drainage culvert, not a liquefaction event. Instead, the report sat between "advice" and "order," and that gap in classification is where 116 children were killed.

What was 1+1? The NCB's answer to "is the tip safe?" was: yes, stable by operational standard. The village's question was: will it come down on us? These are different questions with different answers, and no institutional mechanism existed to make that difference explicit. The NCB's reassurance was an answer to question one; the community heard it as an answer to question two. They were not lying when they reassured. They were answering a question the community was not asking.

The counterfactual pathway is a Redesign, not a revolution. The NCB already had an area engineering function. Owen already had authority to write survey reports. What was missing was a single procedural classification: a rule that survey findings above a certain risk threshold became mandatory engineering notices rather than discretionary recommendations, with escalation to HM Mines Inspectorate if not resolved within 90 days. This rule did not require new legislation. It required a divisional circular. The tool is not a new institution; it is a reclassification of existing documents that changes what they compel. Alongside it: a formal channel for community safety petitions to reach the Inspectorate independently of the operator being petitioned. And a mandate for third-party geotechnical audit of all active tips over inhabited valleys — not self-certified, not managed by the entity whose production schedule the survey might interrupt.

(The Tribunal found "ignorance combined with indifference" responsible for Aberfan. The parliamentary record subsequently showed that senior NCB officials had received reports flagging the drainage concern. The institution converted accessible knowledge into institutional ignorance by routing it through a hierarchy that lacked any mandatory escalation requirement. Principled pragmatism can redesign the inspection regime. It cannot make existing knowledge visible inside an organization with a structural incentive not to see it. The line between ignorance and cover-up is, in complex institutions, not always a line.)

---

**Problem** — How does a community compel a nationalized industrial operator to act on a documented geotechnical risk when the operator controls the inspection regime, the community has no independent standing, and the risk classification system converts survey recommendations into discretionary advice?

**Stakeholders** — Aberfan village (116 children and 28 adults killed; community with no institutional standing to compel action); NCB Area Surveyor Glyn Owen (documented the risk, had no escalation authority); Merthyr Tydfil Borough Council (petitioned twice, absorbed without response); HM Mines Inspectorate (jurisdictionally excluded from surface tips by statute); NCB divisional and senior management (operational confidence culture; received the surveys; held authority to act)

**Binding constraint** — No mandatory mechanism existed to convert survey findings into engineering orders; no external escalation channel allowed community petitions to bypass the operator being petitioned

**Decision pathway** — Redesign: reclassify Owen's 1965 drainage survey recommendation as a mandatory engineering notice; create a direct community-to-Inspectorate petition channel that bypasses NCB self-report; commission third-party geotechnical audit of all tips over inhabited valleys

**Tools** — Mandatory engineering notice: converts survey recommendation from advisory to binding, changing coordination between area survey and divisional engineering; community safety liaison: formal channel from Merthyr Tydfil Council to HM Mines Inspectorate, not routed through NCB management, changing interaction between community and external inspector; third-party geotechnical audit: adds independent expertise outside the operator's operational culture, changing learning

**Metrics** — Active tips with documented drainage concerns awaiting mandatory geotechnical review, measured quarterly by Inspectorate registry (not operator self-report); response time from community petition to formal inspection, measured in days via Inspectorate case files; completion rate of mandatory drainage works as percentage of outstanding notices, measured quarterly

**90-day commitment** — In the 90 days following March 1965: the NCB Area Engineer classifies Owen's drainage concern as a mandatory engineering notice; Merthyr Tydfil Council formally petitions HM Mines Inspectorate (not NCB); the Inspectorate opens a tips-inspection file; drainage works are commissioned by midsummer 1965, before the autumn spring rise

## The Sencer Memo

On March 24, 1976, David Sencer, director of the United States Centers for Disease Control, sent a memorandum to his superiors at the Department of Health, Education, and Welfare. The memo was four pages long and described four options for responding to an influenza outbreak at Fort Dix, New Jersey, where a novel H1N1 strain had killed one soldier, infected thirteen others, and spread through approximately five hundred recruits. The memo called every option by name — including "do nothing" — and then explained why inaction was the wrong choice. Sencer recommended a national mass vaccination program. President Ford announced it himself, at a press conference, the same afternoon.

The influenza strain at Fort Dix was antigenically related to the 1918 pandemic virus, which had killed at least fifty million people worldwide. Sencer was not operating from ignorance or from bureaucratic timidity in the other direction. He knew what the 1918 pandemic had done. He knew the seasonal window — if a pandemic strain was going to spread, it would need the autumn to do it. He knew that vaccine production required months of lead time. He had, genuinely, a narrow window in which to act, and he acted. That is the first thing the case requires saying.

The second thing is what Sencer knew and the public did not. At the advisory committee meetings in February and March 1976, CDC scientists discussed Guillain-Barré syndrome — a rare neurological disorder with a prior documented association with influenza vaccination. The committee concluded that the GBS risk was real but manageable, and that disclosing it to the public before vaccination would reduce uptake below the threshold needed for herd immunity. Congress was briefed on GBS. The forty-five million Americans who lined up for the vaccine were not.

The Fort Dix strain did not spread beyond the base. By October, the pandemic that Sencer's model had assigned a 0-to-10 percent probability never materialized. What materialized instead was GBS: approximately one case per one hundred thousand vaccinated, roughly eight times the background rate. In November, CBS News reported the cases. The program was halted on December 16, 1976. Sencer was fired by the incoming HEW Secretary, Joseph Califano, in early 1977, without public acknowledgment of the scientific difficulty of his position.

The Scientist at Sencer's side sees a probabilistic decision made correctly by the evidence available, which turned out to be wrong in its specific prediction. The 0-to-10 percent pandemic probability was a genuine range, not a confected one: the Fort Dix strain's community-spread potential could not have been known in March 1976. The GBS risk was real. The pandemic risk was real. Sencer chose the larger risk and got unlucky. That is, technically, what probability means.

The Humanist at Sencer's side sees something the probability calculation did not contain. It is 1976, four years after Tuskegee — after the revelation that the US Public Health Service had knowingly withheld treatment from Black men with syphilis for forty years in the name of science. The communities lining up for the swine flu vaccine did not arrive as a neutral population consenting to a calculated risk. Some of them arrived with forty years of reasons not to trust a federal health official who told them the shot was safe. The GBS revelation confirmed what those communities already suspected, and the trust that drained away did not drain only from the swine flu program. It drained from DTP. From MMR. The measles vaccine. The children who did not get vaccinated in the years that followed were not the target of the swine flu program. They were its compound interest.

The Engineer at Sencer's side asks: what was actually available to build, given the constraints? And here the case gets harder. Dr. Russell Alexander of the University of Washington proposed a sentinel program — vaccinate the over-65 population and healthcare workers first, include full GBS disclosure in consent materials, monitor for adverse events for six weeks, and expand to the general population only if a community-spread threshold was crossed. The advisory committee rejected this approach on production grounds: influenza vaccine manufacturing in 1976 required a single committed production run. A staged program that might be cancelled partway through was economically unattractive to manufacturers, and the government had no procurement mechanism to hedge it. This was a real constraint, not a rationalization. But it was a constraint the committee accepted as given rather than negotiated around.

Where was X? Not in the Sencer memo itself, which was scientifically defensible. The fulcrum was the meeting, probably a morning's deliberation in February or March 1976, where the GBS-disclosure question was resolved: Congress but not the public. That decision converted a manageable informed-consent challenge — one page in a consent form — into a trust-destroying revelation when the GBS cases surfaced publicly. The intervention was cheap; the decision not to intervene was consequential in proportion to the trust it withdrew from people who had not been asked.

What was 1+1? Sencer was answering: what must we do to protect two hundred million Americans from a possible 1918-scale pandemic? The communities receiving the vaccine were implicitly asking: what risk am I being asked to accept, and who decided I should accept it? The program answered the first question with rigor and the second not at all — until CBS News answered it on their behalf. These were not the same question, and treating them as the same question is what principled pragmatism looks like when it optimizes for the population at the expense of the individual standing in the line.

The 18th camel was Alexander's staged program: not "mass campaign" and not "do nothing," but a phased structure with a decision point, informed consent built in, and real-time adverse-event monitoring. It required renegotiating the production contract — which the government had the leverage to do, had it tried. It required being honest with the public about a small risk, which the advisory committee had the authority to do and chose not to. The 18th camel was available. It was not taken.

The pathway is a Redesign of the Sencer memo with two modifications: a staged monitoring protocol, beginning with high-risk populations and requiring a pre-specified community-spread threshold before general rollout; and mandatory GBS risk disclosure language on all vaccination consent materials. The first modification changes coordination between scientific advisory and political decision — it makes "expand or halt" a scientific question answered by data, not a political question answered by pressure. The second changes interaction between vaccinators and patients: it treats the person in the line as someone with standing to know the risk they are accepting.

(In public health, the cost of a false alarm is not only measured in adverse events. It is measured in compound trust debt — the reduction in subsequent vaccination uptake that extends for years across programs the false alarm never touched. Principled pragmatism can design a better decision framework for conditions of uncertainty. It cannot make a false positive cost-free, because the trust cost is not yours to control once the damage is done. Sencer's scientific judgment was defensible. The decision not to disclose was a values judgment, and it was made without the people whose values were at stake in the room.)

---

**Problem** — How should a public health authority design a mass vaccination program for a potential pandemic under genuine uncertainty, when the cost of inaction and the cost of action are incommensurable, and when full transparency about rare adverse event risks may undermine the program's effectiveness?

**Stakeholders** — CDC Director David Sencer (bore the scientific and institutional cost of a defensible call that went wrong); 45 million vaccinated Americans (enrolled without GBS risk disclosure); ~450 GBS patients (paid the adverse-event cost); Black American communities (for whom the 1972 Tuskegee revelation made institutional reassurance structurally suspect); childhood vaccine programs (bore the downstream trust-debt cost for a decade); vaccine manufacturers (held production-economics leverage that shaped the program's binary structure)

**Binding constraint** — Vaccine production economics forced a binary commitment: a staged program with a cancel option was not commercially viable under 1976 procurement arrangements; this binary was accepted as given rather than renegotiated

**Decision pathway** — Redesign: two modifications to the Sencer memo — (1) staged monitoring protocol with a pre-specified community-spread decision point before mass rollout; (2) mandatory GBS risk disclosure on all consent materials, treating the vaccinated person as a participant with standing rather than a unit of coverage

**Tools** — Pre-specified decision rule: a community-spread threshold triggers or halts expansion from sentinel to mass campaign, changing coordination between scientific advisory and political decision; informed consent form with GBS disclosure: changes interaction between vaccinator and patient, preserving trust through transparency; real-time adverse-event sentinel surveillance: weekly GBS incidence tracking through state health departments, changing learning by detecting the signal early enough to allow program modification

**Metrics** — GBS incidence per 100,000 vaccinated, measured weekly through sentinel surveillance, threshold 3x background rate triggering mandatory advisory review; DTP and MMR completion rates in pediatric practice, tracked annually as a measure of downstream trust-debt, baseline 1974–75; time from first adverse-event cluster detection to public disclosure, measured in days

**90-day commitment** — In the 90 days following March 24, 1976: DHEW and CDC commission a one-page GBS risk disclosure for all vaccination materials; negotiate a staged production contract or government indemnity for a conditional run; set July 1 as the first decision point; begin vaccinating over-65s and healthcare workers with full GBS disclosure and weekly surveillance; convene an independent adverse-event monitoring committee with authority to recommend program modification
