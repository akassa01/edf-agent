# Engineering Diplomacy — 2026-08-18

## The Compassion Trap: Haiti and the Architecture That Feeling Built

On January 14, 2010, Prime Minister Jean-Max Bellerive stood at the edge of the Port-au-Prince airport and watched the C-130s land. Two days had passed since the earthquake. The United States military had mobilized in under 48 hours—22,000 personnel, dozens of aircraft, the USS Carl Vinson offshore. What was being assembled was extraordinary. Bellerive later said that what troubled him was not the aircraft. It was the question he could not get anyone to answer: who is in charge?

The humanitarian system had come to Haiti at scale, and it had come fast, because the images from Port-au-Prince made any other response morally inconceivable. That is not a criticism. The feeling was real. The earthquake had killed an estimated 220,000 people, displaced 1.5 million, and destroyed the physical infrastructure of the Haitian state—the National Palace, Parliament, multiple ministries, the buildings in which governance had, however imperfectly, occurred. The people arriving with aircraft and supply chains and medical teams were responding to something terrible, and their urgency was proportionate to the scale of the suffering.

What that urgency had no room for was the architecture of the response itself.

The scientific reading is cold. By January 2010, after-action reports from the Indian Ocean tsunami and the Pakistan earthquake had documented, in detail, what happens when hundreds of organizations arrive simultaneously without coordination: duplication in visible areas, gaps in unreachable ones, commodity aid flooding that disrupts local markets, and a systematic erosion of host-government capacity that outlasts the emergency. The evidence base was not thin. Less than one percent of the approximately two billion dollars raised for the response flowed directly through Haitian government institutions. Rice prices in Port-au-Prince rose despite the arrival of food aid, consistent with documented patterns of uncoordinated commodity distribution. The data was repeating what earlier data had already said. The knowledge was not missing. The architecture that would have applied it was.

The humanist reading is harder. For Haitian communities, the earthquake destroyed not just 300,000 lives but the thin institutions through which Haitians exercised any collective agency over their own circumstances. What followed was ten thousand NGOs operating with their own supply chains, their own accountability structures, and their own definitions of need. Haitians who had survived—who knew their neighborhoods, their networks, their priorities—found themselves the passive recipients of decisions made in Geneva and New York. In December 2010, Haitian members of the Interim Haiti Recovery Commission wrote to its co-chairs, Bill Clinton and Prime Minister Bellerive, complaining that they had "time neither to read, nor analyze, nor understand—and much less to respond intelligently" to projects submitted under their nominal authority. The commission had been designed as the mechanism for Haitian co-leadership of recovery. It was not a coordination body. It was a bureaucratic structure surrounding a gap.

The engineer's reading asks: what would it have taken to make the coordination binding rather than voluntary? The UN cluster system existed. Eleven sector clusters—shelter, water, health, food—had the Haitian Ministry of Social Affairs listed as government co-chair. The system had been codified after the 2006 IASC reforms. What it lacked was enforcement: an organization that attended cluster meetings and then operated outside the agreed framework faced no sanction. The binding authority that would have connected international resources to Haitian decision-making was structurally absent, and no one in the first 72 hours had the authority or the incentive to demand it.

Where is X? In the hours of January 14 and 15, when the coordination architecture was still formable. The US military's Operation Unified Response established a logistics hub and operational command at the airport before any agreement had been reached with the Haitian government about how that structure related to Haitian authority. When the IHRC was later formed, months later, it was designed to overlay a de facto structure that had already hardened. What happened on January 14 was not a decision anyone made. It was a decision that was never made, and its absence was the decision.

The 1+1 problem was this: international actors answered "how fast can we get help to Haiti?" The Haitian government answered "who authorizes what happens here?" These are not the same question, and they never connected. The faster the answer to the first question was driven, the more the second became unaskable. Any coordinator who raised the co-authority question was perceived as prioritizing bureaucratic process over the lives of people in the rubble.

This is the urgency-actionability trap at its most complete. The more urgent the feeling—and images from Port-au-Prince made the urgency global within hours—the less politically possible coordination became. Any coordination delay was morally equivalent, in the media environment of January 2010, to a choice to let people die. The humanist mode had captured the global response so completely that the doing of coordination felt like its opposite.

The 18th camel was not a new invention. It was the cluster system with enforcement, pre-negotiated rather than improvised under fire: a bilateral surge framework agreed between major donors and the Haitian government before any disaster, specifying that in the event of a major emergency, Haitian government co-authority over resource allocation would be structurally binding from day one, not nominal. A market-monitoring desk tracking aid's impact on local food prices. Mandatory INGO registration with the Ministry of Interior within 72 hours of arrival. This architecture was theoretically available. It was practically unbuilt. No one had invested in building it when the cameras were off.

What Bellerive could have done—on January 15, with the US military landing and the INGO convoys assembling—was demand a coordination summit within 24 hours as a condition of Haitian cooperation. Not to slow the response. To set its architecture. One meeting, one framework, one answer to "who is in charge"—before the hardening of a de facto structure that would take three years and a global report to partially reform.

He did not do this, and the political environment of January 2010 made it nearly impossible that he would. The prime minister of a country where bodies were still being pulled from rubble could not appear to obstruct the arriving help. This is the flag: principled pragmatism gives Bellerive the right move, but it cannot give him the political standing to make it. The architecture that would have made the response work had to be built before the earthquake, by donors willing to invest in prevention rather than response—and that investment is never funded by the same people who fund the televised emergency.

(The limitation this episode reveals is one principled pragmatism cannot resolve: the structural misalignment between where humanitarian funding flows—toward visible crises—and where it is needed to prevent the coordination failure that crises reproduce. The pre-agreed framework requires donors to fund the unglamorous work of bilateral capacity-building in countries they will only pay attention to when something terrible happens there. No amount of coordination wisdom changes that incentive structure.)

---

**Problem** — How to structure an international disaster response so that communities retain meaningful agency over their own recovery when humanitarian urgency makes any coordination delay politically toxic.

**Stakeholders** — Haitian communities bearing the cost of aid-induced market distortion and institutional erosion; Haitian government ministries with formal authority and no binding resources; INGOs with operational capacity and accountability to their own donors; bilateral donor governments answering to domestic publics; UN coordination bodies with procedural authority and no enforcement power.

**Binding constraint** — Donor accountability to domestic publics, combined with the political toxicity of any coordination delay, made routing resources through Haitian government institutions politically unacceptable. This was a political constraint, not a technical one, and it required a political solution built before the disaster began.

**Decision pathway** — Redesign: pre-negotiate the surge framework so that Haitian co-authority is structurally binding from day one of any major response, removing the need to establish the architecture under fire.

**Tools** — Pre-negotiated bilateral surge framework (Haitian co-authority built into trigger documents); mandatory INGO registration within 72 hours of disaster declaration; market-monitoring desk embedded in OCHA coordination from day one; ring-fenced funding stream (minimum 25%) for Haitian-led organizations.

**Metrics** — Percentage of disaster funding flowing through or co-authorized by the host government (target: ≥20% within six months); cluster compliance rate (≥80% of operating organizations within 90 days); local food price index versus pre-disaster baseline (alert if staples deviate >25%).

**90-day commitment** — Within the first 72 hours after January 12: Bellerive convenes a coordination summit establishing Haitian co-authority over resource allocation; the US military logistics hub is formalized as a service provider to that framework; a market desk is embedded in the OCHA cell; all arriving INGOs register with the Ministry of Interior as a condition of operating. (Counterfactual—available January 14, 2010, had the political will been supported by a pre-negotiated framework.)

## The Legitimacy Gap: Ebola, Armed Escorts, and the Limits of Proven Tools

On the morning of October 18, 2018, the Ebola response coordinators in Butembo, North Kivu, arrived at a vaccination site to find it had been burned overnight. It was the fourth such attack in a month. Dr. Peter Salama, the WHO official overseeing the response, described the situation publicly that week as "a perfect storm"—active armed conflict, community resistance, and an accelerating outbreak, all simultaneously. He was not wrong about the storm. He was still working out whether the response had contributed to its direction.

The tools available to the 2018 DRC Ebola response were the best in the disease's history. The rVSV-ZEBOV vaccine had demonstrated efficacy above 97% in ring vaccination trials conducted in the 2014-2016 West Africa outbreak. Contact-tracing protocols were mature. Oral rehydration therapy and experimental therapeutics were available. Biomedically, everything was in place. What the biomedical model did not include was a column for whether communities in eastern DRC had any reason to accept the tools being offered by the people arriving with them.

They did not, or at least not straightforwardly. North Kivu had been under active armed conflict for more than twenty years. Communities in Butembo, Beni, and surrounding areas had accumulated experience—not paranoia, experience—that outsiders arriving with government connections and institutional resources might be protection or might be threat, and that the difference was not reliably predictable. When the Allied Democratic Forces targeted health facilities specifically, that was not incidental violence: it was deliberate exploitation of the association between vaccination teams and government authority. When WHO and the DRC Ministry of Health began attaching Armed Forces of the DRC to vaccination teams to protect health workers who were being killed, the humanitarian logic was impeccable. The operational logic was working against itself.

The scientist's reading is discomfiting. The scientific consensus on effective vaccination delivery in high-mistrust contexts was well-established. After-action reports from the 2014-2016 West Africa outbreak had identified community trust as a central variable—not a soft consideration but a determinant of whether the hard epidemiological work could proceed at all. Vaccine acceptance at first offer in North Kivu was substantially below rates in non-conflict settings, and the drivers were documented: rumors that vaccines caused sterilization, that they were experimental tools for foreign research, that the outbreak itself had been manufactured for political purposes. These were not random superstitions. They were rational inferences from the community's history of being the object of external actors' agendas. The scientific model knew all of this. It proceeded as if knowing it were the same as integrating it.

The humanist's reading does not begin with the health workers. It begins with the community member who watched vaccination teams arrive with military escorts for the third time and concluded, correctly, that whatever these people were offering, it came with the soldiers. In North Kivu, soldiers are not reliably associated with protection. They are associated with a conflict that has never been explained to the community's satisfaction. The meaning of "help" in that environment cannot be assumed. It must be constructed, through relationship, over time, by people the community has reason to trust. Between August 2018 and November 2019, 386 attacks on health workers and health infrastructure were recorded: 7 dead, 77 injured. Each attack was both cause and consequence of a trust deficit that was not created by the Ebola response and was not fixable by it.

The engineer's reading asks the hardest question: was there actually a binary? The trade-off as it was posed was genuine—protect health workers and lose community trust, or remove protection and risk losing health workers. But this was two decisions that had been treated as one, and they were separable. The delivery mechanism (armed escort) and the tool (vaccination) had been bundled without explicit analysis of whether they were compatible. They were not, in this environment, and the incompatibility was knowable before August 2018. The question of how to deliver the tool in a context where the usual delivery mechanism is a trust-destroyer is an engineering question with a different answer than the question of whether the tool works.

Where is X? The architecture decisions of early August 2018, in the weeks after the outbreak was declared on August 1. In that window, vaccination teams were deployed with standard response protocols into communities that had not yet received community dialogue, trust-mapping, or engagement by local leaders—people who belonged to the neighborhood rather than arriving from outside it. The sequence was: deploy the tool, hope trust follows. The alternative sequence—dialogue first, biomedical contact second—was available and not chosen. The decision in August 2018 was made under time pressure, which is real; but the time pressure was partly a product of the fact that the engagement infrastructure had not been built during the years between outbreaks. X is not the attack in October. X is the absence of a community engagement phase in August.

The 1+1 problem: the response asked "how do we vaccinate the ring contact list and protect health workers?" The community asked "who are these armed people and what do they want?" These were not the same question, and neither party could fully hear the other's. The vaccination program was scientifically sound. It was socially operating in a different problem than the one it was designed to solve.

The 18th camel was not an exotic invention. It was what the literature on community-centered outbreak response had already described: vaccination teams recruited from the neighborhood itself—local religious leaders, teachers, trusted traders—operating from familiar spaces, introduced to the community by people they already knew, with security provided through community social networks rather than armed forces. This model was eventually piloted in limited form in later phases of the DRC response; the lesson is that it needed to be the primary design from day one, not a recovery tool after trust had already been destroyed by the standard approach.

The pathway that was available in August 2018 was a 30-day sequence: a two-week community mapping and dialogue phase led by local leaders and community health workers, with no biomedical contact; followed by recruitment and training of community-embedded vaccination team members; followed by first ring vaccination conducted through those members with community social protection rather than military escort. The epidemiological cost of 30 days would have been real. It would have been smaller than the epidemiological cost of 22 months.

(The limitation this episode reveals is one that no response architecture can fully address. When a state has lost all meaningful legitimacy with a community through decades of conflict, exploitation, and broken promises, public health interventions that would be accepted anywhere else require a legitimacy reconstruction that is slower than most outbreaks allow. The 18th camel—community-embedded delivery—reduces the gap between the tool's efficacy and its social accessibility. It cannot close it entirely, and it cannot be constructed in the weeks after an outbreak starts. It requires investment in community trust during the years between outbreaks. Principled pragmatism gives the right design principle. It cannot manufacture the decade of relationship-building that makes the design work when it is needed.)

---

**Problem** — How to deliver a proven biomedical tool in a conflict-affected community when the delivery mechanism signals threat rather than care, and when pausing delivery has immediate epidemiological costs.

**Stakeholders** — Communities in North Kivu bearing the combined burden of armed conflict and an Ebola outbreak, whose mistrust of external actors is historically grounded; health workers at physical risk from attacks; WHO and DRC Ministry of Health facing contradictory mandates (speed, safety, trust); international donors expecting measurable epidemiological progress; armed groups deliberately exploiting the response's associations with government authority.

**Binding constraint** — The response architecture and the community trust environment are not independent variables: deploying the tool through a mechanism that signals threat makes the tool inaccessible, and the speed demanded by the epidemiological timeline makes the trust-building alternative appear too slow. The constraint is partly self-generated by the sequence of deployment decisions.

**Decision pathway** — Redesign: lead with community dialogue and community-embedded team building before biomedical deployment, treating community trust as an operational prerequisite rather than a downstream communication task.

**Tools** — Community-embedded vaccination teams (local figures as primary vaccinators in their own neighborhoods); pre-deployment community dialogue (minimum two weeks before any biomedical contact); community trust monitoring dashboard tracking refusal rates and rumor prevalence; social protection networks replacing armed escorts.

**Metrics** — Vaccination acceptance rate at first offer (target: ≥80% in community-embedded model); time from community dialogue initiation to first vaccination (minimum 14-day dialogue period); attack incidents per 100 vaccination team contact-days (leading indicator of trust deterioration).

**90-day commitment** — From August 1, 2018: (1) a community mapping exercise identifies trusted local networks in each affected commune within 7 days; (2) a 2-week community dialogue phase, led by local religious and civic leaders, precedes any biomedical activity; (3) community-identified members are recruited and trained as vaccination team co-leads; (4) armed escort is withdrawn as the primary security mechanism in areas where community social protection networks can be activated. First ring vaccination under the redesigned architecture begins day 30, not day 1.
