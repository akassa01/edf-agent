# The Unilateral Truth

In January 2012, Peter Gleick sat at his desk in Oakland, California, and composed an email. He addressed it to the Heartland Institute in Chicago, signed it with someone else's name, and asked that internal documents be forwarded to him for review. He was impersonating a Heartland board member. He had a MacArthur Fellowship, a decade as founder of the Pacific Institute, a reputation as one of the country's leading voices on freshwater and climate, and a chairmanship on the American Geophysical Union's task force on scientific ethics. He was also, in this moment, committing fraud.

Gleick later explained that his judgment had been clouded by "frustration with the ongoing efforts — often anonymous, well-funded and coordinated — to attack climate science and scientists." The frustration was real. The denial machine had spent fifteen years targeting scientists: public records requests for private emails, coordinated harassment, systematic attack on peer review. Gleick had watched colleagues become collateral damage. He had, reportedly, also received an anonymous document claiming to describe Heartland's climate strategy — its donor network, its plans to prevent schoolchildren from learning established climate science, its funding of contrarian researchers. He wanted to verify it.

Heartland sent him what he asked for, and he forwarded the documents to a set of environmental journalists. The documents that were genuine were damning: a donor list, a budget of roughly $4.6 million annually, a detailed education strategy for 2012. One document — the "Confidential Memo: 2012 Heartland Climate Strategy" — was disputed as a forgery, possibly fabricated by whoever sent the anonymous tip that set the whole sequence in motion. Heartland called it a fraud. Gleick said he did not write it. Its origin was never conclusively established.

The contested memo became the story. The genuine documents, on their own damaging, became the exhibit in a trial about method rather than about Heartland. Gleick resigned from the ethics task force. He took leave from the Pacific Institute. He issued a public apology. The denial machine's funders did not withdraw. Its operations continued. The narrative it had been building for years — climate scientists are dishonest actors pursuing an agenda — acquired a case study.

The scientist's reading is clinical: the epistemic situation was not what Gleick thought it was. He did not need the documents to make the case against Heartland; the public record already documented the institution's activities in sufficient detail. Form 990 filings had revealed donor patterns. DeSmogBlog had been building a registry of denial funding since 2006. Inside Climate News had published prize-winning investigations on the industry. The documents were confirmation, not revelation. The decision to obtain them by fraud was not forced by a gap in the evidence; it was driven by a sense of urgency that the evidence, evaluated coolly, did not support.

The humanist reading understands how he got there. Gleick was carrying the moral weight of civilization-scale consequences — decades of preventable warming, coastal communities already losing ground, species already committed to extinction. Against that weight, the methodological scruple of not impersonating someone felt like a formality. The moral arithmetic had simplified: the problem was large enough that any action serving the cause was at least arguable. The urgency had compressed the question "how should I act?" into a single axis: "how bad is the problem?" The more certain the answer to the second question, the more the first question seemed already answered. He stopped asking what he was risking, and who bore the cost of that risk.

The engineer sees what was there and was not used. The institutional infrastructure for exactly the investigation Gleick wanted to run existed and was active. Investigative journalists with legal protection and source-protection track records were working the denial-funding story already. FOIA requests had produced partial results. The organic 990 data was publicly available. Inside Climate News would, three years later, publish the Pulitzer-winning "Exxon Knew" investigation — evidence that its investigative capacity was more than theoretical. Gleick had, by virtue of his institutional standing, access to editorial relationships most scientists lacked. He could have walked into an editor's office, shared what he knew from public sources alone, and let the journalism do what journalism is designed to do. He chose instead to do it himself, faster, because the machinery in place felt slower than the problem was urgent.

Shortcuts through institutional frameworks typically exit somewhere you did not intend to be. Gleick arrived at a place where the content of the documents mattered less than how they were obtained. The principled pragmatist, entering that situation, asks the prior question: where is X? X is not Heartland's donor list. X is the moment when Gleick redefined his role from expert witness — a role at which he excelled, a role that could not be revoked — to investigator, a role for which he had no institutional standing and no legal protection. That moment was the email.

The 18th camel was not a compromise between "expose Heartland now" and "do nothing." It was a third thing: brief an editor at Inside Climate News with everything Gleick knew from public sources, let the journalist pursue the documents using methods the law protects, and position Gleick as the expert commentator for when the investigation breaks. The exposure happens. Gleick's name is source, not suspect. His credibility — a collective resource, not merely a personal one — is intact for the next battle, and the one after.

Would he have given that advice to a graduate student? Almost certainly. The Solomon Paradox applies with particular force here: Gleick was, at the moment he composed the email, the chair of the task force on scientific ethics. The wisdom he would have offered someone else was available. It was not directed inward.

(The flag: the asymmetry of moral damage is not correctable after the fact. When a scientist's credibility is the instrument of their advocacy, fraud committed in service of a just cause damages the instrument without touching the target. Heartland's funders did not withdraw; its narratives survived. Gleick's standing as an independent expert voice was compromised in precisely the arena — policy debate — where it had most value. Principled pragmatism cannot repair this asymmetry, because the moment the instrument and the action became entangled, every future use of the instrument carried the history of that January email.)

---

**Problem** — A credible scientist, facing a coordinated institutional denial campaign with civilization-scale consequences, opts for fraud to verify and expose internal documents rather than using the legal investigative infrastructure available to him.

**Stakeholders** — Gleick (credibility at stake); the climate science community (whose collective standing was implicated in his individual act); Heartland Institute funders (whose operations continued unchanged); Inside Climate News, DeSmogBlog, and investigative journalists (who had the capacity and mandate to do what Gleick did, legally); policymakers who relied on independent expert voices to evaluate contested claims.

**Binding constraint** — Gleick's subjective assessment that legal channels would arrive too late to prevent the distribution of Heartland's 2012 K-12 curriculum materials; an assessment that, in retrospect, overstated the imminence of the harm.

**Decision pathway** — Reframe: from "how do I expose Heartland before the curriculum is distributed" to "how do I maintain the independent expert standing that makes my scientific voice politically actionable, while contributing to public understanding of how climate denial is institutionally organized."

**Tools** — (1) Investigative journalism briefing: share specific, documented knowledge of Heartland's structure with Inside Climate News under source protection, letting the journalist pursue documents using legal methods — changes coordination by distributing the investigation across a network with legal standing. (2) Expert witness repositioning: Congressional testimony on organized denial funding using verified public sources — changes interaction by placing the denial structure on the legislative record in a forum where scientific credibility is relevant. (3) Targeted public writing: write precisely about Heartland's known activities using only verifiable sources, establishing a documented record without implicating Gleick in any acquisition method.

**Metrics** — (1) Donor disclosure: number of Heartland anonymous donors identified through legal channels within 12 months. (2) Curriculum accuracy: percentage of K-12 climate curricula modified following public scrutiny. (3) Expert standing: Gleick's Congressional testimony invitations per year as a proxy for intact independent credibility.

**90-day commitment** — January 2012: Gleick contacts Inside Climate News editor to share structured knowledge of Heartland's composition and activities from public sources. Weeks two through four: ICN investigates using legal methods; Gleick prepares Senate testimony request. Week six: joint publication — ICN's investigation, Gleick's expert commentary — with Gleick as source, not suspect. Week twelve: Gleick testifies, standing intact, the Heartland funding structure documented through two mutually reinforcing channels.

# The Safe Area

On the evening of July 11, 1995, somewhere in the complex of buildings near Potočari, Bosnia-Herzegovina, a camera captured Lieutenant Colonel Thom Karremans raising a glass of brandy in a toast with Ratko Mladic. Karremans commanded Dutchbat III, the Dutch battalion tasked with protecting the Srebrenica "safe area." Mladic commanded the Army of Republika Srpska. Srebrenica had fallen that afternoon. The video would become one of the most viewed documents of the entire investigation: a peacekeeping commander, in uniform, accepting a drink from the general who was at that moment organizing the separation and murder of approximately 8,373 men and boys. Karremans did not know the final number. He likely knew enough.

The Srebrenica safe area had been created by UN Security Council Resolution 819 in April 1993. It designated the enclave — a pocket of Bosnian Muslim civilians in Serb-controlled territory — as a demilitarized zone under UN protection. It said nothing about what the UN would do if the protection were challenged by a force it could not meet. Dutchbat III had approximately 400 lightly armed soldiers. Serb forces numbered approximately 15,000, with armor. The mandate said protect. The force structure said comply.

What the Dutch battalion actually did in the days that followed has been documented across 7,000 pages of NIOD investigation, parliamentary inquiry, trial testimony, and legal proceedings that resulted in the Dutch Supreme Court finding the state 10 percent liable for roughly 350 deaths specifically associated with Dutch operational decisions at the compound. Three Dutchbat soldiers testified for the prosecution. They described the panic, the heat, the 40,000 people crammed into a space designed for a fraction of that number, the separation of men from women at the compound gate, the removal of girls from the crowd, the killings they witnessed from positions they had been ordered to hold. In 2002, the NIOD report's publication triggered the resignation of the entire Dutch government.

The scientist's reading begins with what Karremans knew, and when. The NIOD established that Dutch troops had observed Serb encirclement for weeks. Requests for NATO air support had been sent and denied — not because no air capacity existed, but because the dual-key authorization structure, which required both the UN Secretary-General's representative and NATO to agree before a strike, had been functionally paralyzed since May 1995, when 450 UN peacekeepers across Bosnia had been taken hostage after an earlier series of strikes. Clinton and his national security adviser had become disillusioned with air power as a deterrent tool precisely in the weeks before Srebrenica fell. The scientist sees a command architecture designed for a deterrence logic that had already failed. Karremans was not misled by false data; he was operating in a system that had no protocol for the condition it was in.

The humanist reading begins with the 40,000 people. They had lived alongside Dutch troops for three years. The "safe area" designation had become a reason not to leave: if the UN is here, why go? The label replaced individual flight judgment with collective institutional trust. When the men were loaded onto buses on July 12 — told they were being taken to Muslim-held territory for prisoner exchange — the guarantee that had kept them there had already been converted into a mechanism for their assembly. The felt experience at Potočari was processed by Dutch soldiers primarily through the operational framework: these are civilians in our area of operations, the Serbs are the armed party, we have no authorization to use force. The framework had a category for their feelings. The category was not "moral emergency requiring individual defiance of orders." The humanist reading asks what it means to design a protection mandate that gives soldiers a category for what they see that is not that.

The engineer's reading goes further upstream. The safe area concept was an engineering solution to a political problem: create a protected zone without resolving the political conflict that made protection necessary. Output specification: protect civilians. Input specification: no force. These are incompatible. Karremans inherited a machine designed to fail under load. The engineer's reading of his available moves, given that load, is unsentimental: escalate to Dutch political leadership directly, bypassing the frozen UNPROFOR chain of command that had already demonstrated it could not produce air authorization in time. Formally declare — in writing, by name, to the Dutch Defense Minister — that the safe area mandate was incoherent and that Dutchbat's only viable remaining objective was evacuation and documentation. Position Dutch soldiers at the compound gate as documented witnesses, not facilitators: no assistance in directing men toward buses, no cooperation in clearing the perimeter, visible presence as a record that would not be suppressible later. None of this requires firing a weapon. None of it was tried.

Where is X? The compound gate on the morning of July 12, where Dutch soldiers helped separate Bosnian men from their families. Before that moment, there had been failures of omission — inadequate urgency in the air-strike requests, failure to escalate directly upward, failure to declare the mandate's incoherence formally. At the gate, the failures became a commission. X is the moment operational compliance tipped into operational complicity.

The 18th camel was not a military victory. With 400 soldiers against 15,000, the binary of "fight" versus "comply" was not the real binary. The third thing was this: declare the mandate's failure on the record, refuse operational assistance to the separation, escalate to political authority at the level where the decision about the mandate could actually be made — The Hague, not Sarajevo. The men outside the compound perimeter were beyond any reach Dutchbat had. The men inside the compound were not. The Dutch state's legal liability attaches to those specific deaths because those specific decisions were within Dutchbat's operational authority. X was located exactly there.

The NIOD noted, in its assessment, that Dutch military analysts had previously applied analogous reasoning to other countries' deployments — had flagged the safe area concept's incoherence in theoretical terms — and had not applied that reasoning to their own. Expertise had been directed outward. Proximity narrowed the vision. The Solomon Paradox has institutional forms.

(The flag: the safe area designation itself was the problem. By placing a UN guarantee over Srebrenica, the Security Council signaled safety it could not provide and, in doing so, prevented the individual flight that might have scattered 40,000 people across terrain that was harder to control than a single enclave. Mladic's operation executed in the knowledge that the guarantee was false. Principled pragmatism can identify the 18th camel available at the battalion level. It cannot redesign a mandate that was incoherent at the level of its creation. The problem was not solved at Potočari. It was made in New York.)

---

**Problem** — A UN peacekeeping battalion, mandated to protect an enclave of 40,000 civilians without authorization to use force, faces a militarily superior adversary whose intentions are not fully known but whose operational consequences are visible; the commander must decide whether operational compliance with a mandate that has become structurally incoherent is distinguishable from complicity.

**Stakeholders** — The 40,000 Bosnian Muslims in the enclave (whose safety the mandate named but could not deliver); Dutchbat III soldiers (under orders in a system designed to fail under the load it encountered); Karremans (individual commander at the decisive point); Dutch political leadership (in campaign mode, with limited situational awareness); Mladic and Serb forces (who understood the Dutch mandate's constraints better than the Dutch chain of command did); the UN Security Council (whose authorization of the safe area without a commensurate force commitment created the structural incoherence that made all subsequent decisions possible).

**Binding constraint** — The dual-key NATO/UN authorization structure had paralyzed air strike capability since May 1995. Within Dutchbat's operational authority, the binding constraint was the absence of any protocol for a field commander to formally declare a UN mandate incoherent in real time — no procedure existed for the condition Karremans was in.

**Decision pathway** — Reframe: from "how do I protect the safe area within the mandate" to "at what moment does compliance with an incoherent mandate become complicity, and what is my obligation at that crossing?" The reframe does not produce a military victory. It produces a documentation of non-complicity, a political escalation that forces the decision about the mandate to the level where it can be made, and a refusal to lend Dutch operational assistance to what is unfolding.

**Tools** — (1) Mandate failure declaration: formal written communication to Dutch Defense Minister directly, bypassing UNPROFOR chain, declaring the safe area mandate unexecutable and requesting emergency political authorization — changes coordination by forcing a political-level decision in real time. (2) Compound perimeter witness protocol: Dutch soldiers positioned at the separation point with orders to record and not facilitate — changes learning by creating contemporaneous documentation under military authority. (3) Non-cooperation posture at the gate: no Dutch operational assistance in directing men toward Serb vehicles — changes interaction by removing the Dutch operational role from Mladic's organizational execution.

**Metrics** — (1) Escalation latency: hours between Karremans's knowledge of the mandate's failure and ministerial-level awareness in The Hague, measured by communications logs. (2) Documented complicity incidents: incidents in which Dutch soldiers actively facilitated the separation versus those in which they were positioned as passive witnesses, measured by trial testimony. (3) Political decision lag: hours between the enclave's fall and a Dutch cabinet-level decision about Dutchbat's mandate, measured by parliamentary records.

**90-day commitment** — Counterfactual from July 11: that night, Karremans transmits a formal mandate failure declaration directly to Defense Minister Joris Voorhoeve by name, requesting emergency authorization for a revised objective of evacuation and documentation. July 12: Dutch soldiers at the compound gate are positioned as witnesses, not facilitators — no assistance in separating men, no cooperation in clearing the perimeter, visible presence as a documented record. Simultaneously, the 27 Dutch soldiers held by the Serbs in the surrounding zone are formally designated hostages in the communication to The Hague, triggering a political response at the level above UNPROFOR. Within 90 days, the Dayton negotiations proceed; the Dutch parliamentary investigation begins with contemporaneous documentation rather than reconstructed testimony; and the question of the mandate's structural incoherence becomes a matter of record rather than a conclusion reached seven years later.
