# Engineering Diplomacy — 2026-08-31

## The River That Both Sides Won

On the morning of October 23, 1992, engineers at the Gabcikovo hydroelectric facility on the Slovak bank of the Danube closed the sluice gates and began diverting the river. Within days, 80 to 90 percent of the Danube's flow — roughly 2,000 cubic meters of water per second — was passing through Slovak infrastructure rather than through the old river channel that marked the Hungarian-Slovak boundary. The maneuver was called Variant C, and it was a triumph of engineering: it captured the power and the water that Slovakia's investment required. It was also the moment a fifteen-year treaty dispute became permanent.

The 1977 Treaty of Budapest had committed Communist Hungary and Communist Czechoslovakia to build a paired lock-and-reservoir system on the Danube: Gabcikovo on the Slovak bank, Nagymaros on the Hungarian side, jointly operated. The engineering was ambitious and the investment was real. By 1989, both Communist governments had been peacefully overthrown, and the project had acquired a second meaning. The Duna Kör — the Danube Circle — had collected 100,000 signatures against the project under Communist rule, one of Hungary's first organized legal opposition movements. To Hungary's post-Communist parliament, the dam was not merely a hydroelectric project. It was a symbol of everything they had rejected. In 1992, the parliament voted to terminate the treaty.

The Czechoslovak and, shortly after, Slovak governments faced an engineering problem with no diplomatic solution in sight. Gabcikovo, the Slovak facility, had been designed as one half of a matched pair. Without Nagymaros to regulate flow downstream, the Slovak reservoir would function inefficiently and uncontrollably. The Slovak engineering response was competent and technically logical: construct Variant C, a unilateral diversion that would capture the Danube entirely on Slovak territory, generate the designed power output, and proceed without Hungary. The problem was that Variant C required appropriating the water of an international boundary river without the consent of the downstream riparian state.

The Scientist looks at this and sees a failure of measurement sequencing. In December 1991 — the last window before Variant C construction began — Hungary proposed a joint independent environmental impact assessment as a condition for resuming treaty obligations. Slovakia declined, characterizing the proposal as an inadmissible renegotiation of treaty terms. That refusal had a measurable consequence: the two sides' hydrological models for Variant C's impact on the Hungarian groundwater system disagreed by a factor of two. The old Danube channel, on which hundreds of thousands of people in western Hungary depended for drinking water recharge, would receive either 10 or 20 percent of natural flow — a range that mattered enormously for the Szigetköz alluvial plain's water table. The scientific dispute was real. Both sides had credentialed hydrologists and neither model had been validated against observed data. Without an agreed measurement protocol, there was no shared factual basis on which to negotiate, only competing assertions dressed in peer-reviewed language.

The Humanist looks at this and sees two post-Communist nations trying to solve a governance problem that predates their governments. The 1977 Treaty was the Communist regimes' commitment; neither democracy had chosen it. For Hungarian citizens who had just voted out the government that signed it, the dam was a test of whether new institutions could distinguish their choices from those of their predecessors. For Slovaks approaching independence from the Czech Republic — achieved January 1, 1993, the same week the Danube diversion became fully operational — Gabcikovo was a statement of sovereign capacity. Abandoning it meant confirming a Slovak inability to govern its own natural resources. These were competing stories about democratic legitimacy. They could not be resolved by engineering, and they were never directly addressed as the political questions they actually were. Each side's formal legal position — treaty obligation, environmental necessity, sovereign right — was a disguise for the conversation that neither government's domestic constituency would permit it to have directly.

The Engineer looks at December 1991 and locates where X was. Hungary's proposal for an independent environmental review was not a delaying tactic. It was, properly constructed, an 18th camel: not "cancel the dam" and not "build it as 1977 specified," but a third institution — a Joint Environmental Assessment Commission with authority to establish binding minimum flow obligations in the old Danube channel as a treaty condition. Both countries could have fulfilled their core interests: Slovakia would get Gabcikovo's power; Hungary would get guaranteed groundwater recharge. The engineering was feasible; the legal mechanism existed in international water law. What was missing was agreement to make independent environmental evidence the arbiter rather than negotiating position. Slovakia's refusal was not irrational — accepting a review process meant accepting the possibility that the review might constrain their investment — but it was the moment the 18th camel became unavailable, replaced by unilateral engineering.

What was 1+1? Slovakia was answering: how do we recover our investment and establish that new democracies honor international commitments? Hungary was answering: how do we protect our aquifer and assert that democratically-elected governments are not bound by their predecessors' contracts without consent? Both called their answer "Danube management." The Danube got managed; the underlying questions never got answered.

The International Court of Justice ruled on September 25, 1997. Both sides had violated the 1977 Treaty. Neither got a clean win. And in its judgment, the court did something unusual: it named the 18th camel. It instructed both states to negotiate in good faith a joint management regime for the Danube, specifying minimum flows and shared governance. This was exactly what Hungary had proposed in December 1991.

More than 27 years later, the joint management regime does not exist.

The counterfactual pathway was a Redesign — not of the dam, but of the treaty instrument. Reconstituting the 1977 Treaty from a construction contract into an adaptive governance framework would have given both sides a face-saving process. A joint commission, convened December 1991, with authority to set binding minimum flow thresholds in exchange for a modified construction specification, would have converted the bilateral deadlock into a tripartite evidence-based process. Hungary's parliament could have ratified a provisional continuation contingent on the review's findings. Slovakia would not have needed Variant C. The tools required were three: a Joint Environmental Assessment Commission (making independent evidence rather than negotiating position the arbiter); minimum flow guarantee clauses written into the treaty (converting environmental protection from a Hungarian political position into a legal obligation enforceable by the ICJ); and a staged construction protocol generating real-world hydrological data before each phase rather than forcing reliance on contested models.

(The ICJ named the 18th camel in its 1997 ruling. Both governments accepted the judgment and declined to implement its core instruction. The political cost of shared governance — the coordination, the sovereignty concession, the domestic constituency that would need to be told the win was partial — exceeded, for each party, the political cost of frozen non-compliance. A principled pragmatist can identify the 18th camel. They cannot dismount it from a sovereign government's refusal to ride.)

**Problem** — How two newly-democratic states should govern a shared international river when one withdrew from a Communist-era engineering treaty on environmental grounds and the other responded with a unilateral diversion appropriating 80-90% of the river's flow, in the absence of any treaty mechanism for adaptive modification based on changed political circumstances or new environmental evidence

**Stakeholders** — Hungarian government (treaty termination, democratic mandate, aquifer protection); Slovak and Czechoslovak governments (investment recovery, power generation, post-independence treaty reliability); residents of western Hungary's Szigetköz (drinking water from the alluvial aquifer); the Danube ecosystem; the ICJ (the only body with authority to rule, without authority to enforce)

**Binding constraint** — The 1977 Treaty was written as a construction contract with no mechanism for adaptive modification; the December 1991 proposal for an independent review was rejected as inadmissible renegotiation, foreclosing the only available shared evidentiary process before Variant C construction made it moot

**Decision pathway** — Redesign: reconstitute the 1977 Treaty from a technical specification into an adaptive governance framework via a Joint Environmental Assessment Commission with authority to issue binding minimum flow guarantees in exchange for a modified construction specification that both governments can ratify

**Tools** — Joint Environmental Assessment Commission (converts bilateral deadlock into tripartite evidence-based process; neither party can veto findings unilaterally, changing coordination); minimum flow guarantee clauses (converts environmental protection from a negotiating position to a legally enforceable treaty obligation, changing interaction between the two states and with the ICJ); staged construction protocol (replaces contested hydrological models with real-world data at each gate, changing learning)

**Metrics** — Flow in old Danube channel (m³/s, proportion of natural flow, continuous gauging at bifurcation, quarterly commission report); groundwater depth in Szigetköz alluvial plain (meters below surface at ten monitoring wells, quarterly automatic measurement against pre-diversion baseline); power generation per flow-compliance unit (annual basin-wide assessment)

**90-day commitment** — By March 1992: Hungary and Slovakia jointly commission an independent hydrological review by a body with established transboundary credentials; Slovakia accepts a six-month voluntary pause on Variant C preparation; Hungary's parliament ratifies provisional treaty continuation contingent on review findings; both delegations present draft treaty amendment language at the review's opening session

## The Scientists Who Silenced Themselves

On February 24, 1975, Paul Berg opened the Asilomar Conference on Recombinant DNA in Pacific Grove, California. He had organized the meeting, signed the moratorium letter the previous year, and spent months working out whom to invite. Of the 153 people in the room, roughly 133 were molecular biologists. The other twenty were four lawyers and sixteen journalists, the latter admitted as observers. There were no regulatory officials. There were no public health representatives. There were no citizens who lived near a laboratory or in a city where recombinant organisms might, one day, be produced at industrial scale. The scientists had called a meeting to decide what level of risk was acceptable. They had invited themselves.

The moratorium itself was an act of unusual institutional courage. In 1974, Berg and eleven colleagues had published a letter in Science calling on the scientific community to suspend certain recombinant DNA experiments until hazards could be assessed. The concern was specific: using restriction enzymes to cut DNA at precise sequences and ligase to join fragments across species made it possible to insert the genetic material of animal tumor viruses into E. coli, a bacterium present in human intestinal flora. The question was whether engineered E. coli carrying cancer-associated sequences could, if accidentally released, transfer those sequences into wild bacterial populations. Nobody knew. The technique was too new for any human exposure data to exist.

This is what the scientists did well. The Scientist's mode — rigorous uncertainty acknowledgment, precautionary pause, structured assessment — was exactly applied. At Asilomar, the scientists produced containment guidelines organized into four physical levels (P1 to P4) and two biological containment levels, specifying real laboratory procedures: air filtration, negative pressure rooms, biologically weakened host organisms that could not survive outside controlled conditions. These guidelines were implementable the following Monday. The moratorium was lifted on the conference's final day, February 27, 1975, with a framework that held: no catastrophic release event occurred under laboratory conditions.

The Humanist looks at Asilomar and notices who was not in the room and what they would have said if they had been. The question "is this safe?" has at least two meanings. The scientists' version: is this containable under the P1-to-P4 framework in a laboratory environment? The public's version — had anyone asked — would have included a second question: who decided what "safe enough" means, and why should we trust that judgment? These are not the same question. The failure at Asilomar is not that the scientists gave the wrong answer. It is that the second question was never asked. It could not be asked, because the people who would have asked it were not admitted as participants. This exclusion had a structural feature that went unacknowledged: the participants deciding what level of risk was acceptable were, by 1975, the same people with the most to gain from resuming research quickly. Herbert Boyer co-founded Genentech in April 1976, the year after Asilomar. The scientists were not corrupt. They had a conflict of interest that the closed process could not surface.

The Engineer looks at the planning committee's meeting in December 1974 — Berg, Sidney Brenner, Maxine Singer — and finds where X was. The committee decided whom to invite. They considered including representatives of regulatory bodies and public health authorities, and decided not to. That decision had a consequence visible only in retrospect: it determined the structure of subsequent technology governance for an entire field. The NIH Recombinant DNA Advisory Committee, established in 1975, formalized the Asilomar template with marginal adjustment — four public members among twenty-five, advisory authority rather than gating authority. When the Cambridge, Massachusetts City Council voted in 1976 to impose a moratorium on Harvard's recombinant DNA research — demonstrating that external bodies were willing and able to regulate — the scientific community successfully characterized the Council's intervention as scientifically uninformed. The Asilomar framework held. The doing mode built an excellent set of rules for a bench scientist; it left the factory floor, and the question of who governs it, unaddressed.

What was 1+1? The scientists were answering: how do we resume responsible research? Any member of the Cambridge City Council would have answered: how do we ensure that decisions about our community's health are made with our participation? Both called this question "safety." The scientists' framework produced safety guidelines. The community's framework would have produced a process for determining what "safe enough" means to those who bear the risk. These are not the same thing.

The 18th camel was a two-tiered governance process, running concurrently rather than sequentially. Tier 1: the Asilomar conference proceeds as it did, producing technical containment guidelines that scientists are best positioned to specify. Tier 2: simultaneously, a public advisory commission convenes — with representation from the FDA, public health officers, ethicists, and citizen representatives — charged not with overriding the technical guidelines but with ratifying the risk-acceptability threshold those guidelines are designed to achieve. The NIH RAC could have been constituted as the standing version of this two-tier structure before Asilomar, not after it. The camel was available. The planning committee did not recognize it as a camel; they recognized it as a delay.

The counterfactual pathway is a Redesign, not a rejection. The science at Asilomar was sound. What required redesign was the governance architecture: run the two tiers concurrently, give the public tier genuine gating authority over experimental pacing by risk category, publish the joint framework as the product of a process both scientists and citizens endorsed. The tools required were three: a concurrent public advisory commission (changing the interaction between expert judgment and public consent — neither tier can finalize without the other); a graduated release protocol by risk category (changing what is learned, because lower-risk experimental categories inform decisions about higher-risk ones sequentially rather than all at once); and a five-year sunset clause on all containment guidelines (changing coordination, because it requires active reaffirmation as the risk landscape evolves, rather than allowing 1975 bench-scale guidelines to govern 1982 industrial fermenters).

This matters beyond recombinant DNA because the Asilomar template was invoked explicitly when governing synthetic biology, gain-of-function research, and large-language-model artificial intelligence. In each case the argument was: the experts who understand the technology should set the risk thresholds, and external governance would produce less informed and more restrictive standards. The argument may be correct epistemically. It has the same structural problem every time.

(Asilomar is universally celebrated, and the celebration is not entirely wrong. The moratorium was real. The guidelines were technically defensible. No catastrophic release event occurred under laboratory conditions. But the conference also established the durable template: when a new and potentially dangerous technology emerges, the experts who developed it determine the acceptable risk level for the public. Principled pragmatism can identify the 18th camel — concurrent public deliberation — and can even note that the scientists' risk assessment was probably correct. But "probably correct, determined by the experts with the financial interest" is not the same as "legitimate." Engineering diplomacy has no framework for when expert self-governance is epistemically valid but democratically insufficient — where the scientists are probably right and the process is still wrong.)

**Problem** — When the scientists who invented a potentially dangerous technique convened to decide what level of risk was acceptable, the structure of who was invited determined what "acceptable" meant — and the Asilomar conference determined that it meant acceptable to scientists, creating a governance template that has been applied, with the same structural conflict of interest, to every subsequent technology whose primary experts also have commercial interests in the answer

**Stakeholders** — Molecular biology research community (technical expertise; commercial interests in resuming research); NIH and federal regulatory bodies (oversight responsibility, deferred to scientists); Cambridge City Council and equivalent local bodies (demonstrated willingness to regulate independently); the public (no participant status; bore the risk)

**Binding constraint** — The December 1974 invitation list: the planning committee's decision to exclude regulatory and public representatives from participant status created a self-referential governance process with no external accountability, and no mechanism to surface the conflict of interest between the scientists' technical role and their commercial interests

**Decision pathway** — Redesign: run two tiers concurrently — Tier 1 (scientists) produces technical containment guidelines; Tier 2 (public advisory commission with genuine gating authority) ratifies the risk-acceptability threshold — and constitute the NIH RAC as the standing version of this two-tier structure before the conference, not after it

**Tools** — Concurrent public advisory commission (changes interaction between expert judgment and public consent — neither tier finalizes without the other); graduated release protocol by risk category (changes learning — lower-risk experiments inform decisions about higher-risk ones sequentially); five-year sunset clause on containment guidelines (changes coordination — requires active reaffirmation as the risk landscape evolves, preventing 1975 bench guidelines from governing 1982 factory floors)

**Metrics** — Public-tier participation rate in guideline decisions (proportion with full stakeholder representation, tracked per decision cycle by RAC public registry); containment-level compliance rate in laboratory inspections (NIH site visits, annually published); frequency of guideline review against new experimental evidence (continuous, publicly registered)

**90-day commitment** — By March 1975: planning committee constitutes both tiers; Tier 1 convenes at Asilomar as planned; Tier 2 convenes at NIH Bethesda with FDA, public health, citizen-appointed, and ethics representation; both tiers exchange interim reports; joint document published April 1975 authorizing research resumption by risk category under specified conditions
