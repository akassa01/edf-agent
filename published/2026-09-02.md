## The Certifier's Dilemma — Brumadinho and the Fee-for-Safety Trap

At 12:28 on January 25, 2019, Dam I at the Córrego do Feijão iron ore mine began to liquefy. The process took about ten seconds. By the time the first wave of tailings mud reached the mine's refectory — where hundreds of workers were eating lunch in a building purposely sited below the dam because engineers had certified the structure safe — the dam was already gone. Within minutes, 12 million cubic metres of toxic sludge had travelled six kilometres down the Córrego do Feijão stream into the Paraopeba River. It killed 272 people.

Four months before the collapse, in September 2018, the German certification firm TÜV SÜD had issued a stability certificate for Dam I, confirming it as safe for continued operation. This was not the first time TÜV SÜD had certified the dam. It was the most recent. The certificate was the mechanism by which Vale, Brazil's largest mining company, maintained its legal operating license. The certificate said the dam was stable. The dam was not stable. But the certificate was, in every formal respect, legal.

The relationship between those two facts is the case.

Dam I was built using the upstream construction method — a technique in which the dam is raised incrementally by depositing new tailings immediately upstream of the prior structure. It is cheaper than the alternatives. It is also statistically more prone to liquefaction under saturated conditions, a fact well known to the International Commission on Large Dams (ICOLD) and to the Brazilian dam safety engineering community. Three years and 109 kilometres away, the Fundão dam at the Samarco mine — also upstream construction, also in Minas Gerais — had catastrophically failed in November 2015, releasing 60 million cubic metres of tailings and killing 19 people. The pattern was documented. The risk was known.

TÜV SÜD's June 2018 periodic review of Dam I recorded a factor of safety of approximately 1.09. ICOLD guidelines set a minimum factor of safety of 1.30 for dams in non-operating status — dams no longer actively receiving tailings, which is what Dam I was. The review also noted several deficiencies in the dam's drainage infrastructure: eroded surface channels, clogged exit tubes, damaged draining equipment. TÜV SÜD issued the certificate anyway. The ambiguity that made this formally possible: Vale classified Dam I as "operating," which required only a minimum factor of safety of 1.00. The applicable standard was not applied. The certificate was issued.

The Scientist's reading is precise and stops just short of exculpatory. The technical failure was in the model: TÜV SÜD's analysis underestimated pore-water pressure accumulation in the saturated dam body. Post-failure analysis confirmed that this pore-water pressure was the triggering mechanism — exactly the failure mode that downstream construction and centerline construction are designed to resist, and upstream construction is not. If the question being asked was "is this dam at risk of liquefaction under the moisture conditions that actually obtained?" the model gave the wrong answer. But the question being formally asked — "does this dam meet the certification standard as applied?" — may have been answered honestly. The two questions are different, and the certification architecture only required TÜV SÜD to answer the second one.

The Humanist's reading is where the 272 deaths become legible as more than a failure of engineering. The Córrego do Feijão community downstream had no access to the stability certificate's numerical findings. The factor of safety — 1.09, versus the applicable 1.30 — was not disclosed to municipal emergency planners, to the workers' union, or to the families who lived downstream of the waste stream. A certified structure is, to a non-specialist, an assured structure. The certification system existed to provide exactly this assurance; that is its function. What the workers eating lunch in the refectory below the dam understood — correctly, given the information available to them — was that the dam had been checked and was safe. The system that was supposed to protect them had used their trust as a load-bearing element.

The Engineer's reading locates the structure that made this outcome predictable. Vale paid TÜV SÜD. TÜV SÜD's business model required continued client relationships. The dam safety certification market in Brazil was not large; the major mining operators were a significant share of the available contracts. An engineer at TÜV SÜD who issued a disqualifying finding — who said the dam could not be certified without remediation — would have been right, and would also have ended the contract. No individual was villainous. The doing mode — find a way to keep the mine certified and operational — was the organizing principle of the relationship between the auditor and the client, and it crowded out the independent thinking that a genuine safety audit requires.

Where is X? It is September 2018, in a TÜV SÜD review meeting where engineers recorded a factor of safety of 1.09, noted drainage deficiencies, and chose to classify the dam's status in a way that permitted certification under the lower standard. That meeting — or the document that came out of it — is the precise fulcrum. A different choice at that moment would have required Vale to remediate the drainage infrastructure and address the pore-water risk before the certificate could be issued.

The binary that the certification system presented was stark: certify the dam and continue operating, or issue a disqualifying finding and lose the contract. The 18th camel was not inside that binary. It is a redesign of who pays for the audit. Aviation accident investigation in the United States is funded through a regulatory levy pooled across all aircraft operators and administered by the National Transportation Safety Board, which has no commercial relationship with any manufacturer or carrier. Its investigators can ground an aircraft type without losing a contract, because they have no contract to lose. The equivalent for Brazilian tailings dams would be an industry-funded dam safety pool — financed by a per-tonne-of-ore levy across all mining operators, commissioning audits through randomly assigned firms without prior client relationships. The auditor who issues a disqualifying finding is not punished commercially, because they were not paid by the mine. The mine is not present in the room.

The political obstacles are real. Vale could challenge retroactive certification requirements as a constitutional taking. The Bolsonaro government, inaugurated in January 2019, defunded IBAMA and reduced SNISB's operational capacity within weeks of the Brumadinho collapse — demonstrating that the regulatory infrastructure for any redesigned system is itself politically vulnerable. The pool of qualified independent dam safety engineers in Brazil who have no commercial relationship with the major mining operators is thin; industry capture of an independent pool is a risk, not a theoretical concern.

A principled pragmatist inside Brazil's National Dam Safety Policy agency (SNISB) in October 2018 — three months before the collapse, two months after the certificate was issued — had access to the registry of UCM dams in Minas Gerais and to the record of the Samarco collapse three years prior. The move available: order an emergency review of all UCM dams certified since 2015, flagging any with factors of safety below 1.20, and requiring a second independent audit not conducted by the same firm that issued the original certificate.

*(The honest flag: the problem that Brumadinho reveals is not primarily about one corrupt auditor or one negligent mining company. It is structural: third-party certification markets are systematically vulnerable to capture by their clients, and the pool of qualified independent auditors in any specialized technical domain is small enough that even an industry-funded pool can be captured at one remove — through the governance of the pool, through the nomination of methodology committees, through the training pipelines that produce the next generation of auditors. No certification architecture at scale resolves this tension when the inspected sector and the auditing sector draw from the same talent market.)*

---

**Problem** — How to certify the structural safety of high-consequence tailings dams when the fee-for-service certification market gives auditors a commercial incentive to certify rather than disqualify, and when the downstream community bears the risk but has no standing in the audit process.

**Stakeholders** — The 272 victims and their families; the Brumadinho municipality and downstream Paraopeba River communities (risk-bearers with no standing); Vale (operator and fee-payer); TÜV SÜD (certifier and fee-recipient); Brazil's SNISB and IBAMA (regulators with inspection authority but no independent audit capacity); the ICOLD community (standard-setters whose guidelines were not applied).

**Binding constraint** — The fee-for-service certification market: the auditor's commercial interest is structurally aligned with issuing a certificate rather than a disqualifying finding, and no mechanism existed to protect an auditor who acted against the client's preferred outcome.

**Decision pathway** — Redesign: restructure the dam safety certification market from a bilateral fee-for-service model to an industry-funded independent pool, modeled on aviation's NTSB levy structure, in which the auditor has no client relationship with the mine operator. Immediate trigger: mandate a below-standard factor-of-safety audit review for all UCM dams in Minas Gerais certified since 2015.

**Tools** — (1) Industry-funded dam safety pool: commissioned audits through randomly assigned firms with no prior client relationship — breaks the commercial conflict without requiring the auditor to act against a client. (2) Mandatory public factor-of-safety disclosure: municipal emergency planners and affected communities see the margin and can request explanation when it is thin. (3) Below-standard trigger audit: when recorded FoS is within 20% of the applicable minimum, a second independent firm must validate before the certificate is issued.

**Metrics** — (1) Proportion of high-consequence dam audits conducted by firms with no prior commercial relationship with the operator: target 100% within three years. (2) Time between a remediation recommendation and completion of the remediation: target under 12 months, SNISB mandatory reporting. (3) Number of dam safety certificates with publicly disclosed FoS figures: target 100%.

**90-day commitment** — (Counterfactual, October 2018): SNISB orders a 90-day emergency review of all UCM tailings dams in Minas Gerais certified since 2015. Any dam with FoS below 1.20 receives an independent review by a second firm not previously contracted by the operator, with results disclosed to municipal emergency management within the same window.

## The River with No Standing — Jordan River Restoration and the Governance Gap

Stand on the Abdallah Bridge, where the lower Jordan River crosses into the Kingdom of Jordan, and you are looking at what was once described by pilgrims as a wide, swift, sacred river. What you see today is a narrow, turbid channel, running brown and saline, flanked by invasive reeds on both banks. The water level is low enough that children could wade across. The salinity — more than 3,000 milligrams per litre, compared to the 500 mg/litre threshold below which the water is useful for irrigation — has made the river useless to the Bedouin farmers who once depended on it. The Jordan River, by volume, has lost between 90 and 98 percent of its historical flow. The Dead Sea, its terminal basin, is shrinking at approximately one metre per year.

This is not a failure of scientific knowledge. The hydrology is precise: the Jordan River once delivered 1.2 to 1.3 billion cubic metres per year to the Dead Sea. By the 2020s, it delivers 20 to 100 million. The sources of the diversion are documented. The technical levers for restoration are identified and partially operational. The money exists. The political will, in the form of a November 2022 joint declaration by Israeli and Jordanian environment ministers at COP27, is on record. None of this has produced a single additional cubic metre of flow in the lower river. The failure is not technical. It is a failure of the question the governance architecture is designed to answer.

Three states use the Jordan River basin: Israel, Jordan, and the Palestinian territories. Their relationship to it is governed by the Israel-Jordan Peace Treaty of 1994, specifically Article 6 and Annex II, which fix each state's water allocation entitlement. The Palestinian Authority has no formal standing in this treaty and no bilateral water rights to the Jordan River. Oslo II (1995) allocated groundwater in the West Bank without assigning surface flow from the river. Thirty years on, the Oslo annex has not been updated, the PA's exclusion from Jordan River governance has not been corrected, and the 1994 allocations have not been revisited. The treaty answered the question "how do we prevent another war over water?" It was not asked "what does the river need to survive?"

That distinction is the case.

The Scientist sees three actionable restoration levers that are technically available right now. Israel's desalination capacity now exceeds its domestic water demand; the surplus could fund a proportional reduction in Sea of Galilee diversions, releasing flow southward into the Jordan River, without reducing Israel's overall water security. Wastewater reclamation infrastructure in the lower Jordan Valley — partially funded through USAID and partially operational — could be expanded to reduce agricultural demand on the river itself. EcoPeace Middle East's Regional NGO Master Plan, a 2022 comprehensive engineering analysis, estimates that 400 to 600 million cubic metres per year of ecological flow is achievable through a combination of these levers, sufficient to stabilise the current situation. The science has been done. The models exist. The target is specific and reachable.

The Humanist sees why the science has not moved any water. The Jordan River is not primarily, for any of the parties, a hydraulic object. For Palestinian communities, it is the western boundary of any future Palestinian state — a line of sovereignty as much as a source of irrigation — and to accept its governance through any framework that excludes the Palestinian Authority is to accept permanent marginality in the region's water future. For Jordanians, the 1994 Peace Treaty's water provisions represent a diplomatic achievement the Hashemite Kingdom's government cannot be seen to dilute; domestic water scarcity is severe, and any reallocation triggers domestic political opposition that no government has the standing to absorb. For Bedouin agricultural communities in the valley, the river's salinity has already ended their livelihoods; restoration is survival, not preference. The river carries different questions for each party, and the governance architecture answers none of them.

The Engineer sees the structural problem that the Scientist and the Humanist together name: there is no forum in which the three states, the Palestinian Authority, and the river's ecological interests appear simultaneously. The 1994 treaty is bilateral. The Oslo II water annex is bilateral. Every diplomatic venue that exists is structured around sovereign entitlements, not around the ecological unit — the basin — that determines whether there is water to allocate. The doing-mode tools that would restore the river require a governance layer that does not currently exist.

Where is X? It is the UN Water Conference follow-up process. The 2023 UN Water Conference — the first since 1977 — produced a set of voluntary commitments now being tracked through 2025 and 2026. That process provides a diplomatic occasion at which language can be introduced: not treaty amendment, but a new category of governance instrument — a transboundary ecological guardianship, with co-representatives from all basin parties including civil society, explicitly not modifying bilateral allocations. The moment when that language could be inserted into the preparatory committee documents is the fulcrum. It has not passed.

The 18th camel is not a renegotiation of the 1994 Peace Treaty. It is not a reliance on the Red Sea-Dead Sea Canal to fill the Dead Sea from the south, which would stabilise the Dead Sea's level while leaving the Jordan River itself to die. It is a third thing: a Jordan River Co-Guardianship Council, modeled on New Zealand's Te Awa Tupua framework for the Whanganui River — which designated the river a legal entity co-governed by Indigenous representatives and the Crown in 2017. A Jordan River Council would designate the river a transboundary ecological entity with formal co-guardians drawn from Israeli, Palestinian, and Jordanian civil society. The Council would have no power to redistribute water rights. It would have standing to coordinate the use of surplus capacity, to commission and publish joint ecological monitoring, and to represent the river's interests in any diplomatic forum where the basin's future is discussed.

The political obstacles are not trivial. The Israeli Water Authority has consistently resisted any governance innovation it interprets as a constraint on diversion discretion. The Palestinian Authority's legitimacy in the West Bank is constrained; it may not have the political capacity to participate in a new multilateral framework. And the single most dangerous risk is indirect: if the Red Sea-Dead Sea Canal secures final funding without a Jordan River ecological flow condition attached, the visual evidence of the governance failure is neutralised from the south, the political urgency drops, and the window for a governance reframe closes.

A principled pragmatist at EcoPeace Middle East, or at the UN Economic and Social Commission for Western Asia, has a move available now. A civil society drafting process — trilateral, explicitly framed as supplemental to and not replacing any bilateral treaty — could produce legal text for a Jordan River Co-Guardianship proposal in time for submission to the 2027 UN Water Conference preparatory committee. The proposal would need Israeli, Palestinian, and Jordanian civil society co-signatories. The tool required to begin is not legislation. It is a drafting convening.

*(The honest flag: rights-of-nature designations create legal standing for a river but cannot compel the infrastructure changes needed to restore its flow. The Whanganui River has legal personhood in New Zealand and continues to be the subject of water allocation disputes. A guardianship council changes who can argue about the water; it does not change how much water is diverted. The gap between governance innovation and hydrological change is the unresolved problem, and no governance instrument alone closes it.)*

---

**Problem** — How to coordinate Jordan River ecological restoration when the governance architecture — bilateral water treaties that exclude Palestine and address sovereign allocation rather than ecological function — prevents the coordination of restoration levers that technically exist and are partially operational.

**Stakeholders** — The lower Jordan River as an ecological entity (no current governance standing); Palestinian communities with no treaty water rights; Bedouin agricultural communities in the Jordan Valley whose livelihoods depend on irrigation-viable river water; EcoPeace Middle East and the trilateral civil society network; the Israeli Water Authority; the Jordanian Ministry of Water and Irrigation; the Palestinian Authority (excluded from existing frameworks); USAID and EU (current funders of joint technical infrastructure).

**Binding constraint** — The absence of a governance architecture that includes all three major water users and the river's ecological interests in the same forum. The Palestinian Authority lacks treaty standing in any bilateral Jordan River framework, making any trilateral coordination structurally incomplete.

**Decision pathway** — Reframe: reframe the governance question from "how do we divide the water?" to "how do we co-govern the river?" by establishing a Jordan River Co-Guardianship Council as a parallel legal entity, explicitly not amending bilateral allocation treaties. Who acts: EcoPeace Middle East as civil society convener, ESCWA as diplomatic host. For whom: all basin parties, including civil society currently excluded from intergovernmental forums.

**Tools** — (1) Jordan River Co-Guardianship Council (Te Awa Tupua model): designates the river a co-governed legal entity with civil society co-guardians — changes interaction by giving Palestinian and Jordanian civil society formal governance standing without treaty amendment. (2) Desalination Offset Credit mechanism: allows Israel to publicly report Sea of Galilee diversion reductions proportional to its desalination surplus — changes coordination by making a unilateral technical arrangement into a multilateral visible commitment. (3) UN Water Conference Restoration Pledge: non-binding basin-state commitment to annual ecological flow reporting — changes learning by creating peer-accountability pressure without treaty obligation.

**Metrics** — (1) Annual volumetric flow reaching the Dead Sea from the Jordan River: MCM/year via flow gauging at Damiya Bridge; baseline 30–100 MCM; target 200 MCM by 2030. (2) Dead Sea surface level: metres relative to 1978 baseline via satellite altimetry; target: arrest of annual decline. (3) Basin civil society organisations with formal governance standing in any Jordan River intergovernmental body: count; current 0; target ≥5 by 2027.

**90-day commitment** — EcoPeace Middle East, with ESCWA, convenes a 90-day trilateral civil society drafting process to produce legal text for a Jordan River Co-Guardianship Proposal for submission to the 2027 UN Water Conference preparatory committee. Israeli, Palestinian, and Jordanian civil society organisations co-sign. The proposal explicitly preserves bilateral treaty allocations.
