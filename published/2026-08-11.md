## The Settlement They Could Not Accept

New Delhi, February 14, 1989. Chief Justice R.S. Pathak calls the Supreme Court to order for what everyone in the room understands will be a brief session. Five years after the gas leak at Union Carbide's Bhopal plant killed thousands in a single night — the exact number has never been agreed upon — a settlement has been reached. The Indian government will accept $470 million from Union Carbide. All civil claims will be extinguished. All pending criminal proceedings will be quashed. The Chief Justice reviews the terms and approves.

Outside, in Bhopal, the women of the gas-affected neighborhoods have gathered again. The Bhopal Gas Peedit Mahila Udyog Sangathan has been organizing since 1984: documenting cases, accompanying families to hospitals that did not know how to treat methyl isocyanate poisoning, demanding that someone be named responsible. They were not invited to this negotiation. Under the Bhopal Gas Leak Disaster (Processing of Claims) Act, passed by Parliament in 1985, the Government of India holds exclusive authority to litigate on their behalf. They have no standing in their own case. They learn the terms from the newspapers.

The survivor organizations were not without analysis. They understood that the settlement was calculated on Union Carbide's own casualty estimates — not on ICMR studies still underway, not on their own count of the dying. They understood that the per-claimant payout would amount to roughly ₹25,000, approximately $300 at the time. And they understood that for the community, no dollar figure resolved the question the disaster had opened: whether a corporation could kill thousands of people and buy its way clear of the consequences.

The mode the community leaned on — the feeling that money was not justice, that justice required criminal accountability for the men who had made the decisions — was not irrational. It was internally coherent, morally serious, and deeply felt. It was also the mode that crowded out the mode that could have helped the people already dying of chronic respiratory disease in the gas-affected lanes of Bhopal: the doing-mode, which asks what is actually buildable and who can build it now.

The Scientist's reading of February 14, 1989 is uncomfortable. The death toll was genuinely unknown — Union Carbide claimed approximately 3,800 deaths; ICMR data was emerging; Amnesty International would eventually document over 22,000 excess deaths across two decades. The settlement's $470 million was calculated on the lowest defensible estimate, with no adjustment mechanism if the toll proved larger. The chemical exposure — MIC, hydrogen cyanide, and other gases — had no prior industrial literature for its long-term effects. The Scientist sees a settlement priced on the cheapest assumption and stamped final.

The Humanist sees the families who came to the survivor organizations' offices. They were not asking to be compensated; they were asking to be recognized — that what was done to them had a name, that the name included someone's personal responsibility, that the law had a mechanism for saying so. The ₹25,000 the settlement produced per claimant was not recognition. It was a transaction that closed the case without answering the question.

The Engineer asks what $470 million could have built. Respiratory clinics, rehabilitation centers, vocational programs for the thousands left partially disabled, longitudinal health monitoring of the kind that might have caught second-generation effects documented years later. The Engineer watches those needs go unmet and traces the line back to February 1989: the legal dispute over the settlement's adequacy — which the survivors were right to wage, on the facts — effectively froze the money's deployment while people continued to die of treatable conditions. The Engineer does not disagree with the Humanist about what justice required. The Engineer notes what the Humanist's stand cost, in clinics not built, in years, in lives.

Where is X? The Supreme Court hearing of February 14–15, 1989. Chief Justice Pathak's bench had the procedural authority to impose a 90-day consultation period, to invite survivor organizations as formal intervenors, to require a health baseline assessment before approving a settlement figure calculated on disputed data. The leverage point was not the amount — that would have required a different negotiation — but the procedural moment that locked the settlement's structure before survivors could formally contest it.

The binary looked like this: (A) accept the settlement — civil compensation and criminal quashing bundled — or (B) reject it and fight on in courts with no funding flowing and no legal standing in the government's case. The 18th camel was a third thing: accept the civil funds for medical infrastructure while the government formally decoupled the criminal proceedings — issuing a declaration that the settlement covered civil liability only and that criminal charges against Warren Anderson remained an active, separate track under Indian law. The Supreme Court's own ruling in October 1991 — which struck down the criminal quashing provision at survivor petition, while leaving the civil settlement intact — proves this separation was legally achievable. Someone had to ask for it in February 1989, with the standing to be heard.

1+1 produced three separate negotiations. The Indian government was answering: how do we close this diplomatically without destroying the investment climate? Union Carbide was answering: how do we achieve finality and cap aggregate liability? The survivor organizations were answering: how do we hold the corporation criminally accountable and ensure the people dying now get medical care? Three separate questions. Only the first two had seats at the table.

A principled pragmatist, working from 1985: file for intervenor status in the government case under India's public interest litigation doctrine — survivor organizations had the constitutional basis. Commission an independent health baseline from ICMR with survivor-designated epidemiologists before the settlement figure is set. Insist that any settlement explicitly decouples civil from criminal liability. These three acts would have changed the structure of what was on the table on February 14. They required political will from a government under pressure to close the case quickly. That is, precisely, what principled pragmatism asks of someone with standing.

*(The flag: when a corporation's senior officers can reside in a jurisdiction that will not extradite them, criminal accountability is structurally inaccessible regardless of how well the civil settlement is designed. Warren Anderson died in Vero Beach, Florida, in 2014, having never appeared before an Indian court. The Indian Supreme Court issued its warrant; the US courts declined extradition. Principled pragmatism can design the civil/criminal severability mechanism. It cannot create the jurisdictional authority that international corporate law in 1989 did not provide. The gap between the framework's prescription and the law's reach was not a negotiation failure. It was the architecture of the world as it was.)*

---

**Problem** — A survivor community stripped of legal standing by statute and excluded from settlement negotiations must secure a civil settlement that funds urgent medical care without surrendering the right to pursue criminal accountability, when the criminal track is structurally inaccessible regardless of what the settlement says.

**Stakeholders** — Bhopal gas survivors (medical care, criminal accountability, participatory dignity); Indian government (diplomatic closure, investor confidence); Union Carbide (civil finality, limitation of aggregate liability); Indian Supreme Court (constitutional legitimacy of the settlement process).

**Binding constraint** — Survivor organizations had no formal standing in the government case and could not procedurally block settlement approval; only contest it afterward — which they did successfully on the criminal quashing issue, but not until 1991, two years later.

**Decision pathway** — Reframe: not "accept or reject this settlement" but "change the structure of what is on the table by requiring civil/criminal decoupling and a health baseline as prior conditions of any approval."

**Tools** — Public interest litigation petition for intervenor status (changes interaction: survivor organizations gain procedural standing in a case that excluded them by statute); health baseline commission with survivor-designated epidemiologists (changes learning: puts a jointly produced epidemiological floor under any settlement figure); civil/criminal severability declaration by the Attorney General (changes coordination: makes the legal architecture reflect what the 1991 ruling confirmed was always achievable).

**Metrics** — Criminal proceedings status: whether criminal case against Anderson remains procedurally active post-settlement; medical infrastructure: clinics and rehabilitation centers operating within 36 months of settlement approval; survivor representation: formal intervenor status in settlement hearing recorded in Supreme Court case record.

**90-day commitment** — Counterfactual, beginning late 1985: survivor organizations file for intervenor status under Indian PIL doctrine; ICMR commissions baseline health study with survivor-designated co-investigators; the Attorney General publicly states that any civil settlement will not extinguish the criminal case. Each act costs nothing except the political will to name the distinction between civil and criminal accountability before the negotiation closes.

## The Production Clause That Wasn't

Busan, South Korea. December 1, 2024. Luis Vayas Valdivieso, the Ecuadorian diplomat who has chaired five rounds of negotiations toward the world's first legally binding instrument on plastic pollution, faces a room that will not agree. The "Like-Minded Group" — led by Saudi Arabia, Russia, Iran, and Kuwait, with China and India in loose alignment — has spent two weeks refusing any treaty language that obliges member states to reduce primary plastic production. More than 100 countries support some form of production cap; the blocking bloc is smaller but uses the session's consensus decision rule with precision. Vayas Valdivieso can call a vote. He decides not to. He adjourns.

This moment — a chair choosing process integrity over outcome adequacy, at the point of maximum leverage — is what the plastic pollution crisis looks like through the Engineering Diplomacy frame. Not a villain and a victim. A technically correct position (production must be reduced) that has not yet been translated into a politically executable pathway, meeting a blocking coalition that knows the rules better than the science.

The scientific case is not in dispute. UNEP's flagship 2023 report, "Turning Off the Tap," modeled the options: even if every proposed recycling and waste management improvement were fully implemented, 100 million metric tonnes of plastic would still enter the environment annually by 2040. Achieving plastic neutrality requires a 40% reduction in primary production alongside ambitious recycling. Global production is currently 430 million tonnes per year. Only 9% of all plastic ever produced has been recycled. Waste management alone is, by the science, insufficient. The Scientist looks at this situation and asks: what else is there to discuss?

A great deal, as it turns out. The Humanist sees what the Scientist's clean model misses. Plastic production is not evenly distributed in its costs. The communities absorbing the waste — fisher families in Southeast Asia, coastal neighborhoods in West Africa, informal waste workers across the Global South — did not design the production system and bear its harms without meaningful representation in Geneva or Busan. Meanwhile, the blocking states frame production caps as a sovereignty question: the right of a developing petrochemical economy to build its own industrial base, without a treaty designed by wealthier nations who industrialized freely and now ask others not to follow. Both framings have internal coherence. Neither has found a pathway to the other's interests, because neither has been seriously looking for one.

The Engineer asks what is actually buildable given the rules of the room. The INC process operates on consensus — not a formal voting rule, but a diplomatic norm strong enough that the Chair would not break it even with a majority in hand. The petrostates' blocking power is not scientific; it is procedural. The Engineer sees that the High Ambition Coalition — 71 countries co-chaired by Norway and Rwanda, including the EU and most of the small island states — arrived in Busan with a scientifically correct position and no concrete transition pathway for the fence-sitting developing nations who care about plastic pollution but cannot afford to alienate their petrochemical trade partners. The HAC was answering the question "what does the science require?" The developing nations were answering "what happens to our industries if we sign?" Nobody had tabled an answer to the second question. So the treaty stalled.

1+1 produced three separate negotiations. The High Ambition Coalition was answering: what does the evidence require? The Like-Minded Group was answering: what protects our sovereign industrial development? The middle-group developing nations were answering: what do we get if we support production caps? Three separate questions. The INC Chair's Text from Busan preserved production language as an option, but options without coalition math are editorial.

Where is X? Two moments, in sequence. The first is the session in which the HAC decided not to table a concrete transition finance package alongside its production cap proposal — separating the scientific case from the implementation architecture. That decision was made quietly, in consultations before Busan, and it determined the coalition math. A transition facility linked to national production plans would have changed what developing nations had to gain by joining the HAC. The second is the closing plenary on December 1, when the Chair chose not to call a vote he had the procedural authority to call. Both are specific, locatable, and reversible in the next session.

The binary framing has been: (A) binding global production caps, opposed by the petrostate bloc; or (B) a waste-management treaty with no production obligations, opposed by the HAC and scientifically inadequate. The 18th camel is a nationally determined production trajectory mechanism — modeled on the Paris Agreement's NDC structure. Each country submits a national plan specifying its plastic production levels and a reduction pathway, tied to sustainable consumption benchmarks. Plans are subject to peer review by a UNEP-convened technical body. There is no agreed global cap — only agreed transparency, review, and ratcheting. Petrostates can participate without conceding a global number. Small island states and HAC members get binding accountability. Developing nations without petrochemical sectors get a structure that links plan ambition to eligibility for concessional transition capital. The NDC mechanism was not popular in Paris in 2015 either. It produced a framework that has since set the architecture for every major climate commitment.

A principled pragmatist at the next session: table the national trajectory mechanism as a draft article with three defined options for the scope of "production" — resin-level, pellet-level, product-level — each with its distributional implications made explicit. Simultaneously, have HAC core members announce the transition finance facility with a concrete capitalization figure and eligibility criteria. This sequence changes the question from "will you accept a global cap?" to "will you submit a national plan and qualify for financing?" It does not guarantee success. It addresses the second question in the room that nobody answered in Busan.

*(The flag: the treaty text will eventually contain a definition of "plastic production" — and that definition is the treaty's most consequential and least-discussed design decision. Accountability attaches to whoever is defined as the producer: crude oil exporters, resin manufacturers, polymer pellet producers, or consumer-product companies. Each definition allocates the economic burden of compliance to a different set of countries and industries. Principled pragmatism can map the options and their distributional consequences. It cannot resolve which distribution is just — because that is the policy question, and scientific analysis alone cannot provide it. The definitional choice will be made by negotiators under time pressure. The right time to design that choice carefully was before the next session convened. That window is now.)*

---

**Problem** — How should the INC process secure binding plastic production reduction obligations when a blocking coalition of petrostates uses the consensus rule to prevent them, and when the High Ambition Coalition's science-first framing has so far failed to build the transition pathway that would bring developing nations firmly to its side?

**Stakeholders** — High Ambition Coalition states (scientific adequacy, environmental credibility); Like-Minded Group petrostates (sovereign industrial development); middle-group developing nations (environmental commitment plus industrial transition risk); coastal and fishing communities globally (most harmed by plastic pollution, not directly represented); the INC Chair (process legitimacy, treaty adoption before mandate exhaustion).

**Binding constraint** — The INC's consensus decision norm gives the petrostates' blocking coalition veto power, and the HAC has not designed the institutional package (transition finance, nationally determined mechanism) that would move the middle-group developing nations to its side.

**Decision pathway** — Redesign: replace the "agree on a global cap" frame with a nationally determined production trajectory mechanism that decouples the accountability structure from the global-cap political impasse.

**Tools** — National Plastic Production Trajectory mechanism (changes interaction: replaces binary cap/no-cap negotiation with country-by-country transparency and ratcheting); Transition Finance Eligibility Framework (changes incentives: links plan ambition to concessional capital eligibility); without-prejudice definitional protocol (changes learning: surfaces distributional consequences of each "production" definition before a political choice is made implicitly).

**Metrics** — NPPT coverage: percentage of global plastic production covered by submitted national trajectory plans, tracked via UNEP registry; transition finance activation: USD disbursed within 24 months of treaty adoption; definitional clarity: whether an agreed definition of "plastic production" for treaty purposes is adopted at the next session.

**90-day commitment** — Live recommendation: the INC Secretariat convenes an intersessional technical working group to produce a draft NPPT mechanism design with three structural options; HAC core members (Norway, Rwanda, EU) announce a concrete transition finance facility with published eligibility criteria linked to NPPT ambition; both outputs tabled for the next INC session before its agenda is finalized.
